A demand letter landing in your inbox — or worse, from an attorney’s office — is designed to frighten you. It uses hard deadlines, legal language, and the full balance in bold. But a demand letter is not a court order, and it is not the end of the road. It is a signal that the account has moved to a pre-lawsuit posture, and that you still have a window — often a short one — to change the outcome. This guide explains what the letter actually means, what the real clock is, and how to respond so you protect yourself and open the door to a resolution. What a Demand Letter Actually Is

A demand letter is a formal written notice — from the lender, the merchant cash advance funder, or its lawyer — that states you are in default, declares an amount due, and demands payment by a date. Depending on who sent it and why, it may do several things at once:

  • Declare a breach. It points to the missed payment, the reversed ACH debit, or the covenant you allegedly violated.
  • Accelerate the balance. Most business-financing and MCA agreements contain an acceleration clause. Once triggered, the funder can demand the entire remaining balance at once — not just what you missed. That is why the number in the letter is often far larger than a single payment.
  • Set a deadline. Commonly 5 to 15 days to “cure” the default or pay in full.
  • Threaten next steps. Litigation, a confession of judgment, a UCC lien enforcement, or contacting your guarantors.

Recognizing which of these it’s doing tells you how urgent it is. A soft “past-due reminder’’ from a servicer is different from an attorney letter that accelerates the balance and references a confession of judgment you signed at funding.

The Real Clock Isn’t the Date on the Letter

The letter sets a deadline, but the deadline that matters is different. Unlike a court summons — which carries a legally binding window to respond — a demand letter’s date is set by the creditor. What you’re really racing is escalation: how soon after the date passes will the funder file a lawsuit, or, if you signed one, submit a confession of judgment that can produce a judgment without a trial. Because that escalation can happen fast, treat the letter’s date as a real deadline and respond before it. Waiting for the last day — or past it — is how owners lose the pre-lawsuit leverage that makes settlements possible.

The First Few Days: What To Do

Move deliberately, not in a panic:

  • Read the whole letter and keep the envelope. Note the sender (servicer vs. law firm), the claimed balance, the deadline, the account/agreement referenced, and any acceleration or COJ language.
  • Pull your original agreements. Find the contract, the personal guarantee, and any confession of judgment. Compare the claimed balance against what you’ve actually paid — funders sometimes overstate the payoff.
  • Don’t admit or over-promise in writing. Don’t confirm a disputed balance as correct, and don’t agree to a payment you can’t sustain just to buy a week. Everything you write can resurface later.
  • Don’t take a new advance to make the demand go away. Stacking another MCA on top is the exact pattern that produced the default.
  • Open a line — through the right channel. Respond to the collections or legal/workout contact, not the original sales rep, and keep it short and measured.
  • Bring in help fast. A debt-mediation specialist can respond on your behalf and start a negotiation; a business attorney can assess any defenses. The two work together.

How To Respond — and What Not To Say

You do not have to accept the amount demanded, and you do not have to respond emotionally. A good response acknowledges receipt, disputes anything inaccurate without conceding the rest, and proposes a path forward. What to avoid putting in writing:

  • Admissions of the full balance if you dispute the payoff, fees, or whether the funder honored a reconciliation request.
  • Promises you can’t keep. A broken “I’ll pay Friday” hands the creditor evidence and destroys credibility.
  • New signatures. Don’t sign an amended agreement, a new note, or a fresh confession of judgment without review.

If your MCA payments are the problem, a reconciliation request — adjusting the daily debit to true receipts — may be a legitimate part of your response. When the stakes are high, let a specialist or attorney handle the correspondence so nothing you write is used against you.

Why This Is the Best Time To Negotiate

The pre-lawsuit window is the single best moment to resolve business debt. Before filing, the creditor hasn’t spent money on court fees and attorney time, and a lawsuit is slow, expensive, and uncertain even for them. That math favors a deal. Depending on your situation, a negotiation can lead to:

  • A lump-sum payoff at a discount if you can access funds — funders often accept less to avoid litigation.
  • A structured resolution. Business debt mediation negotiates with all of your creditors at once to build one consolidated, lender-friendly plan; reverse consolidation pays off multiple advances into a single lower payment.
  • A pause in escalation while a plan is put together, so the letter doesn’t become a lawsuit.

What NOT To Do

  • Don’t ignore it. Demand letters don’t expire quietly; the next step is usually a lawsuit or a filed judgment.
  • Don’t go silent with the creditor. Engagement is your leverage; disappearing removes it.
  • Don’t admit the full balance if you dispute it, and don’t sign anything new without review.
  • Don’t take another advance to satisfy the demand — it buys days and costs months.
  • Don’t assume the number is final. Accelerated balances and fees are frequently negotiable.

How Long It Takes and What It Costs

Opening a negotiation after a demand letter can happen within days — often before the letter’s own deadline — and a settlement or structured plan can follow over the following weeks. Frequently Asked Questions

What is a business debt demand letter?

A demand letter (or notice of default) is a formal written notice from a lender, MCA funder, or its attorney stating that you have breached the agreement, declaring the full balance due, and demanding payment by a deadline. It often invokes an acceleration clause, so the whole remaining balance — not just the missed payment — is claimed as due. It is usually the last step before litigation, and also a window to negotiate before a suit or judgment is filed.

How long do I have to respond to a demand letter?

There is no single legal deadline the way there is for a court summons — the letter itself sets the date, often 5 to 15 days. The clock that matters more is how long before the creditor escalates to a lawsuit or files a confession of judgment. Because escalation can happen quickly after the stated date, respond or open a negotiation before the letter’s deadline, not after.

Should I ignore a demand letter?

No. Ignoring it rarely makes the debt disappear and usually accelerates the problem — the next step is typically a lawsuit, a filed confession of judgment, or a restraining notice on your bank account. Silence also removes your leverage, since creditors are far more flexible with owners who engage early. You don’t have to accept the amount demanded, but you should respond — carefully, in writing — or have a specialist or attorney respond for you.

Can I negotiate after receiving a demand letter?

Yes, and it’s often the best moment. Before a lawsuit is filed, the creditor hasn’t spent money on litigation and would usually rather collect than sue — which gives you room to negotiate a payoff, a discounted settlement, or a structured plan through debt mediation or reverse consolidation. A debt-relief specialist negotiating on your behalf can turn a threatening letter into a manageable resolution and stop the escalation to court.

What should I not put in writing to a debt collector?

Don’t make admissions or promises you can’t keep. Don’t confirm a disputed balance as accurate, don’t agree to a payment you can’t sustain just to buy time, and don’t sign anything — including a new agreement or amended confession of judgment — without review. Anything you write can be used later. A short, measured response that acknowledges receipt, disputes any errors, and proposes a path to resolve is far safer. When in doubt, let a specialist or attorney handle the correspondence.
