Getting handed a lawsuit — a summons and complaint — over a business debt or merchant cash advance is jarring, but it is not the same as losing. It is the start of a process that has rules, and those rules give you a window to respond, defend, and negotiate. The single most important thing to understand is the deadline. Everything else follows from protecting it. This guide walks through what the papers mean, what happens if you do nothing, and the realistic paths to resolve a business-debt suit. For related situations, see responding to a demand letter (the step before a suit) and what to do when an MCA freezes your account (what a default judgment can lead to).

What Being Served Actually Means

“Served” means you were formally delivered two documents: a summons (which tells you a case has been filed, in which court, and by when you must respond) and a complaint (which lays out the creditor’s claims and the amount demanded). Service can happen by a process server, by mail, or by leaving papers with someone at your business, depending on your state’s rules. Once you’re served, the clock starts. A few things to identify immediately from the papers:

  • The court and case number — which court, and sometimes which county or state (venue can matter).
  • The plaintiff — the funder, a lender, or a debt buyer that purchased the account.
  • The amount claimed — often the accelerated full balance plus fees, interest, and attorney costs.
  • The response deadline — the date by which you must file an Answer. This is the number that governs everything.

The Answer Deadline: The Date That Decides Everything

The deadline to respond — commonly 20 to 30 days from service, though it varies by state and court — is a firm legal deadline, not a suggestion. Within that window you (or your attorney) must file a written Answer with the court: a formal response admitting or denying each claim and raising any defenses. Filing an Answer keeps the case alive and preserves every option you have. Letting the deadline pass does the opposite: it lets the creditor ask the court to rule against you without ever hearing your side. If you need more time, an attorney can sometimes request an extension — but only before the deadline runs. Treat the date on the summons as the most important date in your business right now.

What Happens If You Ignore It

If you don’t respond, the creditor files for a default judgment — a court ruling in its favor by default, because you didn’t contest the case. A judgment is powerful. With it, the creditor can:

  • Serve a restraining notice and bank levy to freeze your business account up to the judgment amount.
  • Garnish or intercept receivables owed to you by customers and processors.
  • Pursue you personally under the personal guarantee most MCA and loan agreements require.
  • Enforce UCC liens against business assets.

A default judgment can sometimes be reopened, but that is harder, slower, and less certain than simply responding on time. Ignoring the suit is the single most expensive choice available.

Your Three Paths

Once you know the deadline, there are three realistic responses — and they are frequently combined:

1. File an Answer and defend

If the claimed amount is wrong, the funder ignored a reconciliation request, the agreement was legally defective, or you have another defense, an Answer puts those issues in front of the court. Even when you expect to settle, filing an Answer protects the deadline and gives you leverage — the creditor now faces a contested case instead of an easy default.

2. Negotiate a settlement

Most business-debt lawsuits end in a negotiated resolution, not a trial. You can pursue a lump-sum payoff at a discount or a structured plan, then have the case dismissed once terms are documented. Being sued does not close this door; it often focuses the other side on getting paid.

3. Restructure the debt

If this suit is one of several stacked positions, the durable fix is to resolve the whole picture. Business debt mediation negotiates with all creditors at once to build one consolidated, lender-friendly plan, and reverse consolidation can combine multiple advances into a single lower payment — a resolution that can include settling the pending case.

Lawsuit vs. Confession of Judgment

These are often confused, and the difference changes your next move. A lawsuit means the creditor filed a case and served you — you have an answer window and a chance to defend before any judgment exists. A confession of judgment (COJ) is a document some borrowers sign at funding that lets a creditor obtain a judgment quickly, sometimes with no lawsuit and no notice to you at all. If you were served with a summons, you still have time to respond. If a COJ was already entered, a judgment may already exist, and the focus shifts to vacating the confession of judgment or negotiating a resolution. Should You Hire an Attorney?

For a filed lawsuit, having a business attorney handle the Answer and any court filings is usually worth it — deadlines, procedure, and defenses are technical, and mistakes are costly. At the same time, most of these cases resolve financially, not at trial. What NOT To Do

  • Don’t miss the answer deadline. It is the one mistake that’s hardest to undo.
  • Don’t ignore the papers hoping it goes away. Silence produces a default judgment.
  • Don’t move or hide funds to dodge a possible judgment — it can expose you to fraud claims and personal liability.
  • Don’t take a new MCA to settle the suit. Another advance deepens the stack that caused it.
  • Don’t assume the claimed amount is final. Accelerated balances, fees, and attorney costs are frequently negotiable.

How Long It Takes and What It Costs

An Answer must be filed within the summons’s window (often 20–30 days), while a negotiated settlement can come together over the following weeks. Attorney fees apply to the litigation side; in a debt-mediation program, fees are typically contingent on a successful restructuring and built into the reduced payment rather than charged upfront. Across every route, the determining factor is how quickly you engage — ideally the day you’re served. Frequently Asked Questions

What should I do if I’m served with a business debt lawsuit?

Don’t ignore it. A summons and complaint start a real case with a hard deadline — usually 20 to 30 days — to file a written Answer. Miss it and the creditor wins by default judgment, after which it can freeze your bank account and levy assets. Read the papers for the court, deadline, and amount claimed; gather your agreements; and get help immediately. You generally have three paths: file an Answer and defend, negotiate a settlement, or restructure the debt. Acting in the first days preserves all of them.

How long do I have to respond to a business debt summons?

It depends on the state and court, but the window is commonly 20 to 30 days from the date you were served, and the summons states the exact deadline. Unlike a demand letter’s date, this is a firm legal deadline. Miss it and the court can enter a default judgment against your business — and often you personally under a guarantee — without hearing your side. An attorney can sometimes get an extension, but only before the clock runs out.

What happens if I ignore a business lawsuit?

If you don’t respond by the deadline, the creditor asks the court for a default judgment and usually gets it. A judgment lets the creditor enforce collection — serving a restraining notice and levy on your business bank account, garnishing receivables, and pursuing personally guaranteed assets. Ignoring the suit doesn’t make it disappear; it removes your defenses and hands the creditor the strongest possible position. Responding, even just to negotiate, keeps you in control.

Is being served a lawsuit the same as a confession of judgment?

No. A lawsuit means the creditor filed a case and served you, and you get a chance to respond before any judgment. A confession of judgment (COJ) is a document some borrowers sign at funding that lets the creditor obtain a judgment quickly, sometimes without any lawsuit or notice. If you were served a summons, you still have an answer window; if a COJ was already filed, a judgment may already exist and the focus shifts to vacating it or negotiating a resolution.

Can I settle a business debt lawsuit after being sued?

Yes. Being sued doesn’t close the door to settlement — most business-debt cases resolve without a trial. You can negotiate a lump-sum payoff at a discount or a structured plan, and the resolution can be documented so the case is dismissed. Filing an Answer to protect the deadline and negotiating a settlement often happen in parallel: the Answer buys time and leverage while a debt-relief specialist works out terms. The earlier you engage, the better the settlement tends to be.
