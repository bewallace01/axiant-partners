A confession of judgment is one of the harshest tools in business lending. By signing it — usually buried in the paperwork at funding — a borrower agrees in advance that the funder can obtain a court judgment without a lawsuit, without notice, and without a chance to defend. The first sign is often a frozen bank account. The good news: a COJ is not necessarily the final word. Depending on where it was filed and how, it may be vacated, and even where it stands, it can be resolved. This guide explains whether and how a COJ gets vacated, the grounds that make it possible, and why vacating and settling usually go together. For the broader situation, start with being sued by an MCA lender or hit with a COJ.

What a Confession of Judgment Is (Briefly)

A confession of judgment (COJ) is a signed document in which a borrower — and often the personal guarantor — consents to entry of a judgment if the funder declares a default. The funder files it with an affidavit, and the court enters judgment, frequently the first the borrower hears of it. With the judgment in hand, the funder can immediately restrain accounts and levy assets. Because it skips the normal lawsuit-and-answer process (see being served with a lawsuit for how that differs), a COJ can feel like being convicted with no trial. That one-sidedness is exactly why courts and legislatures have created ways to challenge it.

Can a COJ Be Vacated?

Often, yes — a business can ask the court to vacate (set aside) the judgment. A COJ is a court judgment, so undoing it means persuading the court that it should not stand. That’s a legal proceeding with a real burden of proof, and success is fact-specific. But COJs are frequently filed quickly and imperfectly, and the underlying MCA agreements are sometimes legally vulnerable, which means genuine grounds are common. Whether yours can be vacated depends on the paperwork, the court, and your state.

Grounds To Vacate a Confession of Judgment

The specific grounds vary by state, but the recurring ones include:

  • Improper venue. COJs are sometimes filed in a county or state with no real connection to the debtor. Filing in the wrong place can be grounds to vacate.
  • A defective affidavit of confession. Many states require the affidavit to meet strict formalities — stating the amount, the facts, and the authorization precisely. A non-compliant affidavit can void the judgment.
  • Fraud or misrepresentation. If the COJ or the agreement was obtained through misrepresentation, that can be a basis to set it aside.
  • Usury or an illegal disguised loan. If the “advance” was really a loan at an illegal interest rate, the judgment built on it may be challengeable. See is my MCA legal.
  • An overstated balance. If the confessed amount exceeds what is actually owed — ignoring payments already made — that discrepancy is a challenge point.
  • Statutory violations. Failure to follow a state’s confession-of-judgment rules can invalidate the filing.

The New York change matters. Because New York courts handled a huge volume of MCA confessions of judgment, its 2019 reform — which barred entry of COJs against debtors who don’t reside in the state — removed one of the funders’ favorite tools for out-of-state businesses. If your COJ was filed in New York against a business based elsewhere, that alone may be a powerful ground. Laws continue to evolve, so an attorney’s current-law review is essential. None of this is legal advice; it’s the map of where the levers are.

How the Process Works

Vacating a COJ is a court process, not a form you file yourself:

  • An attorney files a motion — often by order to show cause — in the court that entered the judgment, asking it to vacate the judgment and laying out the grounds and evidence.
  • The motion can request a stay of enforcement. If restraints or levies are active, it can ask the court to pause collection — and lift a freeze on your account — while the motion is decided.
  • The creditor responds, and the court rules, sometimes after argument.
  • If vacated, the judgment is set aside and any restraints tied to it should fall away.

Because of the deadlines, procedure, and evidence involved, this is work for a business attorney experienced with confessions of judgment — not a do-it-yourself step.

Vacating vs. Settling: Usually Both

Here’s the part owners often miss: vacating a COJ doesn’t erase the debt. If the judgment is set aside, the creditor can still sue on the contract through a normal lawsuit. So vacating is most valuable as leverage — it strips the funder of its fastest, one-sided collection tool and forces it to negotiate on level ground. That’s why the strongest approach is usually two tracks at once: an attorney moves to vacate or stay enforcement, while a debt-mediation specialist negotiates a payoff or structured resolution that actually ends the matter. Settling is frequently faster and more certain than a contested motion, and the threat of vacating makes the settlement better. Reverse consolidation can also fold the position into a single lower payment as part of the resolution.

What NOT To Do

  • Don’t assume the judgment is permanent. COJs are frequently vacatable and always negotiable.
  • Don’t try to vacate it yourself. This is technical court work with hard deadlines; use an attorney.
  • Don’t drain or hide restrained funds. It can turn a civil matter into a fraud allegation and reach you personally.
  • Don’t take a new MCA to clear the judgment. Another advance deepens the stack that caused it.
  • Don’t wait. The longer restraints sit, the more damage they do — act on both the legal and settlement tracks fast.

How Long It Takes and What It Costs

A motion to vacate can take weeks to months depending on the court and the creditor’s response, and it carries attorney fees. A negotiated settlement can move faster and is often the path that ends the matter; in a debt-mediation program, fees are typically contingent on a successful restructuring and built into the reduced payment rather than charged upfront. Frequently Asked Questions

Can a confession of judgment be vacated?

Sometimes, yes. A COJ is a court judgment, but a business can ask the court to vacate (set aside) it on specific grounds — improper venue, a defective affidavit, fraud, usury, an overstated balance, or a failure to follow a state’s COJ rules. Whether it can be vacated is fact-specific and depends on your state and the paperwork. An attorney evaluates the grounds; a debt-relief specialist can pursue a settlement in parallel.

What are the grounds to vacate a confession of judgment?

Common grounds include improper venue, a defective or non-compliant affidavit of confession, fraud or misrepresentation, usurious or illegal underlying terms, an overstated payoff amount, or failure to follow statutory requirements. In New York — where many MCA funders file — a 2019 law barred confessions of judgment against debtors who don’t reside in the state, affecting a large share of out-of-state COJs. Grounds are technical and vary by jurisdiction, so an attorney’s review is essential.

How do you vacate a confession of judgment?

You (through an attorney) file a motion — often by order to show cause — in the court where the judgment was entered, asking it to vacate the judgment and stating the grounds and evidence. If restraints or levies are active, the motion can ask the court to stay enforcement while it’s decided. The creditor responds and the court rules. Because this is a court proceeding with deadlines and procedure, it typically requires a business attorney experienced with confessions of judgment.

Is it faster to vacate a COJ or to settle the debt?

Settling is often faster and more certain. Vacating a COJ can take weeks or months and doesn’t erase the underlying debt — if the judgment is vacated, the creditor can still sue on the contract. Vacating is most valuable as leverage: it removes the funder’s fastest collection tool and brings it to the table. Many businesses use both tracks at once — an attorney pursues the motion to vacate or stay enforcement while a debt-relief specialist negotiates a payoff or structured resolution that ends the matter.

Does vacating a confession of judgment erase the debt?

No. Vacating a COJ sets aside the judgment, but the underlying obligation remains — the creditor can still pursue the debt through a normal lawsuit. What vacating does is take away the funder’s quick, one-sided collection tool and restore your right to defend on the merits. That shift in leverage is often what makes a favorable settlement possible, which is why vacating and negotiating usually go together.
