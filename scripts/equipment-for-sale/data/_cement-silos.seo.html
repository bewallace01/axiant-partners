What is a portable cement silo?

A silo stores bulk powder on site — cement, fly ash, lime — and dispenses it into your plant or mixer on demand. These are road-legal and portable, so the silo travels to the job rather than the job being organised around the silo. They run from two super sacks up to 55 tons.

Mechanically there are three things to understand. The hydraulic drive raises it into position without a crane. Fluidization keeps the powder moving instead of bridging and packing solid in the cone, which is the failure mode that turns a silo into a very large paperweight. And the auger discharges it — an optional 8-inch auger moves up to 1,500 lb a minute, which is what lets a silo keep up with a batch plant rather than starve it.

Bagged versus bulk: what a silo is really for

A cement silo is not really a storage decision. It is a purchasing decision. Bagged cement is the most expensive way to buy cement, and it is also the most expensive way to handle it, because a person has to cut and empty every single bag. Bulk cement is cheaper per ton and it flows. The silo is simply the thing that allows you to buy bulk at all.

So the break-even is a volume question, and you can answer it from your own paperwork rather than anyone's brochure:

  • Take what you currently pay per ton for bagged cement, and get a bulk quote per ton delivered.
  • Multiply the difference by the tonnage you actually go through in a year.
  • Add the labour: the hours your crew spends cutting, lifting and emptying bags, plus the waste in torn and hardened bags nobody wants to count.
  • Compare the total to the payment on the estimator above.

If you are moving pallets of bags every week, this comparison is usually not close — the cement price alone tends to cover the silo before you even reach the labour line. If you buy a few bags a month, it is not close in the other direction, and you should not buy one.

Sizing a cement silo

Match the silo to what it feeds and to how far you are from a bulk delivery:

  • 2 super sacks — a mixer trailer or a small crew. Bulk-ish convenience without the commitment.
  • 6–15 tons — a working plant on a job site. Enough to run without a delivery every other day.
  • 35–55 tons — a production plant, or any site where the bulk haul is long enough that you want to take delivery rarely and in quantity.

The mistake to avoid is sizing purely on your batch plant and forgetting the delivery. On a remote site, the silo that matters is the one big enough that a cement truck coming out to you is worth the trip. A silo pairs naturally with a dry batch plant or a wet batch plant, and it is usually bought alongside one.
