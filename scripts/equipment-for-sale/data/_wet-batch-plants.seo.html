What is a wet batch plant?

A wet batch plant makes concrete and discharges it finished. Aggregate, sand, cement and water are weighed into a central mixer inside the plant, mixed there, and what comes out the other side is concrete ready to place.

That is the whole difference from a dry batch plant, which weighs the same dry materials and drops them into a mixer truck — the truck then does the mixing on the drive to the pour. A wet plant does the mixing itself, so what leaves it is concrete, not ingredients. Which means it does not need a mixer truck to make the product at all: it can discharge into a buggy, a pump, a dumper or a small transport unit. It is the plant you buy when the concrete has to be made where it gets used.

Why produce your own concrete

Contractors buy a plant for one of five reasons, and usually more than one:

  • No minimum-load charges — ready-mix is priced around full loads, and small pours carry a short-load surcharge. When you make it yourself, a one-yard pour costs one yard.
  • You pour on your schedule — not in the delivery window a dispatcher gives you, and with no waiting time on a truck that is late or early.
  • Remote work becomes possible — ready-mix has a working life (ASTM C94 limits a load to roughly 90 minutes or 300 drum revolutions). If the haul to your site is longer than that, no supplier can deliver you sound concrete at any price. A plant on site removes the clock.
  • You own the mix — the design, the slump, the admixtures. If your work is to a spec, that control is worth more than the savings.
  • It can sell — a plant with spare capacity turns into a revenue line supplying other contractors in a market that ready-mix does not serve well.

The trade is that you are now the producer: you buy the materials, you own the quality, and someone has to run it. For most contractors doing regular small-to-mid pours, that trade is clearly worth making. For someone pouring twice a year, it is not.

Sizing a wet batch plant

Two numbers decide it, and they answer different questions. Batch size (1.3 to 4 cubic yards here) sets how fast you can pour. Aggregate storage (up to 24 yards across two bins) sets how long you run before a loader has to feed the plant again.

  • 1.3+ cu yd — small pours, precast, repair and patch work, remote crews. The plant that replaces the short-load fee.
  • 2.5 cu yd — the working contractor's plant. Enough to keep a crew placing without the plant becoming the bottleneck.
  • 4 cu yd — production. Continuous pours, or supplying concrete to others.

The other real decision is automated or manual. Computer weight batching gives you repeatable mix designs and a printable batch record, which stops mattering the moment you are selling concrete or working to a spec — because then it is not a convenience, it is your evidence. Manual batching costs less and is perfectly sound if you are pouring your own work to a mix you already know. Do not pay for automation you will not use; do not skip it if a customer will ever ask what went into the load.

Wet batch plant FAQs

What is the difference between a wet batch plant and a dry batch plant?

A wet batch plant has a central mixer: it mixes the concrete inside the plant and discharges it finished and ready to place. A dry batch plant only weighs and dispenses the dry materials into a mixer truck, and the truck does the mixing on the way to the pour. Put simply, a wet plant produces concrete; a dry plant produces a truckload of ingredients.

How much concrete can a wet batch plant produce?

These plants batch from about 1.3 up to 4 cubic yards at a time, with up to 24 yards of aggregate storage across two bins. Your real output depends on batch size and how quickly the plant gets reloaded, so match the batch size to your pour rate and the storage to how long you want to run between loader trips.

Do I still need a mixer truck?

No. That is the point of a wet plant. It discharges mixed concrete, so it can feed a buggy, a pump, a dumper or a small transport unit directly. A dry batch plant is the one that depends on a mixer truck, because the truck is where the mixing happens.
