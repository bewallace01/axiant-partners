The Placement Is What Gets Approved

A single vending machine is cheap collateral and easy to move, which is why lenders look past it to the place it will earn. What they weigh:

  • Signed location agreements - an office, plant, gym, hospital or school that has agreed to host the machine, with the term and commission in writing.
  • Sales history - for existing operators, telemetry or card-reader reports per machine. It is the closest thing vending has to an income statement for one asset.
  • Deposits that match - vending still takes cash, so bank deposits should line up with the sales you report.
  • Credit and time in business - the usual equipment financing requirements, applied to small tickets.

For a new operator, one signed placement can do more than a higher credit score. Thin or damaged files can review equipment financing with bad credit.

Rules That Change What a Location Is Worth

Three federal rules decide what you can sell, and where. Lenders rarely ask about them, but they change how much a placement will earn:

  • Calorie labeling at 20 machines. FDA requires operators who own or operate 20 or more food vending machines to show calories where buyers cannot see the Nutrition Facts label before purchase. Growing past 20 machines adds a compliance cost to plan for.
  • Schools. Food and drinks sold to students during the school day, including from vending machines, must meet USDA's Smart Snacks standards. A school placement earns on a narrower product list.
  • Federal buildings. Under the Randolph-Sheppard Act, licensed blind vendors have priority to operate vending facilities on federal property. A federal site may not be open to you at all.

None of these stops a deal. They are worth knowing before you build a financing plan around a specific placement.

Micro-Markets and Buying a Route

Micro-markets - open shelving, coolers and a self-checkout kiosk in a workplace - are financed as one equipment package. Much of the cost is installation and fixtures with little resale value, so lenders lean on the host agreement and want its term to cover the loan. See micro-market financing.

Buying an existing route is a business acquisition: you are paying for placements and cash flow as well as machines. SBA 7(a) loans are the usual tool when the price includes real goodwill, and SBA requires at least a 10% equity injection for a change of ownership. See how to finance buying a vending route and business acquisition financing.

When Financing Is the Wrong Move

  • No placement yet. Machines in a storage unit earn nothing and the payment starts anyway. Sign the location first.
  • The gap is inventory, not machines. Product, fuel and repairs between collections are a working capital question; an equipment loan pays for machines.
  • The machine underperforms where it is. Move it before financing another for the same site.
  • A checkout offer you have not priced. Compare its total of payments with an equipment loan first.

What Happens After You Apply

  1. List what you are buying. Machine types, new or refurbished, and the supplier's quote - or, for a route purchase, the asking price and what it includes.
  2. Gather the placements. Signed location or host agreements for where the machines will go, with their term and commission.
  3. Pull your numbers. Recent business bank statements and, if you already run machines, sales reports from telemetry or your card processor.
  4. Compare the offers. Rate or APR, term, down payment, total of payments and any buyout, side by side.
  5. Close and place. The lender pays the supplier (or the seller) and files its lien; you place the machines and start repaying.

Vending machines have no title, so the lender records its lien with a UCC-1 filing and releases it when the loan is paid. Financed machines can still qualify for depreciation and Section 179 in the year they are placed in service; see IRS Publication 946 and your accountant.

Vending Machine Financing FAQs

How do I finance vending machines?

As business equipment: an equipment loan, a $1-buyout lease or an FMV lease, usually over 24 to 60 months. Bring the supplier's quote, recent bank statements and any signed location agreements. Buying an existing route is financed as a business acquisition instead.

What credit score do I need for vending machine financing?

There is no single cut-off. Rates commonly run about 7-9% APR for 720+ credit with two-plus years in business and rise to around 22% with specialty lenders below about 640. Below 580, programs are limited and usually need a larger down payment.

Can I finance vending machines with no money down?

Sometimes, for strong-credit operators with established placements. Newer operators and refurbished machines commonly need 10-20% down.

Can a new vending business get financing?

Yes, but a signed location agreement matters more for a new operator than for anyone else, because it shows where the payment will come from. Expect a larger down payment and a shorter term.

Can I finance used or refurbished vending machines?

Yes. Refurbished machines are common in vending and finance routinely, usually at a slightly higher rate and a shorter term than new.

Is it better to lease or buy vending machines?

Buy for machines you will keep in stable placements; consider an FMV lease when you want the option to return or upgrade units. Compare pay-later and lease-to-own checkout offers on total of payments, not the monthly figure.

Can I finance a micro-market?

Yes. The kiosk, coolers, shelving and cameras are usually financed as one package. Lenders weigh the host agreement heavily, ideally with a term at least as long as the loan.

Can I get a loan to buy an existing vending route?

Yes. Routes are financed as small business acquisitions: SBA 7(a), which requires at least a 10% equity injection for a change of ownership, or an equipment loan on the machines plus a seller note.
