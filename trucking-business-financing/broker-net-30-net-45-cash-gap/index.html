Freight Factoring for Carriers: How to Bridge Net-30 and Net-45 Broker Pay

How factoring turns delivered loads into next-day cash, what it costs, and when quick pay or a credit line is the better fit.

Home › Industries › Trucking › Freight Factoring for Carriers

Updated November 20, 2026

Quick answer

Freight factoring is when a trucking carrier sells its load invoices to a factoring company instead of waiting 30-45 days for the broker or shipper to pay. Once you send the bill of lading and rate confirmation, the factor typically advances 90-97% of the invoice the same or next day and collects from the broker; freight fees often run about 1-3%. On $25,000 of weekly loads at a 95% advance and a 2% fee, you get $23,750 within a day and pay $500. Approval rests mostly on your brokers' credit, so new authorities can qualify.

What is freight factoring?

When a broker pays net-30, you are financing that load for a month. Fuel, tolls, insurance and maintenance are due now, drivers are paid weekly, and the money arrives later. Freight factoring, also called trucking factoring, ends that wait: you sell the invoice to a factoring company, get most of it the next day, and the factor waits on the broker instead of you.

It is a sale of receivables, not a loan, and it is underwritten on the brokers and shippers you haul for. That is why a carrier with a new authority and no credit history can still factor, and why the cash available grows as you run more loads. Owner-operators and small fleets use it most.

Why net-30 and net-45 break carrier cash flow

One net-30 invoice is manageable. Ten of them open at once is real money. To size it, multiply your weekly burn (fuel, fixed costs, payroll and average maintenance) by your true days-to-cash divided by 7. Days-to-cash means delivery to money in the bank, not the invoice date. At 35 days and $18,000 a week, you are floating about $90,000.

Several things push the gap wider than the stated term:

  • Paperwork: a late POD, a rate confirmation that does not match the invoice, or missing lumper and detention receipts can turn net-30 into net-45.
  • Payment runs: many brokers pay on set cycles; miss the cutoff and you wait for the next one.
  • Disputes over unapproved accessorials.
  • Growth: a new truck adds fuel spend immediately while receipts lag, so the float jumps overnight.
  • Buying equipment with cash, which drains the buffer that was covering the gap.

How does freight factoring work?

  1. Set up with a factor. It checks the brokers you haul for and signs you up; many factors also run free credit checks on brokers before you book.
  2. Deliver the load and send the invoice with the bill of lading and rate confirmation.
  3. Get the advance, typically 90-97% of the invoice, the same day or next day.
  4. The broker pays the factor on its normal terms.
  5. The factor releases the reserve, minus its fee.

Here is a week of $25,000 in loads at a 95% advance and a 2% fee:

Step Amount When
Loads delivered and invoiced this week $25,000 Day 0
Advance from the factor (95%) $23,750 Same or next day
Factoring fee (2%) $500 Taken when the broker pays
Reserve released to you $750 When the broker pays, often 30-45 days

Many freight factors add fuel advances at pickup, so drivers have diesel money before the load delivers, plus fuel card discounts and load-board integration.

How much does a freight factoring company cost?

You pay a factoring fee, sometimes called the discount rate. In freight it often lands around 1-3% of the invoice, lower than many other industries, and it is usually quoted per 30 days, so a broker who pays in 45 days costs more than one who pays in 30. Flat fees are easier to compare than tiered fees that climb while an invoice is open. Watch for add-ons that change the real price: setup fees, monthly minimums, wire fees and termination penalties on long contracts. For general benchmarks, see invoice factoring rates.

Recourse or non-recourse? With recourse you buy back an invoice the broker never pays. Non-recourse sounds safer, but in freight it usually covers only broker bankruptcy, not slow pay or disputes, so the everyday difference is smaller than the price gap suggests. Read the carve-outs.

What are the downsides of factoring?

  • Cost: a carrier who qualifies for a bank line pays less with the line.
  • Broker contact: the factor collects from your brokers, so how it treats them matters.
  • Contracts: monthly minimums, long terms and termination fees can lock you in. Prefer short or no-term agreements.
  • Recourse risk: on a recourse deal, an unpaid invoice comes back to you.

Factoring vs quick pay vs a line of credit

Option How it works Best for
Freight factoring Sell each load invoice; cash the same or next day Carriers paid on net-30/45 who need cash every week, including new authorities
Broker quick pay The broker pays early for a fee A few loads with a broker that offers it
Line of credit Draw for fuel and payroll, repay as brokers pay Established carriers with recurring gaps and clean statements
Working capital loan Lump sum sized to a one-time spike A single slow-pay broker or a one-time crunch
Equipment financing Loan secured by the truck or trailer Adding units without draining operating cash

Factoring is the usual fit when the gap repeats every week. A line of credit costs less for an established carrier with clean statements. Working capital for trucking, typically $50,000 to $500,000+, fits a one-time spike, and equipment financing keeps truck purchases from eating your operating cash. If you are a broker rather than a carrier, see freight broker financing.

Shrink the gap before you finance it

  • Submit PODs the same day and log accessorial documents immediately.
  • Match every invoice to the rate confirmation: amount, entity name and remittance details.
  • Track true days-to-cash by broker and cap your exposure to slow payers.
  • Keep two buffers: one for timing and one for repairs.
  • Update a 6-8 week cash forecast every week before you add a truck or a lane.

Avoid covering net terms with stacked daily or weekly debits. They shrink your cash flow permanently, and lenders read them as a red flag. If your statements already show NSFs or thin balances, start with trucking financing options that match the cycle.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is freight factoring?

Freight factoring is when a carrier or owner-operator sells unpaid load invoices to a factoring company for immediate cash. The factor advances most of the invoice, typically 90-97%, the same or next day, then collects from the broker or shipper. When the broker pays, the factor releases the rest minus its fee.

How much does a freight factoring company cost?

Freight factoring fees often run about 1-3% of the invoice, usually quoted per 30 days, so slow brokers cost more. Factoring fees in general run about 1-5%. Ask about setup fees, monthly minimums, wire fees and termination penalties, which change the real cost.

How fast do you get paid with freight factoring?

Once your account is set up, most load invoices fund the same day or within 24 hours of sending the bill of lading and rate confirmation. Many freight factors also offer fuel advances at pickup, before the load delivers.

Can a new trucking authority use factoring?

Yes. The factor is underwriting the brokers you haul for, not your credit history, so a brand-new authority can factor as long as its brokers are creditworthy. Many factors also run free broker credit checks so you avoid hauling for slow payers.

What are the downsides of freight factoring?

It costs more than a bank line for a carrier who qualifies for one, the factor contacts your brokers to collect, and some contracts carry monthly minimums or termination fees. With recourse factoring, an unpaid invoice comes back to you.

Is non-recourse freight factoring worth it?

Usually less than it sounds. In freight, non-recourse protection typically covers only broker bankruptcy, not slow pay or disputes, so the everyday difference from recourse is small. Read the carve-outs before paying extra for it.

How do I estimate how much cash net-30 terms tie up?

Multiply your weekly operating burn (fuel, insurance, payroll, maintenance) by your true days-to-cash divided by 7. At 35 days to cash and $18,000 a week, you are floating about $90,000. Factoring shrinks that float because cash arrives a day after delivery.
