Worked Example: A Mid-Size Loader at an Aggregate Yard

Illustrative, not a quote. A sand and gravel yard replaces a worn loader with a $210,000 mid-size machine, puts 10% ($21,000) down and finances $189,000 at 8% over 60 months.

  • Monthly payment: about $3,832
  • Total interest over the term: about $40,934
  • At an assumed 180 tons loaded an hour for 120 hours a month, the payment works out to about $0.18 a ton

Cost per ton is how a yard owner and an underwriter both read a loader. Bring scale tickets or sales by ton, and the hours on the machine you are replacing, so the payment reads as a small share of what the loader moves.

Quarry, Aggregate and Mining Buyers

  • Fixed-site contracts: a loader feeding a crusher, a transfer station or a mill under contract is strong evidence of use. Include the contract with the application.
  • Mine safety rules: at surface mines and quarries, wheel loaders need rollover protection and seat belts (30 CFR 56.14130), and the operator examines the machine before each shift (30 CFR 56.14100). A used machine without its ROPS is a repair to price in.
  • Seasonal output: aggregate sales follow construction season, so six months of bank statements shows the pattern fairly, and some lenders offer payment plans that skip or reduce winter months.
  • Bundled fleets: a loader bought with haul trucks or a crusher can go on one master lease or loan, with one credit review.

Tires, Buckets and Couplers on the Deal

On a loader, the wear parts are big-ticket items. A full set of loader tires is a substantial cost, and an inspector checks the tread before anything else. If the machine will need tires within a year, negotiate them into the purchase so they are financed at the machine’s rate.

  • Buckets: general purpose, rock, light-material and high-dump buckets differ in price and in what the machine can load. Specify the bucket on the quote.
  • Quick coupler and attachments: forks, grapples and snow pushers are financeable with the loader when they are on the same quote. Bought later, they are usually a separate small deal.
  • Engine tier: new nonroad diesel engines are certified to EPA standards in 40 CFR part 1039; buyers working on public contracts sometimes face fleet emission requirements, so check before buying an older machine.

Buying a Used Wheel Loader

Loaders leave quarries and recycling yards with hard hours on them. Many lenders commonly finance used loaders up to roughly 5-7 years old; older machines may need a larger down payment or a shorter term. Before you sign, check:

  • Hour meter and service records, including fluid analysis on the transmission and axles
  • Articulation joint and loader linkage pins for play
  • Tire condition and size, matched across the set
  • Bucket cutting edge and wear on the floor
  • Whether a dealer or a private seller provides the bill of sale and serial number

More on age limits and inspections: can you finance used equipment.

Lease, Loan or Seasonal Payments

  • Equipment loan or $1 buyout lease: you own the loader at the end. Fits a machine you will run until the tires and pins are on their second set.
  • Fair market value lease: lower payment, return or upgrade at the end. Fits a high-hour application where you want a fresh machine on a cycle.
  • Seasonal or skip payments: payments sized to the months you earn, common for aggregates, snow removal and agriculture.
  • Rental purchase options: some dealers credit part of the rent toward a purchase; compare the total against a loan.

Purchased loaders and $1 buyout leases are generally depreciable and may qualify for Section 179 (IRS Publication 946); confirm with your tax adviser. Compare structures on equipment loan vs lease.

Steps to Finance a Wheel Loader

  1. Get a written quote with model, year, serial, hours, bucket and any coupler or tires.
  2. Show the work: tonnage, a site contract, or the machine being replaced.
  3. Gather the file: 3-6 months of bank statements, plus tax returns and a profit and loss statement at larger tickets.
  4. Compare offers on total cost and the payoff at year three, not only the payment.
  5. Confirm insurance and the loss-payee wording before funding day.

Credit commonly starts around 600, with the best terms at 680+; see credit needed for equipment financing. Own a loader outright? See borrowing against equipment you own.

Wheel Loader Financing FAQ

How much is a wheel loader payment?

At a typical 8%, a $150,000 loader is about $3,041 a month over 60 months and a $325,000 loader about $5,066 over 84 months. These are illustrations, not quotes.

Can you finance a used wheel loader?

Yes. Many lenders commonly finance used loaders up to roughly 5-7 years old. Hours, service records, transmission and axle condition and the tires matter more than the year.

Can tires and buckets be included in wheel loader financing?

Usually, if they are on the same quote as the machine. Tires, buckets, a quick coupler and forks bought later are normally a separate deal.

What credit score do you need to finance a wheel loader?

Most lenders look for about 600 or higher, with the best terms commonly at 680+. A site contract, strong bank deposits or a larger down payment can offset a lower score.

Can a quarry get seasonal payments on a loader?

Often, yes. Some equipment lenders offer payment plans that skip or reduce slow months for aggregates, snow removal and agriculture. Ask for the total cost of the seasonal plan against a level one.

Is it better to lease or buy a wheel loader?

A fair market value lease suits a high-hour machine you plan to replace on a cycle. A loan or $1 buyout lease suits a loader you will keep for many years. Compare the total cost of each.
