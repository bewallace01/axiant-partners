Quick Answer: What Cargo Van Financing Costs

A business cargo van is financed with an equipment loan or a lease, commonly over 36–72 months with 0–20% down and credit from about 600. New Transit, Sprinter and ProMaster vans commonly run $35,000–$75,000 before upfit, and shelving, ladder racks or refrigeration can usually go into the same financing. Why Cargo Van Financing Makes Sense for Delivery & Field Service

High-roof cargo vans are the workhorse for last-mile delivery, trades, and mobile service. They fit dense routes, curb-side stops, and tight job sites better than larger straight trucks. But new vans commonly land in the $35,000-$75,000 range (before racking, wrap, or upfit), so paying cash can pinch operating budgets.

For a courier, the van is the route. For a plumber or HVAC tech, it is the stockroom that drives to the job. In both cases the van starts earning the day it is upfitted, while the invoices it produces are paid days or weeks later.

Vans are among the easiest vehicles to finance for a business because thousands of the same models trade every month, so a lender can price the collateral quickly. What varies is the upfit: a $12,000 refrigeration unit or a full shelving package adds cost the lender may value at less than you paid. Delivery operators and trades use loans and leases to add a van for a new route or technician, or to retire one with high miles. Apply now to compare van lenders. What Is a Cargo Van?

In commercial lending, "cargo van" usually means a Class 2-3 van with an enclosed cargo area behind the cab-Ford Transit, Mercedes-Benz Sprinter, RAM ProMaster, Chevrolet Express, Nissan NV, and similar. Sizes vary from standard roof to extended high-roof; payload stays below what a Class 4+ straight truck would carry.

They are used for parcel and supply runs, trades (HVAC, electrical, plumbing), mobile repair, and local freight. Lenders care how the van earns: route density, contracts, or invoiced jobs that line up with the payment.

Why Cargo Van Financing Is Different

Most cargo vans sit in weight Class 2 (6,001–10,000 lb gross vehicle weight rating) or Class 3 (10,001–14,000 lb) under the federal weight classes. That line matters. A van rated at 10,001 lb or more that crosses state lines in business needs a USDOT number (FMCSA), while no cargo van needs a CDL. Lenders financing a heavier Sprinter or Transit for interstate delivery may ask for the USDOT number and operating status.

The other difference is the upfit. Lenders commonly finance shelving, racks, partitions and refrigeration with the van when it is on the same invoice or a body builder's quote, but they value the van on the chassis. Used vans with reasonable miles qualify, and the model year sets how long the term can run. Lenders that do trucking and logistics work see vans every day, and approvals on clean files often take 1–5 business days (timelines).

Cargo Van Financing Options

The search "cargo van lease" covers three different contracts, and the monthly payment only tells part of the story. Here is how each one treats the van and its upfit.

Equipment loan

The van is titled to your business with the lender as lienholder. Commonly 0–20% down over 24–72 months, with rates that typically run 6–15%. You can change the upfit whenever you like. Typical rates.

Lease to own ($1 buyout)

Paperwork says lease, but you own the van for a nominal amount at the end, so the payment is about the same as a loan. This is what most "cargo van lease to own" offers are.

Fair market value lease

The lowest payment, because you only pay for the van's expected drop in value. Mileage limits and return condition apply, and the upfit may have to come out. Fits fleets that replace vans on a cycle.

SBA 7(a)

Rarely worth it for one van, but useful when vans are part of buying a business or a larger expansion. Loans go up to $5 million (SBA) and take weeks. SBA loans.

Working capital

Covers fuel, insurance deposits and payroll while new routes ramp up. Keep it for operations and finance the van itself on the van. Working capital for trucking.

How Much Does a Cargo Van Cost?

New cargo vans (Transit, Sprinter, ProMaster, etc.) often land between roughly $35,000 and $75,000 before racks, refrigeration, or branding. High roof, AWD, and long wheelbase push the top of the range; basic low-roof units anchor the bottom.

Used cargo vans commonly trade 25-45% below comparable new pricing depending on age, miles, and upfit wear. Many lenders finance used units up to 5-7 model years; older vans may need larger down payments or shorter terms. Inspect body structure, doors, and service records-racking adds value but also wear. A dealer quote with VIN helps lenders lock structure quickly. Financing used equipment guide.

Van loans and leases commonly price around 6–15% over 24–72 months, with credit, down payment, the van's age and how long the business has run deciding where in that range you land. Typical equipment rates.

Vans for Plumbing, HVAC and Electrical Contractors

Trades buyers ask a different question from couriers: can the shelving, ladder racks and parts bins be financed with the van, and can a few vans go on one approval? Usually yes to both, with three things to know.

  • Put the upfit on the quote. Equipment bought from the dealer or a body builder at the same time is commonly included. Upfit bought later is usually financed separately, often at a higher rate.
  • Ask for a fleet line. When you will add a van per new technician, some lenders approve a total amount you draw on van by van, rather than a new application each time.
  • Show the work. Service agreements, maintenance contracts and job invoices make a stronger file than bank deposits alone.

When to Apply for Cargo Van Financing

Apply when you have the van picked and the upfit quoted, not when the van arrives. Upfit work at a body builder can add weeks, and an approval in hand lets you commit to an order or a van on the lot without losing it.

Delivery operators adding a route should apply as soon as the route agreement is signed, since the agreement is the best document in the file. Trades adding a technician can apply when the hire is confirmed.

Tips to Get Approved for Cargo Van Financing

Title it to the business.

A van bought on a personal auto loan builds no business credit and can complicate the Section 179 treatment. Apply in the company's name.

Pick a common configuration.

Mainstream roof heights and wheelbases resell easily, which lenders price in. Very specialized builds can mean more down.

Quote the upfit with the van.

One invoice, one approval. Shelving added later is usually a separate, smaller and costlier loan.

Match the term to the miles.

A courier van running long days may be worn out before a 72-month term ends. A shorter term keeps you ahead of the balance.

Bring your contracts.

Route agreements and service contracts move a borderline file further than a few extra points of credit.

Common Mistakes to Avoid When Financing a Cargo Van

Signing a fair market value lease for a van you will rack out.

Return condition can mean removing the upfit and repairing the holes, at your cost.

Ignoring payload after upfit.

Shelving, parts and a partition use payload. Check the remaining capacity against what you carry.

Crossing state lines without a USDOT number.

Vans rated at 10,001 lb or more used in interstate commerce need one. Check the GVWR on the door label.

Comparing payments, not totals.

A low payment over a long term on a high-mileage van can cost more than a shorter loan.

Assuming in-house auto lot financing is business financing.

Consumer auto financing on a personal credit file does not build business credit and may restrict commercial use.

Why Businesses Finance Cargo Vans Rather Than Pay Cash

Paying cash for a van makes sense when the business has more cash than it needs, which in delivery and trades is rare. Financing keeps cash for the upfit you buy later, the insurance deposit and the payroll for the driver or technician.

Tax treatment is a frequent reason buyers ask. Heavier SUVs face a lower Section 179 limit, but IRS Publication 946 excludes from that limit a vehicle that fully encloses the driver and cargo area, has no seating behind the driver and no body section more than 30 inches ahead of the windshield, which describes many cargo vans. Confirm with your tax adviser before you count on it.

Cargo Van Financing FAQ

Can you lease to own a cargo van?

Yes. Most "lease to own" van offers are leases with a $1 or other nominal buyout, so you own the van at the end and the payment is close to a loan payment. A fair market value lease has a lower payment but you do not own the van unless you buy it at market value. Cargo van lease vs loan.

How much does it cost to lease a cargo van?

It depends on the price, the term and the residual. In the worked example on our lease vs loan page, a $60,000 van at an illustrative 9% over 60 months is about $1,047 a month on a lease with a 25% residual and about $1,246 on a loan.

What financing options are available for Transit or Sprinter vans?

Equipment loans, lease-to-own, fair market value leases, dealer or manufacturer financing, and for larger projects SBA 7(a). The vehicle brand matters less than the business, the van's age and the upfit.

Can a new business finance a Sprinter or cargo van?

Yes, often with a larger down payment and a closer look at personal credit. A signed route or service contract helps. See financing for new businesses.

Can I finance a cargo van with an SBA loan?

You can, but for a single van an equipment loan or lease is usually faster and simpler. SBA 7(a) makes sense when vans are part of a bigger purchase or expansion.

Can I finance shelving and racks with the van?

Usually, when the upfit is on the dealer invoice or a body builder's quote at the time of purchase. Upfit added later is typically financed separately.

Can you finance a used cargo van?

Yes. Many lenders finance vans up to about 5–7 model years old, with shorter terms and sometimes more down as age and miles rise.

Is Sprinter van financing different from cargo van financing?

No separate product line. Sprinter-style vans are cargo vans and use the same loans and leases; high-roof vans and their upfits just tend to cost more. See the Sprinter van financing guide.
