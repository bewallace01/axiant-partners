Monthly, Seasonal or Annual Payments

A grain or cattle operation that gets paid a few times a year does not have to carry a payment in the months it has no income. Ag lenders commonly offer:

  • Annual payments, due after the main harvest or calf sale.
  • Semi-annual payments for two crops or spring and fall sales.
  • Skip-month schedules that pause payments in the slow months.
  • Deferred first payment, so a tractor bought in spring is first paid for at harvest.

An annual schedule costs a little more interest than monthly, because principal is paid down later. Bring a year of deposits or sales receipts so the lender can see when the money arrives.

Worked Example: A Used 200 HP Tractor

Illustrative, not a quote. A row-crop farm replaces a high-hour tractor with a used 200 HP unit priced at $160,000. It puts 15% ($24,000) down and finances $136,000 at an assumed 8% over six years.

  • Monthly over 72 months: about $2,385 a month, $35,685 total interest
  • Annual over 6 payments after harvest: about $29,419 a year, $40,513 total interest

The annual plan costs more interest but leaves cash in the bank from planting to harvest. The choice depends on when the farm is paid, not on which payment looks smaller.

Financing a Used Tractor

  • Age and hours: many lenders commonly finance used tractors up to about 7-10 years old. Hours tell the lender more than the model year; a low-hour older tractor can finance better than a worn newer one.
  • Term follows life: the older the tractor, the shorter the term a lender will offer.
  • Private sales and auctions: lenders want a bill of sale, the serial number, a lien search and often photos or an inspection. Auction purchases usually need the approval in place before bidding.
  • Check the drivetrain: engine, transmission, hydraulics and PTO. Repairs after funding are yours.

Dealer Promotions vs a Bank or Equipment Lender

New tractors are often sold with promotional financing from the manufacturer’s finance arm, sometimes at low or zero rates for set terms. Before taking it:

  • Ask for the cash price. Some promotions are offered in place of a cash discount or rebate, so the “free” rate is paid for in the price.
  • Compare total cost: the rebate plus an outside loan against the promotional rate.
  • Check what the promotion covers. Promotions usually apply to new units only; used tractors, mixed-brand implements and private sales are where a bank or independent equipment lender fits.
  • Watch the blanket lien. Some farm lenders take a lien on all equipment; a tractor-only lien keeps the rest of the fleet free to borrow against.

Lease or Loan for a Tractor

  • Loan or $1 buyout lease: you own the tractor at the end. Fits a tractor you will run for many seasons.
  • Fair market value lease: lower payments and a return or trade at the end, with hour limits; extra hours are billed. Fits operations that trade every few years.
  • Seasonal rental: for a few weeks of extra capacity, renting can cost less than owning.

Purchased tractors and $1 buyout leases are generally depreciable, and some may qualify for Section 179 (IRS Publication 946); farm property has its own depreciation rules, so confirm with your tax adviser. See equipment loan vs lease.

How Long Can You Finance a Tractor?

SourceTypical or maximum term
Equipment loan or leaseCommonly 36-84 months
FSA operating term loanUseful life or 7 years, whichever is less
SBA 7(a)Up to 10 years; longer only for longer-life assets

FSA terms come from 7 CFR 764.254; SBA maturities from 13 CFR 120.212. A longer term lowers the payment and raises the total interest, and it can leave you owing more than the tractor is worth late in the term.

FSA Farm Loans as an Alternative

USDA’s Farm Service Agency lends to family farms that cannot get enough credit elsewhere, and its operating loans may be used to buy farm equipment (7 CFR 764.251).

  • Direct operating loans: up to $400,000; direct farm ownership loans up to $600,000 (7 CFR 761.8).
  • Guaranteed loans are made by a bank with an FSA guarantee; their limits are raised each year with the Prices Paid by Farmers Index.
  • Trade-off: FSA terms can be attractive, but approval takes longer than an equipment lender’s 24-48 hours, so it suits a planned purchase rather than a breakdown in planting season.

Compact Tractors for Landscapers and Small Operations

Landscapers, nurseries, horse farms and property-maintenance companies finance compact tractors with loaders and mowers as business equipment. Lenders look at the business’s revenue rather than farm income, and the smaller ticket usually means a lighter file. Financing on this site is for business use only; a tractor for a personal property is a consumer purchase. Related: skid steer financing.

Steps to Finance a Tractor

  1. Get a written quote with serial, hours and any implements listed.
  2. Gather the file: 3-6 months of bank statements and, on larger deals, tax returns with Schedule F.
  3. Pick a payment schedule that matches when you are paid.
  4. Compare the dealer promotion with an outside offer on total cost.
  5. Confirm insurance and the lien terms before funding.

Tractor Financing FAQ

How much is the payment on a financed tractor?

It depends on the price, rate and term. At an assumed 8%, $90,000 over 72 months is about $1,578 a month and $150,000 over 84 months about $2,338. These are illustrations, not quotes.

How long can you finance a tractor for?

Equipment loans and leases commonly run 36-84 months. FSA operating term loans run the useful life of the tractor or 7 years, whichever is less, and SBA 7(a) loans can reach 10 years.

Can you finance a used tractor?

Yes. Many lenders commonly finance used tractors up to about 7-10 years old. Hours and condition matter more than the year, and older tractors get shorter terms.

Can tractor payments be made once a year?

Often, yes. Ag lenders commonly offer annual or semi-annual payments timed to harvest or livestock sales. An annual schedule costs somewhat more interest than monthly.

Is 0% dealer financing on a tractor a good deal?

Sometimes. Promotions may replace a cash rebate, so compare the rebate plus an outside loan against the promotional rate on total cost. Promotions usually apply to new tractors only.

What credit score do you need for tractor financing?

Many lenders look for about 550 or higher, with the best terms commonly at 680+. Farm income history and a larger down payment can offset a lower score.
