How to Finance a Tractor

Costs, loan options, requirements, and approval process for farmers and ranchers

← Back to Tractor Financing

Last updated: March 5, 2026

Tractors cost $80,000–$400,000+ new depending on horsepower and type (row-crop, utility, specialty), and typically 30–50% less used. Most farmers and ranchers finance equipment instead of paying cash. Equipment loans and leases spread payments, preserve working capital, and match costs to seasonal revenue.

What Is an Agricultural Tractor?

An agricultural tractor is a vehicle designed for pulling implements, powering PTO (power take-off) attachments, and performing fieldwork. Tractors power planting, tillage, haying, mowing, loading, and material handling. Row-crop tractors handle planting and cultivation; utility tractors handle general farm tasks; specialty tractors include narrow models for orchards and high-clearance models for specialty crops. Horsepower ranges from roughly 25 HP for compact tractors to 600+ HP for large row-crop and articulated units. Tractors are essential for crop and livestock operations of all sizes.

How Do You Finance a Tractor?

Use equipment loans, leases, or SBA loans. Most lenders require credit 550+, down payment 0–20%, and proof of revenue. Approval typically takes 1–5 days. Tractors are essential agricultural equipment with strong resale value, so financing is widely available through agriculture lenders.

How Much Does a Tractor Cost?

Tractor prices vary by horsepower, type, brand, and whether you buy new or used. Understanding the cost range helps you plan equipment financing and set realistic expectations.

New Tractor Prices

New tractors range from roughly $80,000–$150,000 for utility and compact row-crop models (80–150 HP) to $150,000–$400,000+ for large row-crop and high-horsepower units (200–600+ HP). Top brands like John Deere, Case IH, New Holland, and Kubota add cost. Horsepower is the primary price driver. Factor in attachments and delivery when budgeting.

Used Tractor Prices

Used tractors typically cost 30–50% less than new equivalents, depending on age, hours, horsepower, and condition. A 5-year-old 150 HP tractor might run $50,000–$90,000. Older or smaller used units can start around $25,000–$50,000. Many lenders finance used equipment up to 7–10 years old; older tractors may face shorter terms or require larger down payments. Always inspect hours, engine, transmission, and hydraulics before purchasing used.

Factors Affecting Price

Horsepower, type (row-crop vs utility), brand, hours (for used), cab vs open station, and regional demand all affect tractor pricing. Auction and dealer prices can differ. Obtain a written quote from your dealer or seller—lenders use this to structure your financing.

Financing Options for Tractors

Several financing structures work for tractors. Choosing the right one depends on cash flow, tax situation, and whether you want to own the tractor at the end of the term. See equipment loan vs lease for a detailed comparison.

Equipment Loans (Term Financing)

Equipment loans are the most common option. You borrow a set amount, make fixed monthly payments, and own the tractor when the loan is paid off. Loans typically require 0–20% down, with terms of 24–84 months. Interest rates typically range from 6–15% depending on credit and lender. Tractors hold value well, so lenders often offer competitive terms. Ideal if you plan to use the tractor long-term and want to build equity.

Equipment Leasing

Leasing typically offers lower monthly payments than loans because you're paying for use, not full ownership. At lease end, you may return the tractor, purchase it at fair market value, or upgrade. Operating leases treat payments as operating expenses for tax purposes. Leasing suits operations that want to preserve cash flow, upgrade regularly, or prefer off-balance-sheet treatment.

SBA Loans

SBA 7(a) and 504 loans can finance tractors with longer terms (7–10+ years) and lower down payments. Approval typically takes 30–60+ days. SBA loans suit established operations planning equipment expansion or combining with working capital. USDA guaranteed loans also support agricultural equipment. Requirements include 2+ years in business (typically), solid credit, and adequate cash flow.

Working Capital Loans

Working capital loans are flexible but generally carry higher rates and shorter terms. They're better suited for seed, fuel, and operating expenses than equipment purchases. Use working capital for day-to-day needs; use equipment financing for the tractor itself to secure better rates and terms tied to the asset.

Lenders evaluate several factors when approving tractor financing. Meeting these requirements improves your approval odds and terms.

Credit score: Most equipment lenders look for 550 or higher. Scores of 680+ typically qualify for the best rates and lowest down payments. See credit score requirements for equipment financing for tiers. Because tractor financing is asset-backed, some programs work with 550+ when revenue and down payment are strong.

Down payment: Expect 0–20% down depending on credit and equipment. Strong credit may qualify for 0% down; weaker credit often requires 15–20%. A larger down payment can improve approval and reduce monthly payments.

Time in business: Most lenders prefer 1–2+ years. Newer operations may need a co-signer, larger down payment, or may qualify through SBA or USDA programs.

Revenue and documentation: Lenders want to see that your operation can support the payments. Prepare bank statements, tax returns (Schedule F or business returns), profit & loss, and equipment quote. Farm and ranch income documentation is standard for agricultural lenders.

Step-by-Step: How to Finance a Tractor

  1. Determine equipment type and cost. Choose the tractor that fits your horsepower and implement needs. Get a written quote from the dealer or seller including delivery.
  2. Gather business financial documents. Collect bank statements (3–6 months), tax returns, profit & loss, and a balance sheet. Have your equipment quote and business info ready.
  3. Compare lenders. Work with a broker or compare multiple agricultural equipment lenders. Axiant Partners matches farmers and ranchers with lenders experienced in agriculture equipment financing.
  4. Submit application. Complete the lender's application with business and equipment details. Provide the quote and financial documentation.
  5. Receive approval and funding. Approval often takes 1–5 business days. Once approved, sign documents and coordinate funding with the dealer.

Tips to Get Approved for Tractor Financing

  • Improve your credit score. Pay down balances, correct errors on your report, and avoid new credit applications before applying.
  • Provide strong revenue documentation. Clean bank statements, consistent deposits, and organized financials speed approval.
  • Consider used equipment. A quality used tractor may cost significantly less. Inspect hours and condition carefully.
  • Make a larger down payment. Putting more down reduces lender risk and can lower your rate when credit is borderline.
  • Choose equipment with strong resale value. Major brands hold value better, making lenders more comfortable.

Tractor Financing FAQ

Can you finance a used tractor?

Yes. Many lenders finance used tractors, typically those 7–10 years old or newer. Used equipment may require a larger down payment and shorter terms. Hours, horsepower, and condition affect approval.

What credit score is required for tractor financing?

Most lenders look for a credit score of 550 or higher. Scores of 680+ typically qualify for the best rates and terms. Agricultural equipment financing is asset-backed, so some programs work with 550+ when revenue and down payment are strong.

How long does tractor financing approval take?

Approval typically takes 1–5 business days for equipment loans and leases. SBA and USDA loans can take 30–60+ days. Having documents ready speeds the process.

Can startups finance tractors?

Startups face tighter requirements. Most equipment lenders prefer 1–2+ years in business. New farmers may need larger down payments, shorter terms, or a co-signer. SBA and USDA programs can help newer operations.

Is leasing better than buying a tractor?

It depends. Leasing offers lower monthly payments, tax benefits, and easier upgrades. Buying builds equity and works if you plan to use the tractor long-term. Compare both based on your cash flow and ownership goals.
