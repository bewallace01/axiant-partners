Five sources do most combine deals, and each fits a different buyer:

Upper Midwest and Plains buyers see all five; the difference is mostly how each one treats seasonal income.

Financing the Combine and Header Together

Yes, and it's the normal way to do it. When the dealer sells both, the combine and its draper, flex or corn head go on one contract with both serial numbers listed, so there's one approval and one payment. Heads typically add $50,000-$150,000+, which is why the head has to be on the quote before you apply.

  • Trade-in equity on your old combine or head usually counts as the down payment.
  • A second head (a corn head plus a draper) can go on the same deal if you buy them together.
  • A used head from a different seller usually needs its own approval, or an add-on to the first loan.

Grain carts and grain handling equipment are often financed in the same season; ask whether one lender will take the whole package.

Annual, Semi-Annual or Monthly Payments

Ag lenders commonly set payments to when you sell grain, not to the calendar. Here is the same $400,000 at an illustrative 8% over 7 years:

Payment schedulePaymentTotal interest
Monthly (84 payments)$6,234.49$123,697
Semi-annual (14 payments)$37,867.59$130,146
Annual (7 payments)$76,828.96$137,803

Fewer payments cost a little more in interest because the balance stays outstanding longer between payments. The trade-off is cash flow: an annual payment due after harvest, or after you market stored grain, avoids paying through the months with no crop income. Pick the date from your marketing plan.

Combine Lease Rates and Hour Limits

There is no single published combine lease rate. A lease payment depends on what the lessor expects the machine to be worth at the end, so it moves with the model, the term and the hours you're allowed to put on it. Most combine leases set a yearly separator-hour or engine-hour allowance and charge for hours over it.

  • Fair market value lease: the lowest payment; you return it, renew or buy at market value.
  • Lease with a set or $1 buyout: costs close to a loan and ends in ownership.

A custom harvester running far more acres than one farm usually exceeds hour caps and does better owning. A farm that trades every few years for new technology is the classic lease buyer. More on the choice: equipment loan vs lease.

Worked Example: A Combine and Draper Head on One Contract

Illustrative, not a quote. A grain farm buys a $400,000 combine and a $100,000 draper head, $500,000 in total, and trades in its old combine for $100,000 of equity.

  • Financed: $400,000 at 8% over 7 years, annual payments
  • Annual payment: about $76,829, the first one due after the next harvest
  • Total repaid: about $537,803, so about $137,803 of interest
  • Spread over 3,000 harvested acres, the payment is about $25.61 per acre per year

The price, rate and acreage are assumptions inside the typical ranges above. Swap in your own acres to see what the machine costs per acre.

Combine Financing FAQ

What financing options are available for purchasing a combine?

Dealer and manufacturer programs, Farm Credit lenders and ag banks, independent equipment lenders, USDA FSA direct operating loans for farmers who can't get credit elsewhere, and leases. Terms commonly run 5-7 years, with annual or semi-annual payments available from many ag lenders.

Can you finance a combine and header together?

Yes. When the dealer sells both, the combine and its draper, flex or corn head usually go on one contract with one approval and one payment. A used head bought from another seller usually needs its own approval.

Can combine payments be annual?

Yes. Many ag lenders offer annual or semi-annual payments timed to harvest. As an illustration, $400,000 at 8% over 7 years is about $76,829 a year on annual payments, versus about $6,234 a month.

How much does a new combine cost?

New combines typically run $300,000-$400,000 for mid-size models and $400,000-$500,000+ for large ones, before the head. Heads typically add $50,000-$150,000+.

Can you finance a used combine?

Yes. Lenders look at separator and engine hours, condition and the head. Older machines usually get shorter terms and may need more down.

Do combine leases have hour limits?

Most do. Combine leases commonly set a yearly hour allowance and charge for hours over it, which is why high-acre custom harvesters often buy instead.
