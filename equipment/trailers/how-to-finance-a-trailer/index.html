How to Finance a Trailer

Costs, loan options, requirements, and approval process for dry van, flatbed, and utility trailers

← Back to Equipment by Type

Last updated: March 5, 2026

Commercial trailers—dry van, flatbed, utility—cost $3,000–$80,000+ new depending on type, size, and specs, and typically 25–40% less used. Most carriers and contractors finance equipment instead of paying cash. Equipment loans and leases spread payments, preserve working capital, and match costs to revenue.

What Are Commercial Trailers?

Commercial trailers include dry van, flatbed, and utility trailers used for freight, equipment, and materials. Dry van trailers are enclosed and used for general freight. Flatbed trailers have an open deck for oversized loads, steel, and machinery. Utility and dump trailers haul equipment, materials, and debris. Semi trailers (53' dry van, 48' flatbed) complement tractor fleets and range from roughly $25,000 to $80,000+ depending on length and features. Utility trailers (6x12, 7x16) and dump trailers for contractors cost $3,000–$25,000+. Trailers are essential for trucking, construction, and landscaping businesses.

How Do You Finance a Trailer?

Use equipment loans, leases, or SBA loans. Most lenders require credit 600+, down payment 0–20%, and proof of revenue. Approval typically takes 1–5 days. Trailers are common equipment, so financing is widely available through trucking and equipment lenders.

How Much Does a Trailer Cost?

Trailer prices vary by type, size, and whether you buy new or used. Understanding the cost range helps you plan equipment financing.

New Trailer Prices

New 53' dry van trailers typically cost $35,000–$55,000. Flatbed trailers (48', 53') range from roughly $30,000–$80,000+ depending on length and specs. Utility trailers (6x12, 7x16) cost $3,000–$15,000. Dump trailers for contractors run $8,000–$25,000+. Reefer and specialty trailers cost more. Top brands like Great Dane, Wabash, Utility, and PJ add cost.

Used Trailer Prices

Used trailers typically cost 25–40% less than new equivalents. A 5-year-old dry van might run $20,000–$35,000. Older utility trailers can start around $1,500–$5,000. Many lenders finance used equipment up to 5–10 years old. Inspect frame, axles, tires, and flooring before purchasing used.

Factors Affecting Price

Type, length, capacity, specs (alu vs steel, reefer vs dry), brand, and regional demand all affect trailer pricing. Obtain a written quote from your dealer—lenders use this to structure your financing.

Financing Options for Trailers

Several financing structures work for trailers. See equipment loan vs lease for a detailed comparison.

Equipment Loans (Term Financing)

Equipment loans are the most common option. You borrow a set amount, make fixed monthly payments, and own the trailer when the loan is paid off. Loans typically require 0–20% down, with terms of 24–84 months. Interest rates typically range from 6–15%. Trailers hold value, so financing is often accessible.

Equipment Leasing

Leasing typically offers lower monthly payments than loans. At lease end, you may return, purchase, or upgrade. Operating leases treat payments as operating expenses. Suits carriers and contractors who want to preserve cash flow or cycle equipment.

SBA Loans

SBA 7(a) loans can finance trailers with longer terms and lower down payments. Approval typically takes 30–60+ days. Suit established businesses combining equipment with working capital.

Trailer Financing Rates and Terms

Interest rates for trailer financing typically range from 6–15% for equipment loans and leases. Terms commonly run 24–84 months for semi trailers; shorter for utility trailers. A $40,000 dry van financed at 8% over 60 months would result in roughly $811/month. Use our financing calculator to model different scenarios.

Step-by-Step: How to Finance a Trailer

  1. Determine trailer type and cost. Choose dry van, flatbed, utility, or specialty trailer based on your use. Get a written quote from the dealer.
  2. Gather business financial documents. Collect bank statements (3–6 months), tax returns, profit & loss, and equipment quote.
  3. Compare lenders. Work with a broker or compare multiple equipment lenders. Axiant Partners matches businesses with lenders for trucking and equipment financing.
  4. Submit application. Complete the lender's application with business and equipment details. Provide the quote and financial documentation.
  5. Receive approval and funding. Approval often takes 1–5 business days. Once approved, sign documents and coordinate funding with the dealer.

Tips to Get Approved for Trailer Financing

  • Improve your credit score. Pay down balances, correct errors, and avoid new credit before applying.
  • Provide strong revenue documentation. Clean bank statements and freight or contract history strengthen your application.
  • Consider used equipment. A quality used trailer may cost significantly less. Inspect frame, axles, and flooring carefully.
  • Make a larger down payment. Putting more down reduces lender risk and can lower your rate.

Trailer Financing FAQ

Can you finance a used trailer?

Yes. Many lenders finance used trailers—dry van, flatbed, utility—typically those 5–10 years old or newer. Used equipment may require a larger down payment and shorter terms. Condition, specs, and brand affect approval.

What credit score is required for trailer financing?

Most lenders look for a credit score of 600 or higher. Scores of 680+ typically qualify for the best rates and terms. Asset-backed programs may work with 580+ when revenue and down payment are strong.

How long does trailer financing approval take?

Approval typically takes 1–5 business days for equipment loans and leases. SBA loans can take 30–60+ days. Having documents ready speeds the process.

Dry van vs flatbed—does it affect financing?

Lenders finance both. Dry vans and flatbeds typically cost $25,000–$80,000+ and are common trucking equipment. Price and condition matter more than trailer type.

Is leasing better than buying a trailer?

It depends. Leasing offers lower monthly payments and easier upgrades. Buying builds equity and works if you plan to use the trailer long-term. Compare both based on your cash flow.
