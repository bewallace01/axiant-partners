How to Finance a Skid Steer

Costs, loan options, requirements, and approval process for construction, landscaping, and agriculture businesses

Skid steers cost $30,000–$100,000+ depending on size and attachments. Most construction, landscaping, and agriculture businesses finance equipment instead of paying cash. Equipment loans and leases spread payments, preserve working capital, and match costs to project revenue.

What Is a Skid Steer?

A skid steer is a compact loader with rigid lift arms, wheels or tracks, and a variety of swappable attachments. Its design allows it to turn in place by spinning each side independently ("skidding"), making it highly maneuverable in tight spaces. Skid steers are used in construction, landscaping, agriculture, and material handling for tasks like grading, trenching, loading, snow removal, and demolition. Popular attachments include buckets, pallet forks, augers, grapples, and trenchers, making one machine adaptable to many jobs.

How Do You Finance a Skid Steer?

Use equipment loans, leases, or SBA loans. Most lenders require credit 600+, down payment 0–20%, and proof of revenue. Approval typically takes 1–5 days. Skid steers are versatile and widely used, so financing is often accessible for construction and landscaping businesses.

How Much Does a Skid Steer Cost?

Skid steer prices vary by size (rated operating capacity), wheels vs tracks, brand, and whether you buy new or used. Understanding the cost range helps you plan equipment financing and set realistic down payment and monthly payment expectations.

New Skid Steer Prices

New skid steers range from roughly $30,000–$55,000 for compact models (1,000–1,500 lb capacity) to $55,000–$80,000 for mid-size machines (1,500–2,500 lb capacity), and $80,000–$100,000+ for large skid steers (2,500+ lb capacity). Track loaders (sometimes called "compact track loaders") typically cost more than wheel models. Top brands like Bobcat, Caterpillar, John Deere, and Kubota command premium prices. Attachments add cost; factor in delivery when budgeting.

Used Skid Steer Prices

Used skid steers typically cost $5,000–$85,000 depending on age, hours, and condition. A 5-year-old mid-size skid steer might run $25,000–$45,000. Older or smaller used machines can start around $5,000–$15,000. Many lenders finance used equipment up to 5–7 years old; older machines may face shorter terms or require larger down payments. Always inspect hydraulics and undercarriage before purchasing used.

Factors Affecting Price

Rated operating capacity, wheels vs tracks, brand, cab type, horsepower, attachment compatibility, and regional demand all affect skid steer pricing. Auction and dealer prices can differ. Obtain a written quote from your dealer or seller—lenders use this to structure your financing.

Financing Options for Skid Steers

Several financing structures work for skid steers. Choosing the right one depends on cash flow, tax situation, and whether you want to own the equipment at the end of the term. See equipment loan vs lease for a detailed comparison.

Equipment Loans (Term Financing)

Equipment loans are the most common option. You borrow a set amount, make fixed monthly payments, and own the skid steer when the loan is paid off. Loans typically require 0–20% down, with terms of 24–72 months. Interest rates typically range from 6–15% depending on credit and lender. Because skid steers hold value and are versatile across industries, lenders often offer competitive terms. Ideal if you plan to use the skid steer long-term and want to build equity.

Equipment Leasing

Leasing typically offers lower monthly payments than loans because you're paying for use, not full ownership. At lease end, you may return the equipment, purchase it at fair market value, or upgrade to a newer model. Operating leases treat payments as operating expenses for tax purposes; capital leases function like loans. Leasing suits businesses that want to preserve cash flow, upgrade regularly, or prefer off-balance-sheet treatment. Skid steers with strong residual values often qualify for attractive lease terms.

SBA Loans

SBA 7(a) and 504 loans can finance skid steers with longer terms (7–10+ years) and lower down payments. Approval typically takes 30–60+ days. SBA loans suit established businesses planning larger purchases or combining equipment with working capital. Requirements include 2+ years in business (typically), solid credit, and adequate cash flow. If timing allows, SBA financing can reduce monthly payments significantly.

Working Capital Loans

Working capital loans are flexible but generally carry higher rates and shorter terms. They're better suited for operating expenses than equipment purchases. Use working capital for materials and payroll; use equipment financing for the skid steer itself to secure better rates and terms tied to the asset.

Step-by-Step: How to Finance a Skid Steer

  1. Determine equipment type and cost. Choose the skid steer that fits your jobs—size, wheels vs tracks, attachments. Get a written quote from the dealer or seller including delivery and any add-ons.
  2. Gather business financial documents. Collect bank statements (3–6 months), tax returns, profit & loss, and a balance sheet. Have your equipment quote and business info ready.
  3. Compare lenders. Work with a broker or compare multiple equipment lenders. Consider rates, terms, down payment requirements, and approval speed. Axiant Partners matches businesses with lenders experienced in construction equipment and landscaping equipment.
  4. Submit application. Complete the lender's application with business and equipment details. Provide the quote and financial documentation. Most applications can be submitted online.
  5. Receive approval and funding. Approval often takes 1–5 business days. Once approved, sign documents and coordinate funding with the dealer. Funds typically go directly to the seller; you take possession of the skid steer.

Tips to Get Approved for Skid Steer Financing

  • Improve your credit score. Pay down balances, correct errors on your report, and avoid new credit applications before applying.
  • Provide strong revenue documentation. Clean bank statements, consistent deposits, and organized financials speed approval and improve terms.
  • Consider used equipment. A quality used skid steer may cost less and require a smaller loan, improving approval odds and cash flow. Inspect hydraulics and undercarriage carefully.
  • Make a larger down payment. Putting more down reduces lender risk and can lower your rate or improve approval when credit is borderline.
  • Choose equipment with strong resale value. Major brands and popular models hold value better, making lenders more comfortable.

Skid Steer Financing FAQ

Can you finance a used skid steer?

Yes. Many lenders finance used skid steers, typically those 5–7 years old or newer. Used equipment may require a larger down payment and shorter terms. Resale value and condition affect approval. Get an inspection and maintain records of maintenance and hours.

What credit score is required for skid steer financing?

Most lenders look for a credit score of 600 or higher. Scores of 680+ typically qualify for the best rates and terms. Because skid steer financing is asset-backed, some programs work with 580+ when revenue, down payment, and time in business are strong.

How long does skid steer financing approval take?

Approval typically takes 1–5 business days for equipment loans and leases. SBA loans can take 30–60+ days. Having documents ready—financials, equipment quote, business info—speeds the process. Apply early if you need equipment by a specific date.

Can startups finance skid steers?

Startups face tighter requirements. Most equipment lenders prefer 1–2+ years in business. New businesses may need larger down payments, shorter terms, or a co-signer with strong credit. SBA loans and some specialized programs can help newer businesses.

Is leasing better than buying a skid steer?

It depends. Leasing offers lower monthly payments, potential tax benefits, and easier upgrades when the lease ends. Buying builds equity and works if you plan to use the skid steer long-term. Compare both based on your cash flow, tax situation, and ownership goals.
