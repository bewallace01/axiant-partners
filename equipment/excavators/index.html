What Is an Excavator?

An excavator is a heavy construction machine with a boom, arm (stick), and bucket used for digging, trenching, demolition, and material handling. It moves on crawler tracks or wheels and rotates 360 degrees. Excavators range from compact mini excavators (under 6 tons) to large machines (30+ tons) that move massive amounts of earth.

Excavators are commonly used in construction, mining, road building, and land development for tasks like digging foundations, grading sites, loading trucks, demolition work, and utility installation. General contractors, heavy civil operators, site development companies, and specialty trades rely on excavators as core equipment. Understanding what excavators are and how they're used helps lenders assess your financing application-they want to see that the equipment supports revenue-generating work.

Why Excavator Financing Is Different

Lenders view excavator financing favorably because excavators hold value well and have a strong secondary market. Caterpillar, Komatsu, and John Deere models retain 50-70% of value after 5 years. If a borrower defaults, the lender can recover a significant portion through resale. That reduces risk and often translates to competitive rates and terms compared with unsecured credit.

Construction and earthmoving contractors are the primary excavator buyers. Lenders may evaluate your contract backlog, bonding capacity, and project pipeline-not just static financials. Project-based revenue is common; seasonal demand and draw schedules affect cash flow. Lenders who specialize in construction equipment financing understand these dynamics and structure loans around the equipment's useful life. Mini excavators (under 6 tons) often finance more easily than large machines due to lower ticket size and broader buyer demand. Equipment financing approval timelines are typically faster than SBA or commercial real estate loans.

How Much Does an Excavator Cost?

Excavator prices vary by size, brand, and whether you buy new or used. New excavators range from roughly $80,000 for compact/mini models (under 6 tons) to $150,000-$250,000 for mid-size machines (10-25 tons), and $250,000-$500,000+ for large excavators (30+ tons). Top brands like Caterpillar, Komatsu, and John Deere command premium prices. Hydraulic excavators with advanced features, cab options, and attachments add cost. Factor in delivery, setup, and any attachments (buckets, thumbs, hammers) when budgeting.

Used excavators typically cost 30-50% less than new equivalents. A 5-year-old mid-size excavator might run $70,000-$120,000. Older machines or those with high hours sell for less but may require more maintenance. Many lenders finance used excavators up to 5-7 years old; older machines may face shorter terms or require larger down payments. Always get an equipment inspection before purchasing used. Size (machine weight), brand, engine tier, cab type, attachments, and regional demand all affect pricing. Obtain a written quote from your dealer or seller-lenders use this to structure your financing. Financing used equipment guide.

What to Have Ready Before You Apply

Excavator files turn on the condition of two components a lender cannot see from a quote: the undercarriage and the hydraulics. Documentation that speaks to both moves the file faster.

Quote showing make, model, year and serial

· The serial is what the UCC-1 filing attaches to. Without it there is nothing to perfect a lien against.

Hour meter reading and, if available, service history

· Machines with documented scheduled servicing appraise higher than identical machines without it.

Undercarriage condition report on a used purchase

· Track, roller and sprocket wear is the largest single deferred cost on a tracked excavator. Dealers will provide a percentage-remaining figure if asked.

3–6 months of business bank statements

· Consistent deposits matter more than a single strong month.

Tax return and current profit and loss statement

· Twelve months of trading history is the usual threshold for standard programs.

Details of any attachments in the deal

· Thumbs, couplers, breakers and grading buckets belong on the original invoice if you want them financed on the same terms.

If the machine is Tier 4 Final and headed for a jurisdiction with emissions requirements, keep the emissions documentation with the file. It is occasionally requested and rarely to hand.

When to Apply for Excavator Financing

Apply when the quote is written and before you need the machine on site. Excavator approvals typically run one to five business days when documentation is complete, but a private-party purchase, an unusual configuration or a machine being imported can add a week that nobody budgeted for.

There is also a timing argument specific to excavators. The used market is deepest at the end of the construction season and at the large auctions, and the buyers who do well there are the ones who arrive with financing already approved and a ceiling they have agreed in advance. Turning up to bid without that is how contractors end up paying cash they needed for payroll.

Tips to Get Approved for Excavator Financing

Have the undercarriage measured, not estimated.

Percentage remaining is a measurable figure. A machine at 70% is worth materially more than one at 30%, and the difference usually exceeds any rate you are negotiating.

Match the machine to the work you actually bid.

Underwriters look for coherence. A 35-tonne excavator financed by a contractor whose jobs are residential foundations raises a question that a 14-tonne machine does not.

Include the coupler and thumb in the original quote.

They materially change what the machine can do and they are financeable when they are on the invoice.

Document utilization on machines you already own.

If your existing fleet runs full weeks, say so with hour meter readings. It is the clearest evidence that another machine will be busy.

Consider a slightly older, lower-hour machine.

Hours drive value more than model year. Buying on hours often lowers the amount financed without lowering the capability.

Apply through more than one channel.

Common Mistakes to Avoid When Financing an Excavator

Most excavator financing problems are visible before signing if you know where to look:

Treating hours as the only measure.

A 4,000-hour machine that dug trench all its life is not the same asset as a 4,000-hour machine that ran a breaker. Ask what the work was.

Overlooking transport and setup.

Anything above about 20 tonnes needs a lowboy and, in many places, a permit. That cost belongs in your payment calculation, not beside it.

Financing attachments separately after closing.

The second transaction is smaller, shorter and usually dearer. Decide the configuration before the quote is issued.

Skipping the inspection on an auction unit.

Auction machines sell as-is. An independent inspection costs a fraction of a hydraulic pump and is the cheapest insurance in the transaction.

Signing before reading the insurance requirement.

Equipment lenders require specific coverage and loss-payee wording. Confirm your policy meets it before funding day rather than on it.

Compare at least two or three offers on total cost, not headline rate, and confirm how the lender treats early payoff.

Why Businesses Finance Excavators Rather Than Pay Cash

Cash spent on equipment is cash not available for payroll, materials or the next opportunity. Financing spreads the cost over the working life of the excavator and keeps reserves intact, and Section 179 and bonus depreciation reduce the after-tax cost further. Contractors paid on draw schedules feel it most sharply: mobilization, materials and payroll all fall due before the first progress payment arrives.

Section 179 and the After-Tax Cost of Buying

Financed equipment can often still be deducted. Under IRS Section 179 and bonus depreciation, businesses can frequently deduct a portion — sometimes all — of qualifying equipment cost in the year the machine is placed in service, even when it is financed and you have only made a down payment. Limits and rules change yearly, so confirm current figures with your CPA. For many contractors this materially lowers the true after-tax cost of buying versus renting.

Attachments, Transport, and the All-In Cost

Thumb buckets, hammers, and long fronts change collateral value and insurance limits. If you are financing attachments alongside the base machine, disclose everything on the quote so the lien filing matches the funded collateral. Mixed or incomplete invoices are a common source of closing delays.

Model mobilization and lowboy transport into your cash flow too, so the first month after funding is not a surprise. And keep the two problems separate: use equipment financing for the iron itself, and if mobilization, payroll, or materials timing creates a gap before your first pay application clears, handle that with working capital rather than paying operating-style rates on a long-life asset.

Excavator Financing FAQ

Can you finance a used excavator?

Yes. Many lenders finance used excavators, typically those 5-7 years old or newer. Used equipment may require a larger down payment and shorter terms. Resale value and condition affect approval.

What credit score is required?

Most lenders look for 600 or higher. Scores of 680+ qualify for the best rates. Asset-backed financing sometimes works with 580+ when revenue and down payment are strong.

How long does approval take?

1-5 business days for equipment loans and leases. SBA loans add 30-60+ days. Having documents ready-financials, equipment quote, business info-speeds the process.

Is leasing better than buying?

It depends. Leasing offers lower monthly payments, potential tax benefits, and easier upgrades when the lease ends. Buying builds equity and works if you plan to use the excavator long-term. Compare both based on your cash flow, tax situation, and ownership goals. Equipment loan vs lease comparison.

Can startups finance excavators?

Startups face tighter requirements. Most equipment lenders prefer 1-2+ years in business. New businesses may need larger down payments, shorter terms, or a co-signer with strong credit. SBA loans and some specialized programs can help newer businesses. Apply and we'll match you with lenders that fit your profile.

What documents are needed for excavator financing?

Typical requirements: 3-6 months of business bank statements, tax returns (business and personal if required), a recent profit and loss statement, equipment quote from your dealer, and business formation documents. Having these ready speeds approval. What lenders look at.

How do I estimate monthly payments for excavator financing?

Use our financing calculator to model different scenarios. A $150,000 excavator at 8% over 60 months is roughly $3,000/month. A $80,000 mini excavator at 10% over 48 months is about $2,000/month. Rates typically range 6-15% depending on credit and lender.
