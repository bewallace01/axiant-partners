Quick Answer: What Excavator Financing Costs

Financing an excavator typically costs 6–15% a year over 36–72 months, with 0–20% down and credit from about 600. Machine size drives the payment: an $80,000 compact excavator at 10% over 48 months is about $2,030 a month, and a $150,000 mid-size machine at 8% over 60 months about $3,040, roughly $32,500 of interest over the term. Why Excavator Financing Makes Sense for Contractors

Excavators are essential for construction, mining, road building, and land development. They dig foundations, grade sites, load trucks, and handle demolition. But with prices from $80,000 for compact models to $500,000+ for large machines, paying cash ties up capital you need for payroll, materials, and growth.

An excavator is usually the machine that starts a job: it opens the site, digs the footings or trench, and loads the trucks. That means it is bought at the moment cash is tightest, before mobilization is billed and long before retainage is released. If you already own excavators outright, you can also borrow against the excavators you own instead of paying cash for the next one.

Lenders like excavators as collateral because the major brands trade constantly at dealer and auction, so a machine's value can be checked by serial number and hours. Contractors finance excavators to add a second machine for a bigger contract, step up a size class, or replace one whose undercarriage and hours have caught up with it. What Is an Excavator?

An excavator is a heavy construction machine with a boom, arm (stick), and bucket used for digging, trenching, demolition, and material handling. It moves on crawler tracks or wheels and rotates 360 degrees. Excavators range from compact mini excavators (under 6 tons) to large machines (30+ tons) that move massive amounts of earth.

Excavators are commonly used in construction, mining, road building, and land development for tasks like digging foundations, grading sites, loading trucks, demolition work, and utility installation. General contractors, heavy civil operators, site development companies, and specialty trades rely on excavators as core equipment. Understanding what excavators are and how they're used helps lenders assess your financing application-they want to see that the equipment supports revenue-generating work.

Why Excavator Financing Is Different

An excavator is judged on hours, not miles, and on the parts that wear: the undercarriage (tracks, rollers, sprockets), the swing bearing, the hydraulic pumps and the pins and bushings at the boom and stick. A lender or appraiser will ask for the hour meter reading and the serial number, and on a used machine often for photos of the undercarriage. Machines built in recent years carry EPA Tier 4 Final diesel engines (EPA nonroad engine rules), with after-treatment that adds maintenance, so an older Tier 3 machine can look cheaper to run and is still financeable.

The other difference is size. A compact excavator is a broad market with many buyers, so it finances like a truck. A 40-ton production machine has fewer buyers, so lenders look harder at the contract backlog and the operator's experience. Mining and demolition machines go to lenders who specialise in them.

Excavator Financing Options

Contractors searching "excavator leasing" or "lease to own" are usually choosing between four contracts. The right one depends on how many hours a year the machine will run and whether you want it after the term.

Equipment loan

You own the machine, with a UCC-1 filed against it until it is paid off. Commonly 0–20% down over 36–72 months, at rates that typically run 6–15%. Best for a machine you will run for years. Typical rates.

Lease ($1 out or fair market value)

A $1-out lease ends in ownership and prices like a loan. A fair market value lease has a lower payment and lets you hand the machine back, with hour limits. Pricing on each is in excavator lease vs loan.

Rent-to-own at the dealer

Many dealers offer a rental purchase option: you rent by the month, and a share of the rent commonly applies toward buying the machine if you exercise the option. Useful when a job is confirmed but its length is not. Ask how much rent applies and for how long.

SBA 7(a) or 504

For a fleet purchase or a machine bought with a yard or building, SBA loans can run longer terms; 7(a) loans go up to $5 million (SBA). Expect weeks to close. SBA loans.

Payroll and fuel between draws are a working capital question, not an excavator one.

What to Have Ready Before You Apply

For an excavator, the machine details matter as much as the financials:

Dealer quote or bill of sale

Make, model, serial number, hours, and each attachment listed with its price.

Hour meter photo and service records

For used machines, add undercarriage photos and any inspection report.

Bank statements

Three months is common; six if your work slows in winter, so the lender sees the whole cycle.

Work in hand

Contracts, bid awards or a list of recent jobs. Rental income if the machine will be rented out.

Insurance

An equipment floater quote naming the lender as loss payee.

Larger amounts usually add two years of tax returns and a current balance sheet.

When to Apply for Excavator Financing

Apply in the off-season if you can. Dealers and lenders are less busy in late fall and winter, and you start spring with the machine already paid for in installments rather than waiting on an approval while the job is ready.

If the purchase depends on a contract, apply as soon as the award letter arrives. It is the strongest document you will have, and it tells the lender how long the machine has guaranteed work. Ask about seasonal or skip-payment schedules at the same time; they have to be written into the contract at signing.

Tips to Get Approved for Excavator Financing

Buy the size class your work needs.

A machine too big for your jobs is harder to approve and harder to resell. Lenders notice when the machine and the work do not match.

Lead with operating experience.

Years running excavators count, especially for a newer company.

Get an undercarriage report on used machines.

It is the largest wear item on a tracked excavator and the first thing an appraiser checks.

Put attachments on the same invoice.

One approval covers machine and attachments; buying them later means another loan.

Ask for seasonal payments up front.

If winter is slow, lower payments in those months can be built in at signing.

Common Mistakes to Avoid When Financing an Excavator

Buying on model year alone.

A newer machine with heavy hours and a worn undercarriage can be worth less than an older, lighter-used one.

Ignoring transport.

A bigger machine may need a bigger trailer and truck to move between jobs. Price the whole setup.

Signing an FMV lease without checking hour limits.

Excess hours at return are billed, sometimes at a rate that wipes out the lower payment.

Assuming rent-to-own is cheap.

Check how much of the rent actually applies to the purchase, and for how many months.

Taking the first dealer program without comparing.

Promotional rates can come with a higher price or fewer included attachments. Compare the total cost.

Why Businesses Finance Excavators Rather Than Pay Cash

A contractor that pays cash for an excavator often ends up borrowing anyway, just later and on worse terms, when payroll comes due before the first draw. Financing the machine keeps cash for mobilization, materials and the weeks before retainage is released.

Ownership also brings depreciation, and Section 179 may allow much of the cost to be deducted in the year the machine goes into service (IRS Publication 946). Lease payments are treated differently. Confirm the treatment with your tax adviser before you choose.

Excavator Financing FAQ

How much does it cost to finance an excavator?

At typical rates of 6–15%, an $80,000 compact excavator at 10% over 48 months is about $2,030 a month, and a $150,000 mid-size machine at 8% over 60 months is about $3,040, or about $32,500 of interest over the term. Your rate depends on credit, down payment and the machine.

How long can you finance an excavator?

Commonly 36–72 months. Compact excavators often top out around 48–60 months; new larger machines can run 72, and SBA loans longer when the machine is part of a bigger project.

Can you lease to own an excavator?

Yes. A $1-out lease ends in ownership, and many dealers offer rent-to-own where part of the rent applies to the purchase. A fair market value lease has the lowest payment but ends with a buy, return or renew choice. Excavator lease vs loan.

Can you finance a used excavator?

Yes. Lenders judge used machines on hours, undercarriage and brand more than on age alone; older or high-hour machines usually mean a shorter term and more down. Used excavator financing.

What credit score do excavator lenders want?

Many programs start around 600, with the best pricing near 680 and above. Operating experience and contracted work can offset a lower score.

Can a new contractor finance an excavator?

Often, if the owner has years of operating experience, a down payment, and work lined up. A compact or used machine is easier to approve first.

Can I finance attachments with the excavator?

Usually, when they are on the same invoice: buckets, thumbs, breakers and tiltrotators.
