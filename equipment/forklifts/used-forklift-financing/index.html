Used Forklift Financing: Loans and Leases for Pre-Owned Lift Trucks

How to finance used forklifts—age limits, down payment, credit requirements, and what lenders evaluate

Quick answer

Yes. A used forklift is financed with an equipment loan or lease secured by the truck, commonly over 24–60 months with 10–20% down and rates inside the typical 6–15% range. A $24,000 three-year-old electric truck at 10% over 36 months is about $774 a month. On a used truck, lenders look hardest at the hour meter, the battery on an electric unit, and a legible data plate that matches the mast and attachments. Why Buy a Used Forklift

A new electric sit-down truck commonly costs $25,000–$45,000 and a propane or diesel unit $30,000–$55,000, and used trucks often sell 25–40% below that (forklift costs by type). The discount is the point, but the reasons people buy used are more specific: a second shift that needs one more truck, a peak season that needs two more for four months, a yard truck that will only move pallets between trailers, or a backup so one breakdown does not stop the dock. For those jobs, a well-kept used truck does the same lifting as a new one. Pair it with conveyor or racking financing if you are building out a whole area.

Which Used Forklifts Lenders Finance

Almost any common lift truck can be financed used. What changes is how easy the truck is to resell if the lender ever has to, and so how much down and how long a term you are offered. This is how lenders typically see each type:

TypeWhere it worksWhat lenders check
Electric sit-downIndoor warehouses, docksBattery age and charger; the most common used truck
Reach / order pickerHigh rackingMast height and fit to your racking; fewer buyers
Cushion-tire propaneIndoor and smooth floorsEngine hours, LP system, emissions condition
Pneumatic-tire ICYards, lumber, outdoorHours, tires, drivetrain wear
Rough terrainConstruction sitesHours and boom wear; often financed by construction lenders

Mainstream capacities in the 3,000–6,000 lb range have the deepest resale market. A specialized truck, such as a very high-reach order picker built for one building, can still be financed but usually with more down.

Hours, Age and the Battery

Many equipment lenders finance used forklifts up to about 5–7 years old, and some go to 10 years for low-hour trucks with service records. Each lender sets its own hour limit, so ask before you shop rather than after. A low-hour older truck can be a better credit than a newer one run on three shifts.

On an electric truck, the battery is often the deciding item. A lead-acid battery near the end of its life can cost a large share of the truck's used price to replace, so lenders and buyers ask for its manufacture date, its charging history if known, and whether the charger is included and matches. A lithium-ion conversion can extend a truck's life but should be documented on the quote. Propane and diesel trucks are judged on engine hours and service history instead.

The Data Plate and Attachments

Federal workplace rules require the truck's nameplates and markings to be in place and legible, and any modification that affects capacity needs the manufacturer's prior written approval with the capacity plate changed to match (OSHA 29 CFR 1910.178(a)(4) and (a)(6)). If a non-factory attachment such as a clamp or side-shifter is fitted, the truck must be marked with the combined weight and capacity. On a used truck this is a practical check: a missing or mismatched plate is a safety problem you inherit, and an appraiser treats it as a red flag. Ask the seller to confirm the plate reflects the current mast and attachments before you sign.

Down Payment, Trade-Ins and Rebates

Used trucks commonly need 10–20% down, compared with 0–10% on new; stronger credit sits at the low end, and older or high-hour trucks can need more. That cash does not have to come from the bank account:

  • Trade-in equity. If you trade a truck you own, the dealer's trade-in credit (its value minus any payoff) usually counts toward the down payment. Get the trade value in writing on the quote.
  • Rebates and discounts. A dealer rebate that lowers the price lowers the amount financed. Ask whether it is applied to the price or paid to you later.
  • Bundled equipment. A charger, battery or attachments on the same invoice can usually be financed in the same loan.

Terms and Rates on Used Forklifts

Terms on used trucks commonly run 24–60 months, shorter than the 36–60 months typical on new, because the lender keeps the term inside the truck's remaining working life. Rates sit inside the usual 6–15% range for equipment, often toward the higher end for older trucks and lower credit. Illustrative payments:

Worked Example: A Used Electric Forklift With a Trade-In

Illustrative, not a quote. A warehouse buys a three-year-old electric sit-down truck for $24,000 and trades in an older propane truck the dealer credits at $4,000, with nothing owed on it. The trade covers the down payment, so $20,000 is financed at 10% over 48 months.

  • Monthly payment: about $507
  • Total repaid: about $24,300, or about $4,300 of interest
  • Without the trade-in, financing the full $24,000 on the same terms is about $609 a month

Used Forklift Lease or Loan

Both work on used trucks. A loan or a $1-out lease ends with you owning the truck; that is what most "lease to own forklift" offers are. Fair market value leases, with a lower payment and a hand-back option, are more often written on newer trucks, because the lessor is betting on the truck's value at the end. If you want a used truck for a short peak, compare a lease with simply renting. The trade-offs are laid out in forklift lease vs loan.

Pre-Purchase Inspection Checklist

A dealer-certified truck usually comes with an inspection report. For an auction or private sale, a technician's inspection costs little next to the truck's price and gives the lender something concrete. Make sure it covers:

  • Mast, lift chains and carriage: wear, stretch and play
  • Forks: cracks, bends and heel wear
  • Hydraulics: leaks and drift under load
  • Brakes, steering, tires and the parking brake
  • Battery and charger on electric trucks; engine, LP system and exhaust on IC trucks
  • Hour meter reading, and that the data plate is legible and matches the configuration

What to Send the Lender

  • The quote or bill of sale: make, model, year, serial number, hours, capacity, mast, attachments and price
  • Trade-in details, if any, with the payoff on the truck being traded
  • The inspection report and service history, if you have them
  • For electric trucks: battery date and charger details
  • Three to six months of business bank statements; larger amounts may add tax returns and a profit and loss statement

Complete files on used trucks often decide in the same 1–5 business days as new ones (approval timelines).

Dealer, Auction or Private Seller

Where you buy changes the paperwork. A dealer can arrange financing, take a trade and provide the inspection. An auction usually wants payment within days, so get approved before you bid. A private seller may still owe money on the truck, so the lender will search for existing liens and pay off any lienholder at closing. Frequently Asked Questions

Can you finance a used forklift?

Yes. Most equipment lenders finance used forklifts, commonly up to 5–7 years old and sometimes 10 for low-hour trucks, usually with 10–20% down and a shorter term than new.

Can I use a trade-in as the down payment on a forklift?

Usually. The trade-in credit, meaning the trade value minus any payoff, typically counts toward the down payment. Ask the dealer to show it on the quote.

Can you lease a used forklift?

Yes. A $1-out lease on a used truck works like a loan and ends in ownership. Fair market value leases are more common on newer trucks. See forklift lease vs loan.

What credit score is needed for used forklift financing?

Many programs start around 600, with better pricing above about 680. Lower scores can work with a larger down payment and a newer, lower-hour truck.

Electric or propane: which used forklifts are easier to finance?

Both are financed routinely. On electric trucks the battery's age and the charger matter most; on propane trucks, engine hours and service history.

What should I check before financing a used forklift?

Hours, mast and chains, forks, hydraulics, brakes, the battery or engine, and that the data plate is legible and matches the mast and attachments, as OSHA 1910.178 requires.
