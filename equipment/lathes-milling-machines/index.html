Manual vs CNC: How Lenders See Each

  • Manual lathes and knee mills are smaller tickets with a long working life and a broad used market. Small amounts are often approved on an application and bank statements.
  • CNC lathes and mills cost more, and the control matters as much as the iron. An older control with hard-to-find parts can shorten the term a lender offers.
  • Benchtop and personal CNC machines for prototyping and small shops are small tickets. Many are sold with the maker's own financing; an outside equipment loan or line of credit is the alternative.

Lenders also look at what the machine lets you quote. A shop adding live tooling or a second spindle to win a specific job is easier to approve when it can show the work.

Tooling, Workholding, Rigging and Power

A machine tool isn't ready to cut when it's delivered. These costs can often be bundled into the same approval if they're on the quote:

  • Tooling, holders, chucks, vises and other workholding
  • CAM software and the first programming or training
  • Rigging, freight and placement
  • Electrical work for three-phase power, and any foundation the machine needs

Lenders call these soft costs and many limit how much of a deal they can be, so list them separately on the quote. Machines and tooling are generally depreciable business property, and some may qualify for Section 179 (IRS Publication 946); ask your tax adviser.

Worked Example: A CNC Lathe With Tooling and Installation

Illustrative, not a quote. A job shop buys a $75,000 CNC lathe and adds $15,000 of tooling, workholding and rigging, a $90,000 package. With 10% down ($9,000), $81,000 is financed at 8% over 60 months.

  • Monthly payment: about $1,642
  • Total repaid: about $98,543, so about $17,543 of interest
  • Over 72 months instead, about $1,420 a month and about $21,254 of interest

Prices and the rate are assumptions inside the typical ranges above. Compare the payment with the margin on the work the lathe brings in.

Buying a Used Lathe or Mill

  • The control: age, whether parts and support are still available, and whether it runs your programs.
  • Spindle: hours, runout and bearing noise.
  • Ways and ballscrews: wear and backlash; ask to see a test cut and a part measured.
  • Paperwork: a bill of sale, and a lien search on a private or auction purchase.
  • Rigging out: moving a machine from another shop has its own cost.

See financing used equipment for lender age limits.

Lease or Buy, and Longer-Term Options

A fair market value lease suits a shop that upgrades CNC equipment every few years. A loan or a $1 buyout lease suits manual machines and anything you'll run for a decade. For a large machinery purchase, the SBA 504 program has a 10-year fixed-rate debenture for long-life equipment (SBA); see the SBA 504 amortization schedule for how a 10-year term pays down. Lathe & Mill Financing FAQ

How much does it cost to finance a lathe?

It depends on the machine, rate and term. As an illustration, a $75,000 CNC lathe at 8% is about $1,521 a month over 60 months, and a $25,000 manual lathe is about $783 a month over 36.

Can you finance a used CNC lathe or mill?

Yes. Lenders look at the machine's age, the control, spindle condition and whether parts are still available. Older controls can mean shorter terms.

Can tooling and installation be financed with the machine?

Often, yes. Tooling, workholding, CAM software, rigging and electrical work can go on the same approval if they're on the quote, though many lenders limit how much of a deal these soft costs can be.

Is it better to lease or buy a milling machine?

Lease if you'll upgrade within a few years; buy, or use a $1 buyout lease, if you'll run it for a long time. Manual mills are usually bought.

Do benchtop CNC machines qualify for equipment financing?

Usually. They're small tickets, often sold with the maker's own financing, and an outside equipment loan or a business line of credit can also cover them.
