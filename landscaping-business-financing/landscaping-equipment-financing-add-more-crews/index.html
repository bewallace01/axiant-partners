How does landscaping equipment financing work?

You borrow against the machine, so the equipment itself is the collateral. Instead of paying for mowers and trailers up front, you spread the cost over the years you use them and keep cash for payroll and materials, which matters in seasonal markets where spending jumps before collections catch up. Lenders look at your credit, time in business and bank statements, plus the equipment’s value. If you already have the demand but not the capacity, financing is often the cleanest way to close the gap. For the full product, see equipment financing.

What should you finance first to add crews?

Start with the machines that remove your current bottleneck: production mowers, a trailer package and one versatile compact unit for install work. That keeps deployment simple and gets crews billable quickly.

  • Primary route capacity: commercial mower or zero-turn packages
  • Crew mobility: truck and trailer support
  • Higher-ticket jobs: skid steer or mini excavator and attachments

Use equipment financing for the machines, and cover short-term operating gaps with working capital or a line of credit.

How do you know a new crew can carry the payment?

Build a simple 90-day ramp for each added crew. Start with route assumptions: properties per day, average ticket, cancellation rate and travel density. Subtract direct labor, fuel and consumables to get each crew’s contribution before overhead, then compare it with the equipment payment and added insurance. If contribution covers those fixed costs with room to spare, the expansion is usually ready to finance.

For example, if one crew can reliably add 18 recurring stops a week at $65 a stop, that is about $1,170 a week before add-ons. Even if labor and variable costs take 55%–65%, there is real contribution left to cover the payment. The point is to show lenders, and yourself, that the expansion rests on capacity math.

What makes crew expansion fail?

Most failures come from treating financing as one transaction instead of a plan: adding machines before route demand is proven, hiring faster than dispatch can handle, ignoring maintenance as the fleet grows, or using expensive short-term money for long-life equipment. Match each product to its job. Equipment financing carries the machines, a line of credit covers timing gaps, and working capital covers seasonal labor and materials.

Local conditions matter too. Dense suburban routes let one crew service more stops a day; spread-out rural routes burn more time and fuel unless pricing reflects it. Northern markets with short seasons need bigger winter reserves. Match the repayment schedule to your real seasonality, not to an industry average.

What documents do lenders want?

  • Recent business bank statements with clean, explainable cash patterns
  • Equipment quotes with vendor details and model specs
  • A short use-of-funds note tied to crews and route expansion
  • Your current debt schedule and payments
  • Seasonality notes: strongest months, shoulder months and your reserve plan

Having these ready before peak season can be the difference between funding before the rush and funding after it.

A 90-day rollout

  • Days 1–30: finalize the equipment mix, confirm vendor timelines and pick the routes that can absorb a crew now. Hire to the delivery date, not before.
  • Days 31–60: deploy the first machines and watch utilization. Fix routing and dispatch before adding the second wave.
  • Days 61–90: compare real contribution with your plan. If crews perform, add the second wave; if not, fix the routes before taking on more payments.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is landscaping equipment financing?

An equipment loan or lease used to buy mowers, trailers, skid steers and other landscaping equipment, with the equipment as collateral. Terms typically run 2–7 years depending on credit, with 0–25% down. These are typical ranges across lenders, not a quote.

What interest rate is typical on landscaping equipment financing?

About 7–9% APR at 720+ credit with two or more years in business, 10–13% at 680–719, 13–16% at 640–679 and 16–22% at 580–639. On a $58,000 two-crew package, that is roughly $1,176 a month at 8% over 60 months up to $2,126 at 19% over 36 months.

What credit score do I need to finance landscaping equipment?

A 600 score opens most equipment lenders. Mowers and other small-ticket machines can be approved from about 550 through specialty lenders. The best rates and the lowest down payments go to about 680 and up.

Can you finance a lawn mower with bad credit?

Often, yes. Commercial mowers are among the easiest approvals, and specialty lenders work with scores from about 550. Expect a higher rate and more down: the 580–639 tier typically sees 16–22% APR with 15–25% down, and below 550 usually needs 25% or more down or a cosigner.

Can a newer landscaping company still finance equipment to add crews?

Yes, in many cases. Newer businesses may need a larger down payment, cleaner personal credit or clear route demand. Starting with smaller machines is usually easier than a large first purchase.

Should I finance all expansion equipment at once?

Usually not. Most operators do better by sequencing purchases around bottlenecks: finance what makes a crew billable first, then add install equipment once the routes prove out.

Can I get lower payments in winter?

Some equipment lenders offer seasonal schedules with smaller payments in slow months. Ask for one before you sign, because it is much harder to add afterwards.

Is it better to lease or finance landscaping equipment?

A loan builds equity in machines you keep for years, such as skid steers and trailers. A lease can suit mowers you replace often. Compare the total cost, including any buyout at the end.
