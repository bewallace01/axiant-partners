Lawn Care Business Loans: Options, Rates and What You Qualify For

For owner-operators and small mowing crews: how to pay for a mower, a trailer, or the slow months, and what lenders will want to see

Quick answer

Most lawn care businesses need one of two things. Equipment financing pays for mowers and trailers, commonly about 6-15% over 24-48 months with 0-20% down. A line of credit or small working capital loan covers payroll and fuel in the slow months. New companies can also look at SBA microloans up to $50,000. Equipment First: Mowers, Trailers and Trucks

Commercial zero-turn mowers commonly run about $6,000-$20,000 new depending on deck size and engine, and stand-on mowers about $7,000-$15,000. Used commercial machines typically cost 25-40% less. Because the mower secures the loan, this is usually the easiest financing for a small operator to get.

  • Credit. Many programs start around 550-600, with the strongest rates and $0-down offers at about 680 or higher.
  • Dealer financing. Often the cheapest route on a new machine during promotions, but it only covers that brand and usually nothing else you need.
  • Bundling. A single loan covering a mower, trailer and handheld gear can be simpler than several small accounts.

For model-level detail, see zero-turn mower financing and commercial mower financing. Equipment you buy may qualify for Section 179 under IRS Publication 946; ask your accountant about current limits.

Worked Example: A Second Mower and Trailer

Illustrative numbers, not a quote. A two-person crew finances a $14,000 commercial zero-turn at 8% over 48 months.

  • Monthly payment: about $342
  • At an assumed $45 per cut, that is about 8 cuts a month to cover the payment

Adding a trailer and gear brings the total to $20,000 at an assumed 9% over 48 months, or about $498 a month. Before signing, test the payment against your slowest month rather than July. If the winter months do not cover it, ask about seasonal payment structures, which some equipment lenders offer, or set the cash aside during the season.

Covering the Slow Months

Lawn care income can stop for months in northern markets and slow sharply in southern ones, while the truck note, insurance and spring startup costs keep coming. A revolving line of credit fits best: draw it in the off-season, pay it down in the peak, and pay interest only on what you use. Apply during the season, when your deposits look their best, not in January when the account is thin.

Be careful with daily-payment cash advances. They are easy to get, but the payments do not stop when the grass does. See working capital for seasonal businesses for how to plan the amount and timing.

Can a New Lawn Care Company Get a Loan?

Yes, though with fewer choices. Most online working capital lenders want 6-12 months or more of deposits. For a company with little history, the realistic routes are:

  • Equipment financing on personal credit, since the mower is collateral. Expect a higher rate or larger down payment.
  • SBA microloans, which go up to $50,000, average about $13,000 and run up to seven years. They can fund working capital and equipment but cannot pay existing debts or buy real estate, and they come through nonprofit intermediaries, which may also ask for training.
  • Used equipment and dealer offers, which reduce how much you have to borrow in the first place.
  • Buying an existing route, where the seller's customers and records can support an SBA 7(a) application.

What Lenders Check on a Lawn Care Business

  • Bank statements, usually 3-6 months. Keep job income in a business account, because cash and personal-account jobs do not count toward your revenue.
  • Customer base. Recurring route or contract customers read better than one-off cleanups, and a route list helps.
  • Seasonality. Lenders expect it, so show a full year if you can, including how you got through the last winter.
  • Existing debt. List mower notes, truck loans, credit cards and any advances.
  • Licenses and insurance. General liability, and a pesticide applicator license if your state requires one and you apply chemicals.
  • A quote or use of funds. "$14,000 for a second mower" reads much better than "working capital."

Which Option Should You Choose?

  1. Buying a machine? Use equipment financing, not a general loan or a credit card, so the term matches the mower's working life.
  2. Same cash gap every winter? Use a line of credit.
  3. Under a year in business? Start with equipment on personal credit or a microloan.
  4. Ready for commercial accounts or more crews? See financing to win commercial contracts and equipment financing to add crews.
  5. Compare total cost, including fees, guarantees and prepayment terms, and get them in writing. The FTC has warned small businesses about misrepresented costs.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I get a loan for a lawn care business?

Yes. Most lawn care owners use equipment financing for mowers and trailers, and a line of credit or working capital loan for slow months. New companies can also look at SBA microloans of up to $50,000.

What credit score do I need for a lawn care business loan?

Many equipment programs start around 550-600, with the strongest rates and $0-down offers at about 680 or higher. Bank lines and SBA loans usually want stronger credit. These are typical ranges across lenders, not a quote.

How much does it cost to finance a commercial mower?

As an illustration, $14,000 at 8% over 48 months is about $342 a month. Rates commonly run about 6-15% with 0-20% down, depending on credit, time in business and whether the mower is new or used.

Can I start a lawn care business with no money?

It is difficult to borrow with no cash and no history. Equipment financing on good personal credit, used equipment, or an SBA microloan are the most realistic routes, and you should expect to put something down.

How do lawn care companies get through winter?

Usually with cash saved in season, a revolving line of credit, and off-season income such as snow removal or cleanups. Apply for a line during the season, when your deposits look strongest.

Are lawn care business loans different from landscaping loans?

The products are the same. Lawn care owners usually borrow smaller amounts for mowers and slow months, while landscaping firms more often finance crews, heavier equipment and commercial contract ramps.
