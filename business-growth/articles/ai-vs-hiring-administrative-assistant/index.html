AI vs. Hiring an Administrative Assistant: What It Really Costs

A full-time admin is one of the biggest fixed costs a small business can take on. Before you post the job, it's worth knowing what the hire really costs, which parts of the work AI can already handle, and how a new salary changes what a lender will offer you.

Most owners reach the same point: the work is coming in, but evenings disappear into email, unanswered leads, review sites, and social posts that never get written. The instinct is to hire an administrative assistant. Sometimes that is exactly right. But a salaried hire is a permanent fixed cost, and fixed costs are what lenders weigh hardest when you apply for a working capital loan, a line of credit, or a term loan. This guide compares AI vs. hiring an administrative assistant on cost, capability, and what each does to your ability to borrow.

How much does it cost to hire an administrative assistant?

A full-time administrative assistant costs a typical U.S. business about $65,000 to $70,000 a year, or roughly $5,400 to $5,800 a month, once wages, benefits, and payroll taxes are counted.

The math starts with salary. The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics puts the median annual wage for secretaries and administrative assistants at $47,460 (May 2024). Wages are only part of what an employer pays: across private industry, wages and salaries make up about 70% of total compensation costs, and benefits and payroll taxes make up the other 30% (BLS Employer Costs for Employee Compensation, June 2026). Dividing $47,460 by 0.70 gives roughly $67,800 a year.

That figure still leaves out recruiting, training, a computer, and software seats. It also leaves out ramp-up: a new hire needs weeks to learn your customers, pricing, and tone, and the owner usually carries much of the work while training them. If the person leaves in the first year, you pay that cost again. Your number will vary with your local market and the benefits you offer. Either way, it's a big monthly bill, and it stays the same in a slow month.

Does hiring an employee affect getting a business loan?

Yes. New payroll lowers the cash flow a lender counts toward your loan payments, so an unmatched hire can shrink the amount you qualify for.

Most lenders start with one question: after paying operating costs, how much cash is left to cover debt? That's your debt service coverage ratio (DSCR). Many lenders want at least 1.25, meaning $1.25 of available cash flow for every $1.00 of loan payments.

Consider a business with $20,000 a month in net operating income and $12,000 a month in existing debt payments. Its DSCR is about 1.67, comfortably bankable. Add a $5,650-a-month admin hire without new revenue to match, and operating income drops to $14,350. DSCR falls to about 1.20, below the line many lenders draw. The owner didn't do anything reckless, but the next loan just got harder to get. Run your own numbers in our DSCR calculator.

That doesn't mean you should never hire. It means a new fixed cost should be matched by new revenue or capacity, ideally before you apply for your next round of capital. For more on that balance, see top-line growth vs. bottom-line profit.

Can AI replace an administrative assistant?

AI can replace much of an administrative assistant's repeatable, text-based work, but not the whole role. It works best when a person approves anything that reaches a customer. Here's what today's AI assistants do well:

  • Inbox triage. Sorting incoming email, flagging what is urgent, and drafting routine replies for you to approve.
  • Lead capture and follow-up. Logging new website inquiries, flagging the ones worth chasing, and drafting the first follow-up so no lead waits overnight.
  • Online reviews. Watching review sites, drafting polite responses, and sending the negative ones straight to you.
  • Marketing posts and website copy. Drafting social posts and page updates in your voice.
  • Website checks and weekly summaries. Flagging when something on your site breaks and sending a short weekly read on activity and spend.

You don't need a separate tool for each job. Small businesses can now use bundled AI business teams, sometimes called an AI secretary or AI admin assistant. Platforms like Lightsky give a small business a set of AI assistants covering the inbox, leads, reviews, marketing, SEO, and website monitoring, with each one drafting the work and waiting for the owner's approval before anything is sent, posted, or published. At the time of writing, Lightsky is free to start and costs $50 a month on its monthly plan, with no per-seat fees. Whatever platform you pick, you're comparing tens of dollars a month in software with thousands a month in payroll.

What AI can't replace

AI has real limits, and it's better to know them before you cancel a job posting. It's a poor fit for:

  • Phone-heavy businesses. Most AI business assistants work in email, chat, and web. If customers mainly call, you need a person, an answering service, or a dedicated AI receptionist product.
  • Physical or in-person work. Greeting patients, handling deliveries, running the front counter, or filing paper.
  • Judgment and relationships. Negotiating with a difficult customer, handling a billing dispute, or any call that needs context the software doesn't have.
  • Regulated or financial tasks. Payroll, bookkeeping, and tax work belong with your accountant or a dedicated system.

Budget some of your own time, too. Approving drafts usually takes a few minutes a day. The software does the writing and keeps track of follow-ups, but someone still has to read what goes out.

How to decide between AI and a new hire

Use this five-step check before you post a job or buy software:

  1. List every admin task for two weeks. Note what it is and roughly how long it takes.
  2. Sort each task into three groups. Automate (repeatable, text-based, low risk if a draft needs editing), hire (phones, in-person, real-time judgment), or keep (pricing, key customers, direction of the business).
  3. Automate the first group. Set up AI assistants with your approval on anything customer-facing.
  4. Re-measure after 90 days. If the hire group is still large and growing, a hire is likely justified.
  5. Check the loan math before you hire. Confirm your DSCR stays above 1.25 with the new payroll, and plan financing for the ramp-up period.

If the hire is justified, tie the role to capacity you can measure, such as more jobs completed, faster quotes, or more appointments kept. Our guide to unit economics before you scale covers how to check that the math works.

Composite example (illustrative, not a real client record): A residential HVAC contractor doing about $1.4 million a year was losing web leads that sat unanswered overnight and had dozens of unreplied reviews. The owner planned to hire a full-time administrative assistant at roughly $5,600 a month all-in. Instead, they set up AI assistants to draft lead follow-ups and review replies for approval each morning, and kept a part-time dispatcher for phones. Response time to web leads dropped from next day to under an hour. Six months later, with the extra jobs booked, they hired a second install crew. The payroll dollars went to a role that produced revenue, and their DSCR stayed above 1.4 when they financed a new service van.

Takeaway: let software handle the routine work, and spend payroll on people who bring in revenue.

How to finance a new hire the right way

When a hire is the right call, plan the cash before the start date. A new employee often takes two to three months to pay for themselves, and payroll is due every pay period regardless. A business line of credit is often the best fit for bridging that ramp-up, because you draw only what you need and pay interest only on what you use. For larger expansions that combine hires, equipment, and marketing, see how to finance growth without a cash flow crisis. Borrow to grow capacity, not to cover overhead you could have avoided (see borrowing to grow vs. borrowing to survive).

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Hiring to fix a process problem. If leads are lost because nothing tracks them, a new person inherits the same broken process. Fix the system first.
  • Letting AI send without review. Anything that reaches a customer should be approved by a person until you trust that type of message completely.
  • Ignoring the lender's view. Adding fixed payroll right before a loan application can lower the amount you qualify for. Time big hires around your financing plans.
  • Stacking single-purpose tools. Separate subscriptions for email, reviews, social, SEO, and reporting often cost more than one bundled platform and create more to manage.

FAQ

How much does it cost to hire an administrative assistant?

About $65,000 to $70,000 a year for a full-time hire at the median wage. BLS puts the median wage at $47,460 (May 2024), and wages are about 70% of total employer compensation costs, so benefits and payroll taxes add roughly $20,000 more.

Can AI replace an administrative assistant?

Partly. AI can take over repeatable, text-based work such as email triage, lead follow-up, review replies, and routine marketing posts. It can't do in-person work, handle sensitive calls, or manage relationships, so many small businesses pair AI assistants with a part-time person.

How much does an AI assistant cost for a small business?

Most AI assistant platforms for small businesses cost about $20 to $100 a month, often with a free tier or free credits to start. That's less than 2% of the monthly cost of a full-time administrative assistant.

What is an AI secretary?

An AI secretary is software that handles office admin work such as sorting email, drafting replies, following up with leads, and responding to reviews. The better tools draft the work and wait for the owner's approval before anything goes to a customer.

Does hiring an employee affect my ability to get a business loan?

Yes. New payroll lowers the cash flow available for debt payments, which reduces your debt service coverage ratio (DSCR). If revenue doesn't rise to match, lenders may approve a smaller amount or a higher rate. Many lenders look for a DSCR of at least 1.25.

When should a small business hire an administrative assistant?

Hire when the admin work that needs a person (phones, in-person service, judgment calls) is large and growing, and when the role frees the owner or team to produce more revenue. If most of the work is repeatable email and follow-up, automate it first and re-measure in 90 days.

Should I use a business loan to pay for a new hire?

Financing a hire makes sense when the role adds measurable capacity or revenue, such as a technician, salesperson, or crew. A line of credit can bridge the first few months of payroll while the hire ramps up. Avoid borrowing to cover admin overhead that software could handle for far less.

Takeaway

An administrative assistant is a long-term fixed cost that lenders weigh against every future loan. Start by moving the repeatable admin work to AI assistants, with your approval on everything customer-facing, then spend payroll and borrowed capital on people who add capacity and revenue. Doing it in that order protects your cash flow and makes your next loan application easier to approve.

Sources: U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Occupational Outlook Handbook: Secretaries and Administrative Assistants; U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Employer Costs for Employee Compensation.
