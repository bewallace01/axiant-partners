Business Expansion Loans: How to Fund Inventory, Equipment and Growth Without a Cash Crunch

Which loan fits each part of an expansion, what it costs, and how to borrow for growth without running short of cash

← Back to Growth Articles All Articles

Updated November 20, 2026

Quick answer

Business expansion loans are the loans you use to grow: a new location, more equipment, bigger inventory or more staff. The main options are an SBA 7(a) loan (up to $5 million, about 10% in 2026, 10-year terms, 30-90 days), a business term loan ($10,000 to $5 million+, 12-84 months, approval in 3-10 days), a line of credit for timing gaps, and equipment or inventory financing secured by what you buy. A $500,000 SBA expansion loan at 10% over 10 years is about $6,608 a month.

Growth uses cash before it returns it. You buy inventory, run ads, hire ahead of revenue or add a machine, and the money comes back weeks or months later. An expansion loan bridges that gap, but only if the repayment schedule matches when cash actually arrives. Most growth-stage cash crunches come from the wrong loan for the job, not from borrowing itself.

SBA loans for business expansion

The SBA 7(a) is usually the cheapest long-term money for an established business that is growing: it can fund a new location, build-out, equipment, inventory and working capital in one loan, at about 10% in 2026 over 10 years. The trade-off is time and paperwork, typically 30-90 days. If the expansion is a building you will occupy, the 504 puts you in with 10% down and a long fixed rate.

Which Loan Fits Which Part of the Expansion?

Match the loan to what the money buys, and the repayment to how that thing pays you back:

  • Inventory and receivables turn over in weeks or months, so they fit a line of credit or short-term inventory financing tied to sell-through.
  • Equipment earns over years, so it fits a term or equipment loan matched to the asset's life. Finance machines that add revenue or cut cost per unit, and hold off on nice-to-have equipment until your current assets are busy.
  • A new location or build-out is a long-payback project: an SBA 7(a) or a term loan of 60-84 months.
  • Marketing is the riskiest thing to borrow for. Debt-fund it only when the unit economics are proven and payback is fast relative to the interest; otherwise fund early tests from operating cash in small amounts.

How Much Does a Business Expansion Loan Cost?

At the rates used across this site: a $500,000 SBA 7(a) at 10% over 10 years is about $6,608 a month. A $150,000 equipment loan at 12% over 60 months, typical for 680-719 credit, is about $3,337 a month. A $50,000 equipment loan runs about $1,014 a month at 8% over 60 months with strong credit, or $1,392 at 15% over 48 months. Whether a rate is too high depends on the profit the expansion will add and how fast it pays back, not on the rate alone.

How Do You Avoid a Cash Crunch When You Borrow to Grow?

  • Build a 13-week cash view before you sign. Model base, upside and downside cases with the actual payment dates. Stress-test late customer payments and a 20% revenue miss. If the downside breaks your minimum cash level, borrow less, phase the plan or change the terms.
  • Draw in phases. Release money as milestones hit: inventory sells through, a campaign hits its cost-per-lead target, a machine passes acceptance. Phasing limits the damage when an assumption is wrong and builds lender confidence.
  • Plan seasonal inventory with a kill switch. Short-term funding for a peak season makes sense when past sell-through supports it. Set reorder points, a markdown plan and the point at which you stop buying if demand misses.
  • Do not stack draws on the same cash. Three growth bets in one quarter should each have their own funding source and repayment timing.

Composite example (illustrative, not a real client record): A light manufacturer needed a CNC line, a larger raw-material buy for a new contract and a modest marketing push in the same quarter. They used a line of credit tied to receivables for the inventory build, a term equipment loan for the machine, and kept marketing on operating cash with a hard cap until the first milestone invoices paid. Cash never went below a pre-set floor.

Takeaway: matching each use of funds to its own loan and timing avoids stacking three draws against the same cash.

What Do Lenders Want to See for an Expansion Loan?

A clear use of funds, the timing, and how the expansion pays the loan back. Most term-loan and line-of-credit programs work with 550+ credit, and 680+ usually gets better terms; most prefer two or more years in business. Typical expansion term loans run from about $100,000 for a smaller project to $1 million or more for a multi-location rollout. For how lenders read growth borrowing versus survival borrowing, see borrowing to grow vs to survive.

Can You Get an Expansion Loan for a New Business?

It is harder. With less than two years in business, options are more limited; some lenders consider newer businesses with strong credit (often 680+) and solid revenue, with lines typically $10,000-$75,000. Equipment financing is often the easiest place to start because the equipment secures the loan. SBA loans for startups are possible but are underwritten on projections and usually need more equity.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are business expansion loans?

Loans used to grow an existing business: opening a location, adding equipment, building inventory or hiring. The usual options are SBA 7(a) loans, business term loans, lines of credit, and equipment or inventory financing.

What is the largest business loan you can get for an expansion?

An SBA 7(a) loan goes up to $5 million, and conventional term loans can go to $5 million or more for qualified businesses. How much you can actually borrow depends on cash flow, credit and collateral.

How much would a $50,000 business loan cost?

As an equipment loan, about $1,014 a month at 8% over 60 months with strong credit, or about $1,392 a month at 15% over 48 months with credit in the 640-679 range. Other loan types price differently, so compare the total cost.

Is an SBA loan good for business expansion?

Often, yes, for an established business. The 7(a) covers a new location, build-out, equipment and working capital in one loan at about 10% in 2026 over 10 years, but it takes 30-90 days to close.

Line of credit or term loan for growth?

Use a line of credit for timing gaps such as receivables, seasonal inventory or hiring ahead of revenue. Use a term loan for a defined asset or project with steady repayment capacity, such as a second location or a machine.

Can I get a loan to expand a new business?

It is harder with under two years in business. Some lenders consider newer businesses with strong credit (often 680+) and solid revenue, and equipment financing is often the easiest start because the equipment secures the loan.
