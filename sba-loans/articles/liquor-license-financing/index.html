Most financing guides for bars and restaurants cover the build-out and equipment, but in many markets the single largest line item is the liquor license itself. This is different from financing the store or restaurant as a business — if you want to fund a turnkey package store, see liquor store financing. This guide is about funding the license: why it costs what it does, which loan products cover it, and how the transfer works.

Why a Liquor License Can Cost Six Figures

States regulate alcohol sales in one of two broad ways. In license-available states, the state issues a license to any qualified applicant for a set fee, so the cost is modest — often a few hundred to a few thousand dollars. In quota states (and quota counties within other states), the number of on-premise or off-premise licenses is capped relative to population, so once they are all issued the only way to get one is to buy it from an existing holder on the open market. Scarcity sets the price, and in dense or high-demand markets that price climbs into the hundreds of thousands. A full liquor license in a popular metro can rival the cost of the build-out, which is exactly why it gets financed.

How to Finance a Liquor License

Because a license is an intangible asset rather than equipment, it is rarely financed on its own with a simple equipment loan. Instead it is funded through business-level products:

  • SBA 7(a) loan. The workhorse. A 7(a) loan can finance the purchase of a business that holds a transferable license, or fund a license purchase tied to a startup or expansion, with long terms and a high loan-to-value for qualified borrowers. See SBA loans and what lenders look for in SBA approval.
  • Business acquisition financing. When you buy an existing bar, restaurant, or store, the license comes with the deal and is financed as part of the purchase price — see buying a business financing guide.
  • Conventional term loan. For established operators with strong cash flow, a term loan can fund a license purchase outright.
  • Working capital. In low-cost license states, the fee is small enough to cover with a working capital loan or your opening budget.

What Lenders Look At

  • Transferability and market value. In quota states a license has documented resale value and can strengthen the collateral package; lenders will want evidence of comparable license sales.
  • The business behind it. Projected or actual food and beverage sales, location, and management experience drive the underwriting — the license is only valuable attached to a viable operation.
  • State ABC approval. The transfer is contingent on the alcohol authority approving the new owner, so lenders structure funding around that approval and the escrow that holds it.
  • Credit and equity injection. Standard SBA expectations apply; buyers typically contribute an equity injection toward the deal.

The Transfer Process

A license purchase usually runs through an escrow that protects both sides while the state reviews the transfer. You agree on a price with the seller (or the price is set within a business-acquisition deal), open escrow, and apply to the state ABC for approval of the transfer and the new licensee. The lender commits financing subject to that approval, funds release when the transfer clears, and the license is recorded to the new entity. Because ABC review can take weeks to months, building that timeline into your loan and lease commitments is essential — the same discipline covered in why SBA approval can take time. None of this is legal advice; work with counsel and your state ABC.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you finance the cost of a liquor license?

Yes. In quota states where a license trades on the secondary market for tens or hundreds of thousands of dollars, the license is usually financed as part of a business-acquisition or expansion loan rather than paid in cash. SBA 7(a) loans can fund the purchase of a transferable license as an intangible business asset, and conventional term loans can too. In low-cost license states, the fee is small enough to roll into working capital.

How much does a liquor license cost?

It varies enormously by state and license type. In license-available states the state fee may be only a few hundred to a few thousand dollars. In quota (population-capped) states and counties, a transferable license bought on the open market can run from roughly $25,000 to well over $1,000,000 depending on the market. These are illustrative ranges, not quotes; check your state ABC authority.

Will an SBA loan cover a liquor license?

Generally yes. The SBA 7(a) program can finance the acquisition of a business including its transferable liquor license, and can fund a license purchase tied to a startup or expansion, subject to underwriting and the license being a legitimate, transferable asset. The license transfer must still be approved by the state alcohol authority. Confirm eligibility with the lender.

Can you use the liquor license as collateral?

In quota states a transferable license has real resale value, so some lenders will consider it part of the collateral package alongside the business and other assets. In states where licenses are not freely transferable, the license has little standalone collateral value and the loan leans on the business cash flow and other security instead.
