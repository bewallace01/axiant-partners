SBA Loan to Buy a Business: Requirements and What Stops Deals

10% down, 1.25x coverage, clean books: what SBA lenders check and how to fix the gaps

Updated November 20, 2026

Quick answer

Yes, an SBA 7(a) loan is the most common way to buy an existing U.S. business. It lends up to $5 million over 10 years (up to 25 for real estate in the deal), and you need at least 10% of total project cost as equity; a seller note on full standby can cover up to half of that. The business's cash flow has to cover the new payment at 1.25x or better, and many lenders look for a 650-680+ credit score. Plan on 30-60+ days to close.

Most SBA acquisition deals don't fall apart because the buyer was a bad risk. They stall on things that could have been fixed months earlier: down payment money nobody can trace, seller books that don't tie to the tax returns, a price the cash flow can't carry. This page covers the SBA requirements for buying a business, what the payment looks like, and the five blockers that stall most files, with the fix for each. If you're still choosing between SBA, a bank loan, seller financing or a portfolio loan, start with the business acquisition financing guide.

What are the requirements for an SBA loan to buy a business?

The SBA sets the floor and each lender adds its own policy on top. For a buyer who doesn't already own the business, the deal falls under an "initial acquisition" in SBA SOP 50 10 8.1, effective October 1, 2026. The core rules:

The business and the way you use the money also have to be SBA-eligible. Lending companies, passive investment and speculation don't qualify, so check eligibility before you sign a purchase agreement. The full list is in SBA loan requirements.

What would the payment be on a $500,000 SBA loan?

About $6,539 to $7,030 a month over 10 years, depending on the rate. SBA 7(a) rates are usually variable, tied to prime, and capped by the SBA. Most banks price at or near the SBA's maximum spread over prime (see typical SBA loan rates). The table uses two illustrative rates, 9.75% and the 11.5% used in the acquisition financing guide; your quote will depend on prime and the lender.

So a $500,000 loan needs a business earning roughly $105,400 a year in SDE, and you still have to pay yourself out of what's left. Estimates only, not a quote.

Can I get a $2 million loan to buy a business?

Yes. $2 million sits well inside the 7(a) limit of $5 million, and with 10% down it supports a price of about $2,222,000. At 11.5% over 10 years the payment is about $28,119 a month, so the business needs roughly $422,000 a year in SDE. At this size the lender takes available equity in the personal real estate of anyone owning 20% or more when the loan isn't fully secured, and a Quality of Earnings report is required once the price reaches $3 million. What stops people from buying a business with an SBA loan?

Five things, and every one of them is easier to fix before you're under contract than after.

1. The business or the use of funds isn't SBA-eligible

Some businesses can't get an SBA guarantee at all; lenders, passive investment companies and speculative ventures are the usual examples. If the target, or the way you plan to use the money, doesn't qualify, no lender can fix that later. Fix: confirm eligibility before you go under contract, and work with a lender that closes SBA acquisitions regularly.

2. Not enough down payment, or money you can't trace

The SBA minimum is 10% of total project cost, and that base includes closing costs and any working capital, not just the price. On a $900,000 purchase that's at least $90,000 before costs, and lenders can ask for more; 10-25% is the usual range across SBA loans. The source has to be documented: savings, the sale of an asset, or a gift with the right paperwork. If you're short, a seller note on full standby can cover up to half of the 10%, or look at a smaller deal. See where an SBA down payment can come from and how to buy a business with 5% down.

3. The cash flow or your credit won't support the loan

The lender gets repaid from the business's cash flow, so those numbers have to work before anything else matters. A business that can't cover the new payment at 1.25x gets declined or sent back to renegotiate the price, and that 1.25x comes before you take a salary. Credit counts too: many SBA lenders want 680+, and 650-679 can work with strong cash flow and a clean file. Fix: run the coverage math on the seller's real numbers first, and pay down card balances and dispute errors 3-6 months before you apply. See what credit score you need for an SBA loan.

4. No relevant experience

Lenders want to know you can run what you're buying. A new industry with no related background makes them nervous. Fix: show transferable management experience or training, put a transition plan in writing, and pick a lender that does acquisitions and can present your case. One catch on the 5%-down structure with a standby seller note: the seller can't stay on as an officer, director, owner or employee, though a consulting contract of up to 24 months is allowed.

5. An incomplete file or slow answers

Acquisition loans need a lot of paper: the purchase agreement, the target's financials and tax returns, your own tax returns and personal financial statement, SBA Forms 1919 and 413, and a use-of-funds narrative. Gaps and slow replies push the closing back or kill the deal. Fix: hand over a complete file at the start and answer every condition within a day or two. Worked example: a $900,000 deal that almost stalled

A buyer is acquiring a $900,000 business with an SBA 7(a) loan and 10% down, so the loan is $810,000. At an illustrative 11.5% over 10 years that's about $11,388 a month ($136,700 a year), and the business needs about $170,800 in SDE to clear 1.25x. The numbers worked. The deal still nearly stalled on three things: the seller's financials weren't clean enough for the lender to verify earnings, part of the buyer's down payment was a recent deposit the lender couldn't source, and there was no transition plan showing the buyer could run the business.

Once the seller produced reconciled financials, the buyer documented where the down payment came from, and the two agreed on a short transition period, the file moved. That pattern holds across most SBA acquisitions: the deal rarely dies on the merits. It dies on the paperwork behind the merits.

Start before you sign

Every blocker above is fixable, but only if you start before a closing clock is running. Season and document your down payment months ahead. Ask the seller for reconciled, lender-ready financials early, and walk away from a deal where the books can't be verified. Get a valuation that supports the price, line up proof of experience or a transition plan, and clean up your credit and existing debt before you apply.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you use an SBA loan to buy a business?

Yes. SBA 7(a) is the most common way to finance an acquisition and can cover goodwill, inventory, equipment and working capital when the business and the use of funds are eligible. It lends up to $5 million over 10 years. You need at least 10% down, cash flow that covers the payment at 1.25x, and credit and experience the lender accepts.

How much down payment do you need for an SBA loan to buy a business?

At least 10% of total project cost, which includes closing costs and any working capital as well as the price. A seller note on full standby can cover up to half of that, leaving about 5% in cash. Lenders can ask for more, and 10-25% is the usual range across SBA loans.

Can you buy a business with an SBA loan and no money down?

No. For a first-time buyer the 10% minimum equity can't be reduced or eliminated. The closest you can get is about 5% in cash, with a full-standby seller note covering the other 5%, and the seller has to agree to take no payments for the term of the SBA loan.

What credit score do you need for an SBA loan to buy a business?

The SBA sets no minimum; each lender does. Many look for 680 or higher, and 650-679 can work with strong cash flow and a clean file. Below 650 is hard and usually takes a bigger down payment or other strengths.

What would the payment be on a $500,000 SBA loan?

About $6,539 a month at 9.75% or $7,030 at 11.5%, both over 10 years. To cover the higher payment at 1.25x, the business needs roughly $105,400 a year in seller's discretionary earnings.

How long does it take to get an SBA loan to buy a business?

Plan on 30-60+ days from application to closing. It runs longer when the seller's books need cleanup or the valuation comes in below the price. Having your down payment documented and the seller's financials in hand before you apply is the biggest time saver.

What stops people from buying a business with an SBA loan?

The usual blockers are an ineligible business or use of funds, a down payment that's too small or can't be traced, cash flow or credit that won't support the loan, and no relevant experience. Incomplete paperwork stalls most of the rest. Each one has a fix if you start before you're under contract.
