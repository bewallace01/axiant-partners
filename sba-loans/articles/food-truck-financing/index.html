Quick answer

Food trucks are usually financed with an equipment or vehicle loan (the truck and kitchen build-out are the collateral), an SBA microloan (up to $50,000, built for startups), or a term loan / working capital. Projects typically run ~$50,000 to $150,000+ depending on new vs used vs conversion. Because most food trucks are brand-new businesses, lenders lean on personal credit, a 10–20% down payment, and the equipment itself — and you should keep separate cash (or a small working-capital loan) for permits, commissary fees, and inventory.

A food truck is a small but capital-intensive launch: a vehicle, a built-in commercial kitchen, permits, and enough cash to survive the first slow months. Most owners are starting from scratch, which shapes which loans are realistic. Here's how to fund the truck and the launch without running out of runway.

Qualifying as a Startup or With Limited Credit

Most food trucks have no business track record, so lenders underwrite the owner: personal credit, available down payment, and any industry experience, with the equipment as collateral. The most accessible startup paths are SBA microloans and CDFI/nonprofit lenders, which are designed for new and underserved owners. A clear business plan, a realistic budget that includes soft costs, and a 10–20% down payment materially improve approval odds when there's no revenue history yet.

Budget for More Than the Truck

  • Permits & licenses (health, fire, mobile-vendor) — vary widely by city.
  • Commissary / commercial kitchen rent, required in many jurisdictions.
  • Insurance, branding/wrap, POS, and initial inventory.
  • A cushion for slow opening months while you build a following.

Financing the truck but forgetting these soft costs is the most common way new owners end up cash-tight. Keep a working-capital buffer separate from the equipment loan.

What Lenders Check

  • Personal credit and available down payment (for startups).
  • The truck/equipment as collateral — new vs used, condition, and resale value.
  • Business plan & budget — realistic, including soft costs and a cushion.
  • Industry experience — prior food-service background helps.
  • Once operating: sales history unlocks easier working capital and lines of credit.

Worked Example: Financing a Food Truck Build

Say an operator wants a fully built-out truck — the vehicle, kitchen equipment, wrap, generator, and permits — for around $120,000. A traditional bank often shies away from a single-vehicle food business, but an SBA 7(a) loan is built for exactly this kind of small, owner-operated venture. With roughly 10% down ($12,000) and a multi-year term, the monthly payment is sized to the truck's expected sales rather than demanding the full cost up front. For an operator with restaurant or culinary experience and a clear concept, that experience often matters more to the lender than a large balance sheet.

The numbers shift quickly for a first-time owner with no food-service track record: expect a larger down payment, a tighter approval, or a request for a commissary agreement and a realistic sales projection before funding. SBA lenders are comfortable with small deals, but they still want evidence the operator can actually run the truck.

What SBA Lenders Weigh on a Food Truck

  • Operator experience — culinary or restaurant background is the single strongest signal for a first truck.
  • Concept and location plan — where you will park, your events calendar, and a commissary or prep arrangement.
  • Permits and health compliance — local mobile-vendor permits and a commissary agreement are often prerequisites.
  • Down payment and personal credit — typically ~10% equity plus a personal guarantee on a 7(a) loan.
  • Realistic projections — sales tied to your actual event and lunch-service plan, not a generic template.

How to Strengthen Your Application

The fastest way to improve a food-truck loan file is to remove the lender's doubts before they ask. Line up your mobile-vendor permits and a commissary agreement so compliance is not an open question. Build a realistic sales projection tied to your actual lunch-service spots and event calendar rather than a generic template, and document any culinary or restaurant experience that shows you can run the operation. Keep your personal credit clean and have your ~10% down payment sourced and ready. If you are a first-time owner, a short stint working in food service — or partnering with someone who has it — can be the difference between a decline and an approval. A well-prepared first-time operator with a clear concept often funds; an under-prepared one with a great recipe usually does not.
