SBA Loans for Restaurants: Buying, Opening or Expanding One

7(a) vs 504, down payment, loan sizes, credit and timelines, with buying an existing restaurant as the easiest path

← Back to SBA Loans Articles All Articles
Home › SBA Loans › Articles › SBA Loan for a Restaurant

Updated November 20, 2026

Quick answer

An SBA 7(a) loan is the usual SBA loan for a restaurant: it can cover the purchase price, kitchen equipment, leasehold improvements, inventory and working capital in one loan, with 10-20% down and up to 10 years to repay. Add an SBA 504 if you are buying the building (10% down, long fixed rate). Lenders want 660-680+ credit, cash flow that covers the payment 1.25 times, and restaurant experience. Buying an existing restaurant is far easier to finance than opening a new one. A $600,000 purchase with 10% down is about $7,362 a month at 10.75% over 10 years.

If you are buying a restaurant that already has sales, an SBA loan is usually the cheapest long-term money you can get, and the 7(a) can roll almost the whole deal into one loan. Opening from scratch is harder, because the lender has nothing to underwrite but your projections. For the wider menu of restaurant loans, see restaurant business financing.

SBA 7(a) for buying a restaurant

The 7(a) is the main program for buying an existing restaurant. One loan can cover:

  • The purchase price (goodwill, recipes, trade name, non-compete)
  • Kitchen equipment, furnishings and fixtures
  • Leasehold or tenant improvements
  • Inventory and working capital for the transition

Lenders underwrite off the restaurant's actual sales, labor, food cost and rent, plus the lease and your experience. A franchise transfer fee can usually be included when it is documented in the deal.

SBA 504 for the building

If you are buying the building the restaurant occupies, a 504 splits it into a 50% bank first mortgage, a 40% CDC loan at a long fixed rate, and your 10%. A newer business or a special-use property can be asked for 15-20%. Use the 504 when the real estate is the main need and the 7(a) when you need the business, equipment and working capital too. See SBA loans for commercial property.

How Hard Is It to Get an SBA Loan for a Restaurant?

Harder than for most businesses. Many SBA lenders decline restaurants and bars as a policy because of thin margins, high failure rates and alcohol liability, so the first job is finding a lender that funds them. Restaurants are eligible; the bar is just higher. What lenders want to see:

  • Credit: 660-680+ FICO preferred. See what credit score you need for an SBA loan.
  • Down payment: 10-20% on an acquisition, 10% on 504 real estate. A first-time operator often lands at the high end.
  • Experience: restaurant, hospitality or management background. Without it, expect a bigger down payment or an industry partner.
  • Cash flow: DSCR of 1.25x or higher, shown by the restaurant's tax returns and P&L.
  • A workable lease: a short or unfavorable lease can sink an otherwise good deal.

Starting a new restaurant is the hardest version. With no sales history, lenders underwrite projections, and many SBA lenders will not do projections-only deals. New build-outs that do get funded usually run $100,000 to $2 million through a 7(a) or a term loan, often with equipment financing for the kitchen on the side.

These ranges include the business price, equipment and often working capital; real estate adds to the loan. The SBA 7(a) itself goes up to $5 million.

On payments, at an illustrative 10.75% over 10 years: a $500,000 SBA loan is about $6,817 a month, and a $1.8 million loan (a $2 million purchase with 10% down) is about $24,541 a month. The restaurant's cash flow has to cover that payment about 1.25 times, which is what usually caps the loan size, not the program limit.

What Lenders Want From a Restaurant Buyer

Restaurant deals improve a lot when you show a credible transition plan, not only the seller's financials. Lenders want to know that sales, staff and vendors survive the change of owner. Focus diligence on labor-cost trend, rent escalations and lease options, sales by channel, and the condition of equipment that will need replacing soon. Then turn it into a 90-day plan:

  • Before closing: check tax returns against POS data, and confirm the lease transfer terms and any liquor license transfer.
  • Days 1-30: keep the labor schedule, inventory ordering and vendor payments steady.
  • Days 31-60: work on menu pricing and delivery-channel profitability.
  • Days 61-90: track labor ratio, food cost and weekly cash, and tighten cash controls.

Seller carry helps when it is structured properly: it can support your equity and lower the lender's risk, but keep enough cash after closing for payroll and inventory rather than stretching to minimize cash at close. Spell out the seller's transition help (how long, doing what) in the purchase agreement; lenders value short, specific commitments over vague promises.

Documents for a Restaurant SBA Loan

On top of the standard SBA package, restaurant buyers usually need:

  • Purchase agreement or letter of intent
  • 2-3 years of the restaurant's tax returns and P&L
  • Equipment list and values
  • Current lease and landlord contact
  • Liquor license documents (if applicable)
  • Seller transition agreement (if applicable)

See SBA loan documents for the full checklist.

When SBA May Not Fit

  • You need the money in under 30 days. SBA loans typically take 30-90 days; see how long SBA approval takes.
  • Your credit is below 660 and cannot be improved quickly.
  • The restaurant's sales are weak or falling.
  • The lease is short or unfavorable.

For other routes, see SBA loan alternatives, or term loans for multi-unit restaurant expansion if you are adding locations.

Bottom Line

For most buyers, an SBA loan for a restaurant means a 7(a) with 10-20% down, plus a 504 if the building is part of the deal. Get the seller's tax returns, the lease and your transition plan together early, and apply with lenders that actually fund restaurants. Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use an SBA loan to buy an existing restaurant?

Yes. SBA 7(a) loans are commonly used for restaurant purchases and can combine the purchase price, equipment, leasehold improvements and working capital in one loan with 10-20% down. Lenders look at the restaurant's historical financials and your experience.

How hard is it to get a loan to start a restaurant?

Harder than buying one. A new restaurant has no sales history, so lenders underwrite your projections, and many SBA lenders will not do projections-only deals or restaurants at all. Restaurant experience, a larger down payment and a solid lease improve your odds.

What would the payment be on a $500,000 SBA restaurant loan?

About $6,817 a month at an illustrative 10.75% over 10 years. A real-estate loan on a longer term would cost less a month, and your actual rate depends on the lender and your file.

Can I get a $2 million SBA loan to buy a restaurant?

Yes, the SBA 7(a) goes up to $5 million. With 10% down on a $2 million purchase, the $1.8 million loan runs about $24,541 a month at 10.75% over 10 years, and the restaurant's cash flow has to cover that about 1.25 times.

Do restaurant franchises qualify for SBA loans?

Yes, as long as the brand is listed on the SBA Franchise Directory. Franchise transfer fees can usually be financed when they are documented in the deal.

What credit score do I need for an SBA restaurant loan?

Most SBA lenders prefer 660-680+ for restaurant loans. Strong cash flow can give some flexibility, and first-time buyers may need industry experience or a larger down payment.

How long does SBA restaurant loan approval take?

Typically 30-90 days from application to funding. Review of the restaurant's financials, the lease and any liquor license transfer drives the timeline.

Does seller carry help an SBA restaurant acquisition?

Yes. A properly structured seller note can support your equity and lower the lender's risk in some acquisitions.
