Lease vs Buy Equipment: Side-by-Side Math on Lease, Loan and Cash

Total cost, monthly payment, taxes and ownership on the same $100,000 machine, three ways

Quick answer

Buying with a loan is usually cheaper if you keep the equipment for its useful life; leasing wins on monthly payment and on equipment that goes out of date fast. On a $100,000 machine over 5 years, an FMV lease at $1,950 a month costs $117,000 and you hand it back. A 12% loan costs $2,224 a month, $133,467 in total, and you own it. After tax at a 25% rate, the lease nets about $87,750 and the loan about $100,100. Paying cash only wins when the money has no better use.

Is Leasing Better Than Buying Equipment?

Not on total cost if you keep the machine. Leasing gives you the lowest payment and an easy exit, and you pay for both. Most owners compare monthly payments, skip the tax effect and never price what their cash could earn elsewhere. Look at all three and the answer is usually clear.

What Does Lease vs Buy Look Like on a $100,000 Machine?

Take a $100,000 machine with a 5-year useful life, a business paying a 25% effective tax rate, and three ways to get it:

  • Lease: an FMV operating lease at $1,950 a month for 60 months. You pay $117,000; deducting the payments saves $29,250, so the after-tax cost is $87,750. That excludes any buyout: keep the machine and you pay fair market value on top.
  • Loan at 12%: $2,224 a month for 60 months, $133,467 in total with $33,467 of interest. Section 179 on the $100,000 cost saves $25,000 in year one, and the interest deduction saves another $8,367, for an after-tax cost of $100,100. You own the machine with nothing left to pay.
  • Cash: $100,000 out today, $25,000 back through Section 179, so $75,000 after tax. If that cash could have earned 8% a year in the business, five years of lost returns adds about $40,000, for $115,000.

Credit changes the answer. At 12%, the rate a 680-719 borrower might see, the lease is about $274 a month cheaper and about $12,350 cheaper after tax, but you end up with nothing. At 8%, the range for 720+ credit with two years in business, the loan costs only $3,494 more after tax than the lease and you keep a machine with five years of work behind it. Cash looks cheapest until you count what the money would have earned. These are illustrations, not quotes; run your own numbers in the equipment payment calculator.

When Does Leasing, a Loan or Cash Make Sense?

Lease when

  • The equipment goes out of date fast: technology, some medical equipment, copiers.
  • You want to upgrade every 3-4 years instead of running the machine for its full life.
  • You cannot use Section 179 because taxable income is low.

Take a loan when

  • You will keep the equipment for its full useful life.
  • Section 179 or bonus depreciation gives you real tax savings this year.
  • The interest rate is below what your cash earns in the business.
  • You want to own it at the end.

Pay cash when

  • The cash has no productive use elsewhere.
  • The purchase is small next to your total operations.
  • The loan rates on offer are unusually high.
  • Staying debt-free matters more to you than the return on that cash.

What Are the Tax Benefits of Leasing Equipment?

With a true (operating) lease, the lessor owns and depreciates the equipment and you deduct each lease payment as an operating expense. The deduction is spread evenly over the term. With a loan or a $1 buyout lease, the IRS treats you as the owner: you can expense the cost under Section 179 or take bonus depreciation in year one, then deduct the interest as you pay it. Paying cash gets the same Section 179 treatment as a loan.

So a lease gives a steady deduction, and buying gives a large one up front, if you have the taxable income to use it. Section 179 cannot create a loss, so a business near break-even may get more from a lease. Limits and bonus depreciation rates change with tax law; check the current figures in our Section 179 guide and with your CPA before you sign.

How Do You Decide Between Leasing and Buying?

  1. Estimate how long you will keep it. Match the structure to your real hold period: lease for 3-4 years, loan for longer.
  2. Price all three options. Loan: payments plus fees, minus tax savings. Lease: payments plus any buyout, minus the payment deduction. Cash: the price minus tax savings, plus what that cash would have earned.
  3. Work out the tax effect with your CPA. A loan or $1 buyout lease can take Section 179 and depreciation; a true lease deducts the payments. Timing matters as much as the total.
  4. Put a number on your cash. If working capital earns more in your business than the loan rate costs, keeping the cash usually wins.
  5. Get real quotes. Get a lease quote and a loan quote on the same machine so you compare actual rates, not examples.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is leasing better than purchasing equipment?

Leasing is better for equipment you will replace in 3-4 years or that goes out of date fast, and when you need the lowest payment. Buying, with a loan or cash, is cheaper if you keep the equipment for its useful life. On a $100,000 machine over 5 years, an FMV lease nets about $87,750 after tax and you return it; a 12% loan nets about $100,100 and you own it.

What are the tax benefits of leasing equipment?

With a true lease, you deduct each payment as an operating expense, spread evenly over the term, and the lessor takes the depreciation. That helps a business that cannot use a large year-one Section 179 deduction. A $1 buyout lease is treated as a purchase instead.

Should leases be capitalized or expensed?

For taxes, a true (operating) lease is expensed: you deduct the payments. A $1 buyout lease is treated as a financed purchase, so the equipment is capitalized and depreciated or expensed under Section 179, and only the interest part of each payment is deductible. Your CPA will confirm how it is booked on your financial statements.

Is leasing or buying equipment cheaper?

Buying with a loan is usually cheaper if you keep the equipment for its useful life. Leasing wins on the monthly payment but typically costs more in total once you buy the equipment out. Cash beats both only when the return you give up on that cash is below the loan rate.

Should I lease, finance, or pay cash for equipment?

Pay cash only if the equipment is cheap relative to your reserves. Finance to keep cash working while you build ownership. Lease for the lowest payment and easy upgrades. The right answer depends on cash flow, how long you will keep the equipment and your tax position.

When does paying cash for equipment make sense?

When you have surplus cash with no higher-return use, the equipment is small relative to your working capital needs, and loan rates are high. For most established businesses, financing the equipment and keeping cash for operations is the better move.

What is a $1 buyout lease?

An equipment lease that ends with a $1 purchase option, so you own the equipment at the end. It works like a loan and is taxed like one, including Section 179. Compare it with a true lease on an after-tax basis to see which costs less.

Is a TRAC lease the same as a regular lease?

No. A TRAC lease is a vehicle lease with a Terminal Rental Adjustment Clause: you guarantee a residual value at the end, and settle the difference when the truck is sold. It is common on Class-8 trucks. Compare it with a standard FMV lease and with a loan.
