Equipment Sale-Leaseback: How to Turn Owned Equipment Into Working Capital

Sell equipment you own to a lessor, lease it back and keep it running: how much cash it raises, what it costs and how it is taxed

Quick answer

An equipment sale-leaseback means you sell equipment you own to a lessor for cash and lease it straight back, so the machines never leave your yard. Lessors commonly pay about 70-80% of appraised value for late-model heavy equipment and trucks, 55-70% for mixed-age machinery and 40-55% for older or specialized gear, then lease it back over 2-5 years. On $200,000 of late-model equipment, that is roughly $140,000-$160,000 in cash.

What Is an Equipment Sale-Leaseback and How Does It Work?

A sale and leaseback is two deals signed together. You sell equipment you own to a lessor, and the lessor leases it right back to you. The machines stay on the job, the cash lands in your account, and you make a monthly lease payment instead of owning the equipment outright. Construction, trucking and manufacturing companies use it most, because they often have real value sitting in a paid-off fleet.

  1. Appraisal and sale. The lessor appraises the equipment and buys it from you for a share of that value.
  2. Lease back. You sign a fixed-term lease, commonly 2-5 years, and keep using the equipment without interruption.
  3. End of term. You buy it back for a nominal amount or fair market value, depending on the lease type, or return it.

Economically it works like a loan secured by equipment you already own, but it is written as a lease, which changes the tax and accounting treatment.

What Does a Sale-Leaseback Cost? A Worked Example

A contractor owns a paid-off excavator and skid steer appraised at about $200,000 and needs cash for a big project. As late-model heavy equipment, they draw about 70-80% of appraisal, so a lessor buys them for roughly $140,000-$160,000.

Lease termMonthlyTotal paid
36 months$4,911$176,789
48 months$3,877$186,088

That table assumes $150,000 of cash at an 11% lease rate. On 36 months, the contractor pays back about $26,789 more than they received; stretching to 48 months lowers the payment by about $1,034 a month but adds about $9,299 to the total. At the end they own the machines again on a $1 buyout structure, or hand them back. The money costs more than a bank loan against the same equipment would, but it is open to an asset-rich, cash-tight business that could not pass conventional underwriting. When Does a Sale-Leaseback Make Sense?

  • You own valuable, paid-off equipment and need cash for growth, payroll, a slow season or a big job.
  • The alternative is a high-cost short-term loan or merchant cash advance. Leasing back your own equipment is often cheaper than those.
  • You would struggle to qualify for unsecured credit. The equipment secures the deal, so approval leans on the asset.
  • You want to consolidate higher-rate debt into one predictable payment.

What Are the Disadvantages of a Sale-Leaseback?

The main one is cost: you pay more over the lease than you receive, and you give up ownership of equipment you had already paid for. Here is the full trade-off:

ProsCons
Cash now from equipment you already ownYou give up ownership and pay to use your own machines
The equipment secures the deal, so it is easier to qualify than unsecured creditTotal lease payments exceed the cash you receive
Fixed monthly payments; buy-back option at the endThe advance is a share of appraised value, not full value
Payments are generally deductible as an expenseYou may lose depreciation, and the sale can trigger a taxable gain

Always price the alternatives against the same need: a straight equipment loan or lease, working capital, or an equity loan on the machines you keep.

Should You Use a Sale-Leaseback or an Equipment Equity Loan?

Both raise cash from equipment you own and let you keep using it. An equipment equity loan keeps the machines in your name with a lien filed against them; lenders commonly advance 50-80% of forced liquidation value over 24-72 months, and there is no taxable sale. A sale-leaseback can price closer to fair value, but you give up ownership and may owe tax on the sale. If the equity loan raises enough, it is usually the simpler path. Is a Sale-Leaseback an Operating Lease?

It can be either. If the leaseback is a true lease, the lessor owns the equipment and you deduct the lease payments as a business expense. If it is written as a $1 buyout, it is generally treated as a financing (capital) lease, closer to a loan: you keep depreciating the equipment and deduct only the interest portion. Selling equipment you have already depreciated can also trigger a taxable gain. The wrong classification can erase the benefit you were after, so confirm the structure with your accountant before you sign.

What Equipment Qualifies, and Who Does Sale-Leasebacks?

Lessors want titled or serialized, long-lived equipment with a clear resale market that you own free and clear, or with only a small balance left: heavy construction equipment, trucks and trailers, CNC and manufacturing machinery and similar. Used equipment qualifies; age, hours and condition set the advance. Deals usually stall over unclear ownership, a machine titled to someone other than the business, a blanket lien that will not be released, very high hours with no records, or a niche machine with too few buyers.

Sale-leasebacks are done by equipment lessors and asset-based lenders, not most banks. Bring a list of the machines with make, model, year, serial number and hours, photos and service records; an equipment appraisal sets the number. Frequently Asked Questions

What is meant by sale and leaseback?

You sell an asset you own, such as equipment, to a lessor for a lump sum and immediately lease it back, so you keep using it. You get cash out of something you already paid for, and in exchange you make lease payments and give up ownership, usually with an option to buy it back at the end.

How much cash can an equipment sale-leaseback raise?

Lessors commonly advance about 70-80% of appraised value on late-model heavy equipment and trucks, 55-70% on mixed-age machinery and 40-55% on older or specialized equipment. On $200,000 of late-model equipment that is roughly $140,000-$160,000.

What are the disadvantages of a sale-leaseback?

You give up ownership and pay to use equipment you had paid off, and total lease payments exceed the cash you receive. The advance is a share of appraised value, not full value, and the sale can trigger a taxable gain on equipment you have already depreciated.

Is a sale-leaseback an operating lease?

It can be. A true lease is treated as an operating lease and the payments are deducted as an expense. A $1 buyout leaseback is generally treated as a financing lease, closer to a loan. Confirm the classification with your accountant before signing.

Is a sale-leaseback a loan?

Not technically. You sell the asset and lease it back, so it is a sale plus a lease rather than borrowing. In practice it works like asset-based financing: owned equipment turns into cash and you pay to keep using it.

Can you do a sale-leaseback on used equipment?

Yes. Most sale-leasebacks are on used equipment the business already owns. Age, hours and condition set the advance, from roughly 70-80% of appraisal on late-model machines down to 40-55% on older or specialized ones.

When does a sale-leaseback make sense?

When you own valuable, paid-off or lightly financed equipment but need cash for working capital, growth, payroll or a slow season, or to replace higher-cost debt. It is most attractive when the alternative is a high-rate short-term loan or merchant cash advance.
