Can You Get Equipment Financing With No Money Down?

Yes–in many cases, established businesses with strong credit and stable revenue can qualify for no-money-down programs. Zero-down financing is more common for equipment than for real estate or acquisitions because the asset itself serves as collateral and can be repossessed if needed. Typical strengths lenders look for include:

  • 650+ credit score (often stronger terms above 700)
  • 2+ years in business
  • Stable cash flow and bank statement history
  • Equipment with strong resale value

Typical Down Payment Ranges

Down payment requirements are most often structured by credit quality and overall deal profile:

Strong Credit (700+)

  • 0-10% down
  • Longer terms and better pricing

Good Credit (650-699)

  • 0-15% down
  • Competitive approvals with moderate documentation

Fair Credit (600-649)

  • 10-20% down is common
  • Higher rates or shorter terms may apply

Below 600 Credit

  • 20%+ down is often required
  • More restrictive programs and heavier documentation

What Determines Whether a Down Payment Is Required?

Multiple factors influence down payment requirements. Lenders balance risk across credit, collateral, and business stability. A weaker factor in one area can often be offset by strength elsewhere–for example, a moderate credit score combined with strong revenue and high-quality equipment may still qualify for low or no down payment.

Credit Score

Higher scores reduce perceived lender risk and often reduce or eliminate down payment requirements. If you need a deeper process breakdown, see our equipment loan vs lease guide.

Equipment Type

Equipment with strong resale value (construction machinery, commercial trucks, manufacturing equipment, agriculture equipment) is generally easier to finance at lower down payments.

New vs Used Equipment

New equipment generally qualifies for more favorable structures. Used or highly specialized equipment may require additional equity.

Time in Business

Businesses operating for 2+ years usually qualify for more competitive structures. Startups may be asked for more upfront commitment.

Why Lenders Require Down Payments

Down payments serve several purposes for lenders. They reduce loan-to-value (LTV) exposure–if you finance 100% of the equipment and default, the lender may recover less than the outstanding balance after repossession and resale. A down payment creates a buffer. It also signals borrower commitment: putting your own capital at risk demonstrates confidence in the investment. Additionally, equipment depreciates over time; a down payment helps offset that depreciation in the early years. Finally, it protects against default risk by reducing the amount at stake for the lender.

  • Reduce loan-to-value exposure
  • Demonstrate borrower commitment
  • Offset collateral depreciation
  • Protect against payment default risk

Can a Down Payment Improve Approval Odds?

Yes. A strategic down payment can improve your file by lowering risk and improving payment-to-income metrics. Even modest contributions can help reduce overall financing cost.

Down Payment vs Monthly Payment Trade-Off

Higher down payments usually reduce monthly payments and total interest cost. Lower down payments preserve liquidity but can increase monthly obligations. The right balance depends on your working capital strategy and growth plan. For example, a $100,000 equipment loan at 10% APR over 5 years with zero down yields a higher monthly payment than the same loan with 20% down. Running the numbers helps you decide whether to deploy cash upfront or retain it for operations.

Documentation That May Support Lower Down Payments

Lenders may reduce or waive down payment requirements when you provide strong documentation: recent bank statements showing consistent cash flow, profit-and-loss statements, tax returns, and a clear equipment invoice or purchase agreement. Demonstrating financial stability and repayment capacity can sometimes offset the need for a larger equity contribution.

Equipment Loan vs Lease and Down Payments

Equipment lease structures often offer more flexibility on upfront cash, while loan structures may require more equity but build long-term ownership value.

Is a Down Payment Always Required?

No. Many businesses qualify for zero-down equipment financing, especially with strong credit, stable revenue, and high-quality collateral. Borrowers with weaker profiles or highly specialized equipment should expect some equity contribution. The key is understanding your profile and choosing equipment and structures that align with it. If zero down is important, focus on building credit, demonstrating revenue stability, and selecting equipment with strong resale value.

Lease Structures and Down Payment Alternatives

Lease programs often require less or no down payment compared to traditional equipment loans. With a fair market value (FMV) lease, you pay to use the equipment over the term and have options at the end–purchase, return, or renew. The lessor retains ownership during the term, which can reduce upfront requirements. If minimizing down payment is a priority, compare both loan and lease structures. See equipment loan vs lease for a detailed comparison.

Comparing Offers: Down Payment and Total Cost

When evaluating multiple equipment financing offers, compare the full picture: down payment, interest rate, term length, monthly payment, and total cost over the life of the financing. A lower down payment with a higher rate might result in a higher total cost than putting more down upfront. Use the monthly payment and total interest as your primary comparison metrics. Some businesses prioritize preserving cash and accept higher total cost; others prefer to minimize interest by putting more down. There is no single right answer–it depends on your capital position and priorities.

Scenario Planning and Post-Funding Governance

Why Down Payments Map to Risk and Advance Rates

A down payment is equity in the deal. It absorbs first-dollar market softening in collateral value and demonstrates borrower commitment. When credit, industry, or collateral are weaker, equity often moves before rate—because it directly changes loss severity if something goes wrong.

If you cannot fund a down payment, alternatives include trading in older equipment, accepting a lower advance, or choosing a shorter term with higher payment but smaller total obligation. Each lever changes underwriting math differently.

Zero- or Low-Down Programs: What Changes in Underwriting

Promotional zero-down offers usually compensate with stricter credit thresholds, vendor participation, or shorter amortization. Read total cost of ownership, not only monthly payment. If cash preservation is the goal, compare a low-down structure with a reserve account requirement versus true zero-down with a higher rate.

How to Discuss Down Payment Without Slowing the Deal

Bring a clear use of proceeds and liquidity snapshot. If you can move equity from another asset sale or AR collection, say so with dates. Lenders respond well to specific timing rather than vague promises. For seasonal businesses, pairing a down with a seasonal payment schedule may be possible in select programs—ask explicitly.

Down Payments in Loans Versus Leases

Leases may advertise low upfront cash but build reserves into the rent factor or end-of-term options. Loans show equity explicitly. Compare total cash outflows over the expected hold period, including insurance and maintenance responsibilities, not only the signing number.

Cash Reserves After Closing

Equipment financing approvals sometimes assume you will retain liquidity for insurance deductibles, repairs, and slow months. If a down payment drains all cash, you may be a stronger applicant with a slightly smaller request and a healthier reserve. Underwriters often prefer sustainable balance sheets over razor-thin liquidity.

Summary Action List

Confirm advance rate, compare equity alternatives, and document where funds originate. If you need a precise document list, use documents needed and consider getting matched so you compare structures without fragmenting your submission.

Questions Underwriters Ask About Your Equity

Expect questions about the source of funds for any down payment, especially if deposits recently spiked. Document gifts, asset sales, or equity injections clearly. If you are rolling equity from a trade-in, provide the purchase agreement and payoff letter.

If you are not putting money down, expect scrutiny on liquidity and collateral coverage instead. Neither path is automatically wrong; both need proof.

Summary

Down payments are one of several tools that align risk between you and the lender. Whether you bring equity in cash, trade, or a smaller loan amount, the objective is sustainable payments and credible liquidity after closing. Compare structures holistically and keep documentation transparent about fund sources.

How to Compare Programs Fairly

Normalize offers to the same term length and payment frequency before comparing rates. Include insurance requirements, fees, and prepayment terms. A lower rate with a large fee or restrictive covenant can be worse than a slightly higher rate with flexibility. If you are unsure, walk through scenarios with a single intake through matching so you evaluate apples-to-apples.

Insurance and Reserves in the Same Conversation as Down Payment

Even when a lender approves minimal upfront cash, you still need liquidity for deductibles, repairs, and slow weeks. Underwriters often view uninsured or underinsured collateral as a hidden leverage problem. Budget insurance premiums and maintenance reserves alongside any equity contribution so your post-close position is credible.

Making the Decision

Whether you contribute cash equity up front or accept different pricing and covenants instead, choose the path that preserves liquidity for operations and insurance. Equipment financing should support production, not create a cash cliff the month after delivery.

Working capital loans cover soft costs equipment financing typically won't.
