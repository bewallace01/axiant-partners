How Heavy Equipment Payments Work

Every heavy equipment payment is built from four inputs: the price of the machine, your down payment, the interest rate, and the term in months. Subtract the down payment from the price to find the amount financed, then amortize that balance across the term at your rate. Stretching the term lowers the monthly payment but raises the total interest you pay; a larger down payment does the opposite, cutting both the payment and the lifetime cost. A calculator does this instantly so you do not have to run amortization by hand. Because heavy machinery is a working asset, lenders frequently fold soft costs—freight and assembly, attachments and buckets, extended warranties, and sales tax—into the financed amount, so the balance is often a little higher than the sticker price. Heavy Equipment Price Ranges by Class

Price drives everything else in the calculation, and heavy equipment spans a wide range. Use these realistic ballparks as a starting point before you plug numbers into the calculator:

  • Skid steers and compact track loaders: roughly $40,000 to $90,000. The most-financed entry point for site crews and landscapers.
  • Backhoes and small wheel loaders: roughly $90,000 to $200,000.
  • Dozers, motor graders, and mid-size wheel loaders: roughly $150,000 to $600,000 depending on size and configuration.
  • Excavators: mini units run $40,000 to $120,000, standard machines $120,000 to $350,000, and large production excavators $350,000 and up. See the dedicated excavator financing calculator for that class.
  • Cranes, articulated dump trucks, and large production machines: typically $500,000 and up, often well into seven figures.

Used machines sell for a fraction of new but finance differently. For the full lineup and brand-specific programs, visit the heavy equipment financing hub and our construction heavy equipment guide.

New vs Used Heavy Equipment Financing

New machines tend to qualify for the most attractive terms: lower rates, longer terms up to 84 months, and frequently 0 to 10 percent down. Manufacturers and dealers sometimes offer subsidized or promotional rates on new units to move inventory, which can beat a standard bank loan. Used heavy equipment is very financeable too, but lenders price in the added risk of age and accumulated engine hours. Expect a slightly higher rate, a term often capped at 36 to 60 months, and sometimes a larger down payment. A high-hour machine or one bought at auction may need a third-party inspection or appraisal to set value. The trade-off is real: a used unit costs far less up front and depreciates more slowly, so even at a higher rate the total cost can come out lower. What Affects Your Rate

Two contractors looking at the same dozer can be quoted very different rates. The biggest drivers are:

  • Credit profile: personal and business credit are the single largest factor. Scores above 700 earn the best pricing; 600 to 700 is solidly financeable; 550 to 620 typically routes to specialty lenders at higher rates.
  • Machine age and hours: newer, lower-hour equipment holds value better and earns lower rates and longer terms than older, high-hour units.
  • Time in business: established companies with two-plus years of operating history qualify more easily and price better than startups, especially on large-ticket machines.
  • Down payment: putting 10 to 20 percent down reduces the lender's exposure and can shave the rate, while 0 percent down is reserved for the strongest files.
  • Deal size: very large deals may move from a simple application-only approval to a full financial-package review, which can affect rate and timeline.

Lease vs Loan for Heavy Equipment

A loan builds ownership: you finance the machine, make payments, and own it free and clear at the end. A lease is closer to a long-term rental, often with a lower monthly payment and a buyout option (a $1 buyout or a fair-market-value option) at the end of the term. Loans usually make sense when you plan to run the machine for many years and want to build equity in the asset. Leases can be attractive when you want the lowest possible monthly payment, expect to upgrade equipment on a cycle, or want to preserve working capital for payroll and materials. There are tax and accounting differences between the two as well, so it is worth a conversation with your accountant. The calculator estimates loan payments and can model a lease when you switch modes and add a residual; a matched lender can quote a parallel lease structure so you can compare side by side.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I calculate heavy equipment loan payments?

Take the machine price, subtract any down payment to get the amount financed, then amortize that balance across your term at your interest rate. The result is your level monthly payment. The fastest way is to use a heavy equipment financing calculator: enter the price, down payment, rate, and number of months, and it returns the estimated payment instantly. Soft costs like freight, attachments, and tax are often rolled into the financed amount, so the balance can be a little higher than the sticker price.

How long can you finance heavy equipment?

Heavy and construction equipment financing terms typically run 36 to 84 months. Newer machines with strong credit can reach 72 to 84 months, while older or high-hour units are often capped at 36 to 60 months so the loan is paid off before the machine depreciates too far. Lenders match the term to the equipment's useful life.

Can you finance used heavy equipment?

Yes. Used dozers, loaders, excavators, and cranes are financed routinely, including dealer, auction, and private-party purchases. Lenders weigh the machine's age and engine hours, so expect a slightly higher rate, a shorter term, and sometimes a larger down payment than a comparable new unit. A current valuation or inspection report strengthens the deal.

What credit score do I need to finance heavy equipment?

Many equipment lenders work with credit scores from about 600 up, and the best pricing generally goes to borrowers above 700. Scores in the 550 to 620 range can still be approved through specialty lenders, usually with a higher rate, a larger down payment, or a shorter term. Time in business and revenue also weigh heavily on large-ticket machinery.

How much down payment do you need for heavy equipment?

Down payments on heavy equipment commonly range from 0 to 20 percent. Established borrowers with strong credit buying newer machines can qualify for 0 to 10 percent down, while used machines, newer businesses, or lower credit scores may require 10 to 20 percent down to offset the lender's risk on a large-ticket asset.
