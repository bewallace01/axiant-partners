What Happens If You Default on an Equipment Loan

The sequence from a missed payment to repossession and sale, and the points where it can still be stopped

← Back to Heavy Equipment Equity Loans Articles

Updated October 22, 2026

Quick answer

If an equipment-secured loan defaults, the lender can repossess the machine without a court order as long as it avoids a breach of the peace, then sell it in a commercially reasonable way after giving notice. If the sale brings less than you owe, you can still owe the deficiency, often under a personal guarantee. The cheapest point to fix it is before the first missed payment.

The Sequence, Step by Step

  1. Missed payment. Most agreements allow a short grace period and a late fee before the loan is in default.
  2. Default and acceleration. The agreement usually lets the lender declare the whole balance due once you are in default.
  3. Repossession. Under the UCC a secured lender can take the collateral without going to court, provided it does so without a breach of the peace.
  4. Notice of sale. Before selling, the lender must send notice of the planned disposition to the borrower and guarantors.
  5. Sale. Every aspect of the sale - method, timing, place and terms - must be commercially reasonable.
  6. Deficiency or surplus. Proceeds go to the costs of repossession and sale, then the debt. A shortfall can be pursued; a surplus goes back to you.

Heavy Equipment Is Easier to Repossess Than You Think

A machine parked on an open jobsite is reachable in a way a vehicle in a locked garage is not. Many equipment lenders also require GPS or telematics, so they know where the machine is. Owners sometimes assume a machine on a remote site is safe; it rarely is.

What a lender cannot do is breach the peace - for example, by force, by threats, or over your objection at the time. If that happens, speak to a lawyer.

Why the Sale Price Matters to You

The sale of repossessed equipment is usually fast - often at auction - which is exactly why lenders lend against forced liquidation value. If that sale brings less than the balance plus costs, the difference is a deficiency, and most equipment loans to small businesses carry a personal guarantee that makes the owner liable for it.

Illustrative figure
Balance owed at default$80,000
Repossession and sale costs$6,000
Auction proceeds$62,000
Deficiency still owed$24,000

A sale that was not commercially reasonable can reduce or eliminate a deficiency claim, which is one reason to keep every notice you receive.

Leases Default Differently From Loans

If the machine is leased rather than financed with a loan, the lessor already owns it. In a default the lessor typically takes the equipment back and claims the remaining lease payments under the agreement, rather than selling the machine to pay down a loan. Whether a leftover balance is owed, and how it is calculated, depends on the lease's default and early-termination terms.

Some documents called leases - especially those with a nominal buyout, such as $1 - are treated as secured loans in substance, and the loan rules above then apply. Read which kind of agreement you signed before deciding how to handle a missed payment, because the exposure can be very different.

Cross-Default and Blanket Liens

A default on one loan can trigger defaults on others. Many agreements contain cross-default clauses, and a lender with a blanket lien may have a claim on more than the machine you fell behind on. Read the default section of every agreement you have signed before you decide which payment to miss.

Where You Can Still Stop It

  • Before the first missed payment: call the lender. Deferrals, interest-only periods and re-amortizations are far easier to get before a default is on the record.
  • After default, before repossession: a refinance against other equity, or selling the machine yourself, usually recovers more than a forced sale.
  • After repossession, before the sale: you have the right to redeem the collateral by paying what is owed, including costs.

If the problem is wider than one loan, see business debt relief options.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can an equipment lender repossess without a court order?

Yes. Under the Uniform Commercial Code a secured lender may take the collateral after default without going to court, provided it does so without a breach of the peace.

Do I still owe money after the equipment is sold?

You may. If the sale brings less than the balance plus the costs of repossession and sale, the lender can pursue the deficiency, often against the owner under a personal guarantee.

Does the lender have to tell me before it sells the machine?

Generally yes. A secured lender must send notice of the planned disposition, and the sale itself must be commercially reasonable.

Can I get the equipment back after it is repossessed?

Before it is sold, you generally have the right to redeem it by paying the full amount owed, including the lender's costs.

Sources & Further Reading

  • UCC § 9-609: Secured Party's Right to Take Possession After Default — The rule allowing repossession without a court order, provided there is no breach of the peace.
  • UCC § 9-610: Disposition of Collateral After Default — The requirement that every aspect of a sale of repossessed collateral be commercially reasonable.
  • UCC § 9-615: Application of Proceeds; Surplus and Deficiency — How sale proceeds are applied and when the borrower owes a deficiency or is owed a surplus.
  • UCC § 9-623: Right to Redeem Collateral — The borrower's right to recover the collateral by paying what is owed before it is sold.
