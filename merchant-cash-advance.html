What Is a Merchant Cash Advance?

A merchant cash advance (MCA) is upfront capital repaid as a percentage of your daily credit card sales or through daily or weekly bank debits. You receive a lump sum and repay via a “holdback”—a fixed percentage of each day’s card sales or a fixed daily/weekly withdrawal from your business bank account. When sales are strong, you pay more and finish faster. When they dip, payments scale down. That flexibility suits businesses with variable daily traffic: restaurants, retail, salons, and others that accept card payments.

Unlike traditional term loans, MCA uses a factor rate (not APR) and repayment ties directly to revenue. Designed for businesses with consistent card volume, it offers one of the fastest paths to capital when you need it quickly. Many business owners confuse merchant cash advance with revenue-based financing or working capital loans. While all provide capital for operations, MCA is distinct: it focuses on daily card sales or bank deposits, typically funds faster than most alternatives, and uses a factor rate rather than an annual percentage rate. How Merchant Cash Advance Repayment Works

MCA uses a factor rate (e.g., 1.15 to 1.40) and a holdback percentage. You receive an advance and agree to repay the advance plus the factor by either:

  • Percentage of card sales—A fixed percentage (e.g., 10-20%) of daily credit/debit card sales is withheld until the obligation is satisfied. Repayment naturally flexes with sales volume.
  • Daily or weekly ACH—A fixed amount is debited from your business bank account daily or weekly until the obligation is satisfied. Provides predictable debits regardless of daily sales.

Repayment flexes with sales when tied to card volume. Slower days mean lower payments. Understand total cost (factor rate, holdback, and timeframe) and cash flow impact before committing. Merchant Cash Advance vs Other Options

Understanding the differences helps you choose the right structure. RBF vs MCA—full comparison.

MCA vs Revenue-Based Financing

MCA: Repayment typically daily or weekly from card sales or bank debits. Often funds in 1-3 days. RBF: Repayment monthly as a percentage of revenue. May offer clearer terms. Both tie repayment to sales; RBF is often preferred for larger amounts and growth companies with stable monthly revenue. Compare RBF vs MCA.

MCA vs Term Loan

MCA: Factor rate, flexible repayment tied to sales. Fast funding. Higher effective cost. Term loan: Fixed monthly payments, APR. Lower total cost for established businesses. MCA for urgent short-term needs; term loans for defined projects with predictable cash flow. Compare term loans.

MCA vs Line of Credit

MCA: Lump sum, fixed repayment schedule. Line of credit: Revolving access, draw and repay as needed. MCA for one-time needs; LOC for ongoing liquidity. Consider business line of credit if you need recurring access.

Risks & Considerations

Merchant cash advances have tradeoffs. Understand them before you apply:

Effective cost

Can exceed traditional bank debt. Factor rates and frequent payments add up. You’re paying for speed and flexibility. Compare total cost to alternatives.

Daily debits

Holdback or ACH withdrawals occur daily or weekly. Plan for cash flow impact. Slow sales periods can strain operations if debits are fixed.

Short-term tool

Best for urgent, short-duration needs. Plan to refinance with lower-cost financing (term loan, line of credit) when possible. See our guide on refinancing MCA debt.

Stacking risk

Taking a second or third advance to cover the first compounds the daily debits fast. If you’re already stacked, see business debt relief for ways to consolidate, restructure, or settle the advances into one lower payment.

Credit Score Requirements for an MCA

A merchant cash advance is one of the most credit-flexible forms of business funding. Because repayment comes from your future card and bank receipts, providers weigh your revenue and deposit consistency far more than your credit score. Many approve businesses with credit in the 500s, and some have no hard minimum at all. That accessibility is the whole point — an MCA funds businesses a bank would decline. The trade-off is cost: the weaker your profile, the higher the factor rate, so treat an MCA as a speed-and-access tool, not a low-cost one.

How Fast Can You Get an MCA?

Speed is an MCA's biggest advantage. With a simple application and a few months of bank statements, many providers approve within hours and fund in as little as 24 to 72 hours — no lengthy underwriting, no collateral appraisal, and often no tax returns for smaller amounts. That makes an MCA useful for a genuine time-sensitive need: covering payroll, taking a bulk-inventory discount, or handling an emergency. Just remember that fast money is expensive money — confirm the total payback and the daily or weekly remittance before you accept.

Common MCA Mistakes to Avoid

The same handful of mistakes turn a useful bridge into a cash-flow trap. Stacking — taking a second or third advance on top of the first — is the most dangerous, because the combined daily payments can outrun your revenue. Others: accepting a daily remittance when your cash flow is uneven, not converting the factor rate to an APR so you understand the true cost, and using an MCA for a structural shortfall rather than a short timing gap. Used once for a clear, short need and paid off, an MCA does its job; used repeatedly to plug an ongoing hole, it accelerates the problem.

Why Your Daily Payment May Be Higher Than Expected

Borrowers are often surprised by how much an MCA takes each day. The payment is not an interest rate on a declining balance — it is a fixed total payback (principal times the factor rate) collected as a percentage of daily receipts or a fixed daily draft over a short term. Squeeze a large total payback into a few months and the daily amount is large by design, and a slow sales week does not lower a fixed daily draft — which is what strains cash flow. Before signing, divide the total payback by the expected number of business days in the term to see the real daily hit, and make sure a bad week can still absorb it.
