Commercial Fishing Boat Loans: Vessel, Permit and Who Lends

Who finances a commercial fishing boat, what the federal program covers, and why the permit can decide the deal

← Back to Commercial Marine Financing Articles

Updated November 12, 2026

Quick answer

As of November 2026, commercial fishing boat loans come from marine lenders, banks with fishing customers, SBA 7(a) loans of up to $5 million, and NOAA’s Fisheries Finance Program, which can lend up to 80% of the cost of an existing vessel at a fixed rate 2% above the Treasury’s borrowing cost. Lenders typically want a survey, a multi-season landings history and proof the permit transfers. Axiant Partners, a business-loan broker in Boca Raton, matches fishing operators with marine lenders.

Who Lends on Commercial Fishing Boats

Fishing boat finance is a small market, and most general equipment lenders stay out of it. The realistic sources, and what each tends to fit:

SourceBest fitWatch for
Marine lendersUsed boats, quick purchases, refinancingSurvey and lien rules; terms set by the boat’s age
Fishing-focused banksEstablished operators in ports they knowUsually want a long relationship and landings history
SBA 7(a) through a bankBoat plus working capital, up to $5 millionSlower; the bank decides if it lends on vessels
NOAA finance programBuying or refurbishing an existing boat, refinancingNo capacity-increasing refits; slower than a private loan
Seller financingCovering a permit a lender won’t takeNeeds the same transfer checks as any sale

The source matters less than the file. Every one of them will ask the same three questions: what the boat is worth, what the permit is worth and whether it transfers, and what the boat has landed over several seasons. For the wider marine picture, including workboats and charter boats, see commercial marine financing.

NOAA's Fisheries Finance Program in Plain Terms

The federal program is the one source built only for this trade. Its rules are public, so they can be stated plainly:

  • What it finances: buying, refurbishing or modernizing existing fishing vessels and shore facilities, certain federal catch-share privileges and quota, and refinancing debt taken on for those purposes.
  • How much: no more than 80% of actual cost, and the program can approve less (50 CFR 253.11).
  • Rate: fixed, 2% above the U.S. Treasury’s cost of borrowing for a similar maturity at closing.
  • Fee: a one-time filing and commitment fee of 0.5% of the proposed loan.
  • What it won’t do: fund a refit that materially increases the boat’s harvesting capacity, or lend to an applicant who can’t document fishing industry experience.

Source: NOAA Fisheries Finance Program and 50 CFR 253.11. Because it is slower than a private loan, buyers on a deadline often close with a marine lender and look at the program for refinancing later. Ask before you sign whether the private loan allows that.

Worked Example: A $450,000 Boat-and-Permit Purchase

Illustrative numbers, not a quote. A buyer agrees to pay $300,000 for a used vessel and $150,000 for the permit that goes with it. How much of that a lender will finance depends on which half it can secure:

CaseFinanceable costLoan at 80%Buyer brings
Permit can be financed$450,000$360,000$90,000
Vessel only$300,000$240,000$210,000, or a seller note for the permit

“Permit can be financed” means the permit is a federal catch-share privilege the program can lend against. In that case the program’s 0.5% filing fee on $360,000 is $1,800. The gap in the second case is why the permit question comes first: the same boat needs $120,000 more from the buyer when the permit can’t be pledged. The 80% figure is the program’s ceiling. Private marine lenders set their own advance, so ask each one what share of the boat and the permit it will count.

What the Federal Program's Rate Looks Like Today

No private marine lender publishes a fishing boat rate, and we don’t either. The federal program is the exception, because its rate is a formula: Treasury’s cost of borrowing for a similar maturity, plus 2%. Treasury sets that cost at closing, so the market yields below are a guide, not the program’s number. On November 10, 2026 the Treasury yield curve showed:

MaturityTreasury yieldPlus 2%Payment on $360,000
10 years4.98%About 6.98%About $4,176 a month
20 years5.39%About 7.39%About $2,876 a month

Illustrative, not a quote. The $360,000 is the loan from the worked example below, paid monthly over the full term. The program sets the maturity for each project, so a 20-year term may not be offered for your boat. Over 10 years the interest adds about $141,000; over 20 years about $330,000, which is the price of the lower payment. Private loans are priced case by case and usually close faster.

First-Time Buyers: From Deckhand to Owner

Most first boats are bought by people who have fished for years on someone else’s deck. That experience is the qualification lenders ask about first, because the program requires documented industry experience and private lenders want the same comfort.

  • Show your seasons: settlement sheets, crew shares or a letter from the captain you fished for stand in for an operating history.
  • Expect to bring cash: the program lends at most 80% of cost, and SBA 7(a) loans for a start-up or a change of ownership require an equity injection of at least 10% under SOP 50 10 8.1.
  • Ask the seller: a seller note on the permit is common when a lender will finance only the hull.
  • Price the first season: fuel, ice, bait, insurance and crew before the first landing, which is a working capital need, not part of the boat loan.

If the boat comes with a business, such as a dock lease or a buyer relationship, see business acquisition financing.

The Permit Is Part of the Business, and Sometimes Most of It

This is the feature that makes fishing vessel finance unlike any other marine lending.

In many managed fisheries, the right to fish is a limited, transferable asset — a permit, a license, or an allocation of quota. It is bought and sold in its own right, and in some fisheries it is worth considerably more than the vessel that uses it.

The consequence for financing is direct. A buyer purchasing "a fishing boat" is often purchasing two distinct assets, and a lender securing only the hull is securing the smaller half. Whether and how the permit can be pledged, and whether it transfers cleanly to a buyer, is central rather than incidental.

The most common way these purchases fall apart is a buyer assuming the permit comes with the boat. Some permits transfer freely, some require the buyer to meet eligibility rules, some are restricted by region, length or history, and some don't transfer at all. Get the answer in writing from the managing authority, not the seller, before you make an offer.

Federal fisheries regulation, including permit and quota systems, is set out by NOAA Fisheries. The rules differ by fishery and by region, which is exactly why this cannot be generalised.

What the Two Halves Look Like

The vesselThe permit or quota
What it isA physical, surveyable assetA regulatory right, often transferable
Valued byMarine surveyorMarket for that fishery
Secured byPreferred ship mortgage or state lienDepends entirely on the fishery
TransfersWith the saleOnly under the fishery’s rules
RiskCondition, marketRegulatory change

That last row is the one to sit with. Permit value depends on management decisions that can change, and a lender lending against it is taking regulatory risk as well as credit risk.

Ownership matters too. A documented fishing vessel needs a fishery endorsement, and 46 U.S.C. 12113 sets U.S. citizenship rules for the owner. A financing or lease structure that puts title with an ineligible owner can cost the boat its endorsement, which is why marine lenders check it.

Seasonality and Landings History

Fishing revenue is seasonal, weather-dependent and quota-constrained, which makes a single quarter almost meaningless as evidence.

What lenders want instead is a multi-season landings history — what was caught, when, and what it realised. That shows the operation's actual capacity rather than one good or bad run, and it is the closest thing this trade has to contracted revenue.

Alongside it, expect questions on how the off-season is funded and whether the boat works a second fishery. If you want a seasonal payment schedule, ask for it at term-sheet stage.

Preparing a Fishing Boat Loan Application

  1. Confirm permit transferability in writing from the managing authority, before an offer.
  2. Value the two halves separately — survey for the vessel, market evidence for the permit.
  3. Bring multi-season landings data, not one year.
  4. Document the permit or quota holding and any conditions attached.
  5. Run a lien search on the vessel — and ask whether anything is secured against the permit.
  6. Get commercial cover quoted for the actual fishery and waters.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the interest rate on a commercial fishing boat loan?

Private marine lenders price each boat and file, and none publish a fishing rate. NOAA's Fisheries Finance Program is fixed at 2% above Treasury's cost of borrowing for a similar maturity. With Treasury yields of 4.98% (10-year) and 5.39% (20-year) on November 10, 2026, that is roughly 7.0-7.4%, set at closing. This is an illustration, not a quote.

Can a first-time buyer get a commercial fishing boat loan?

Yes, if you can document fishing experience, such as settlement sheets or crew shares, and bring cash. The federal program lends at most 80% of cost, and SBA 7(a) loans for a start-up or change of ownership need at least 10% equity. A seller note on the permit often fills the gap.

Who gives loans for commercial fishing boats?

Marine lenders, banks with fishing customers, SBA 7(a) lenders, NOAA's Fisheries Finance Program and sometimes the seller. Most general equipment lenders don't lend on fishing vessels.

How much will NOAA's Fisheries Finance Program lend?

Up to 80% of actual cost under 50 CFR 253.11, and it can approve less. The rate is fixed at 2% above the Treasury's cost of borrowing, and there is a 0.5% filing and commitment fee.

Can the Fisheries Finance Program pay for a new boat?

Its program page describes financing for buying, refurbishing or modernizing existing vessels and refinancing related debt, and it won't fund a refit that materially increases harvesting capacity. Ask NOAA's Financial Services staff about your project before planning around it.

Can a fishing permit be worth more than the boat?

In many managed fisheries, yes. Permits and quota allocations are limited, transferable assets traded in their own right, and in some fisheries they carry considerably more value than the vessel using them.

Does the permit automatically transfer with the vessel?

No, and assuming it does is the most common way these purchases collapse. Transfer rules vary by fishery — some permits transfer freely, some require buyer eligibility, some are restricted, and some do not transfer at all.

What revenue evidence do lenders want?

A multi-season landings history rather than a single year — what was caught, when, and what it realised. Fishing revenue is seasonal, weather-dependent and quota-constrained, so one period tells a lender very little.
