Farm Operating Loans: Financing Seed, Fertilizer and Fuel Each Season

You buy the whole crop’s inputs in spring and don’t get paid until fall. Here’s how operating loans bridge it.

Quick answer

A farm operating loan pays for a season's seed, fertilizer, chemicals, fuel and labor and is repaid when the crop or livestock sells, usually as a revolving line so you pay interest only on what you draw. Secured bank and credit-union lines run about 8-13% APR, FSA makes direct operating loans up to $400,000, and an SBA 7(a) loan at prime plus 2.75% was about 9.75% in November 2026. What is a farm operating loan?

It's the money that carries a crop from planting to sale. In spring you commit most of the year's production cost at once: seed in the ground, fertilizer and chemicals applied, fuel in the equipment, custom work and labor paid. Then comes a growing season with more costs and no revenue until the crop is harvested and sold. Livestock runs on a different calendar, but feed and care are paid all along while income arrives in bursts at sale.

An operating loan bridges that gap. Most are set up as a revolving operating line of credit: you size it to the season's budget, draw as each bill arrives, pay interest only on the balance, and pay it down after harvest. The line then resets for next spring, so you never carry more debt than the season needs at that moment. A working capital loan works differently. It's a lump sum on a fixed schedule, better for one defined need than for a full season of open-ended costs. Needing operating credit isn't a sign of trouble; it's how a profitable farm pays for six-plus months of inputs before it sells a bushel.

How do FSA farm operating loans work?

The USDA's Farm Service Agency lends in two ways: directly, up to $400,000 for operating needs, or by guaranteeing a loan your bank makes, with guaranteed limits indexed each year. FSA operating loans are aimed at production agriculture and beginning farmers. Besides seasonal inputs, they can buy equipment and livestock, and operating term loans run for the useful life of what they buy or up to seven years. FSA doesn't publish an approval rate we can point to, so the practical test is fit: if you're a working farm, especially a newer one, ask about FSA alongside a commercial line. How FSA compares with SBA 7(a) is covered in SBA loans for agriculture.

How much operating credit does a farm need?

Start from the input budget. A farm planting 800 acres at an all-in input cost of about $400 an acre (seed, fertilizer, chemicals, fuel and custom work) commits roughly $320,000, most of it between planting and mid-season, with nothing coming in until fall. Few operations want to pull that from reserves and sit exposed through the season, especially with land rent and equipment payments due too.

With a line sized to that budget, the farm takes a large draw for seed and fertilizer at planting and smaller draws for fuel and chemicals later. Even if the whole $320,000 were out for six months, interest at 8-13% would be about $12,800-$20,800; drawing gradually costs less. When the crop sells, say for $520,000, the farm repays the balance plus interest, keeps the margin, and the line is clear for spring. These figures are illustrative, not a quote.

Using the line to buy inputs at the right price

Fertilizer, fuel and chemical prices have moved sharply in recent years, and a jump in any of them raises the per-acre cost before you know what the crop will bring. A well-sized line lets you buy when pricing is good, not when cash happens to show up.

That includes co-op and dealer programs. Prepay and early-order discounts can save real money per acre, but they need cash even earlier, months before harvest. The test is simple: if the discount saves more per ton than the interest to carry the draw until harvest, financing the prepay is a net gain. Keep co-op balances and line draws in one view, too. A big dealer balance stacked on a fully drawn line with no repayment plan is how input costs quietly outrun the season's revenue.

What do lenders look for in a farm operating loan?

Lenders read the whole operation, not one number:

  • A crop or livestock plan with a realistic budget and cash-flow projection for the year.
  • Production history and the borrower's overall financial strength: balance sheet, equity and existing debt.
  • Risk protection: crop insurance against a yield disaster and forward contracts or a marketing plan for the revenue side. Both make a lender more comfortable with a bigger limit at a better rate.
  • Clean books that keep farm and household finances apart.
  • Credit: some commercial line lenders work with scores from 550 at higher rates, and most prefer two or more years of history.

For the stretches between sales, when the question is holding grain for a better price rather than funding inputs, see the farm line of credit guide. Farm operating loan FAQs

What is a farm operating loan?

A loan or revolving line that pays for a season's seed, fertilizer, chemicals, fuel, labor and livestock costs, repaid when the crop or animals sell. Most are set up as a line, so you draw as bills arrive and pay interest only on what's out.

What is the interest rate on a farm operating loan?

Secured bank and credit-union operating lines generally run about 8-13% APR, unsecured bank lines about 10-18%, and online lenders 15% to 60% or more. An SBA 7(a) loan priced at prime plus 2.75% was about 9.75% in November 2026. Your rate depends on credit, collateral and the operation's track record.

How much can FSA lend for farm operating costs?

FSA's direct operating loans go up to $400,000 and are aimed at production agriculture and beginning farmers. FSA also guarantees bank loans, with limits indexed each year. Operating term loans run for the useful life of what they buy or up to 7 years.

How do farmers pay for seed and fertilizer before harvest?

Most draw on an operating line at planting for seed, fertilizer, chemicals and fuel, then repay after harvest when crops or livestock are sold. Supplier and co-op credit programs and working capital loans cover part of the bill for many farms.

Can newer or smaller farms get an operating loan?

Often, yes. FSA's direct operating loans are built partly for beginning farmers, and commercial lenders look at the crop or livestock plan and the borrower's overall strength, not only years in business. Crop insurance, forward contracts and clean records all help the limit and the rate.

Should I finance a fall fertilizer prepay?

It can pay. If the prepay or early-order discount saves more per ton than the interest to carry the draw until harvest, financing it is a net gain. Run that comparison before you draw, and keep co-op balances and line draws inside what the crop can repay.
