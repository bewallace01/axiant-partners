SBA Loans for Agriculture: Can a Farm or Ranch Use One?

Which farms qualify, the size standards by crop and livestock type, how SBA compares with FSA and USDA B&I, and what the payments look like

← Back to Agriculture Business Financing

Updated November 14, 2026

Quick answer

As of November 2026, farms and ranches can use SBA loans: agriculture is not on SBA’s ineligible list (13 CFR 120.110). A 7(a) loan goes up to $5 million, with terms to 10 years for equipment and working capital and up to 25 years for land and buildings. Most farm types must stay under a receipts size standard of about $2.25-$5.5 million a year, or pass SBA’s net worth test. Can a Farm Get an SBA Loan?

Yes. SBA’s list of ineligible businesses names nonprofits, lenders, passive landlords, life insurers, speculative ventures and a handful of others; crop farms, ranches, nurseries, dairies and aquaculture are not on it (13 CFR 120.110). In practice the questions a lender asks a farm are the ones it asks any borrower: is the business small under SBA’s rules, does the cash flow cover the payment, and is the borrower operating the farm rather than only owning land that someone else farms.

A landowner who only leases ground to a tenant looks like a passive real estate holder, which SBA excludes outside its eligible passive company rules. An owner-operator buying the land it farms is the normal case.

Many agricultural lenders steer farm production loans to the Farm Service Agency first, and some banks that make SBA loans do not make farm loans at all. Ask a lender whether it does both before you send a file.

A farm over its receipts limit can still qualify for a 7(a) or 504 loan under the alternative size standard: tangible net worth of no more than $20 million and average net income after federal income tax of no more than $6.5 million over the two prior fiscal years, affiliates included (13 CFR 121.301(b)). For many family operations with high gross sales and thin margins, that test is the easier one to pass.

What SBA Money Can Do on a Farm

  • Buy or expand land and buildings the operation uses: barns, packing sheds, greenhouses, a nursery’s propagation houses. Real estate can run up to 25 years (13 CFR 120.212).
  • Equipment packages that are too mixed for one dealer program, such as irrigation, a packing line and used tractors on one loan. Single machines usually finance faster as equipment loans; see tractor financing.
  • Working capital for inputs and payroll, though a seasonal line is often a better fit than a 10-year term loan for a crop cycle.
  • Buying an existing agricultural business, such as a nursery, a feed store or a custom harvesting outfit, with goodwill in the price.

A 7(a) loan is capped at $5,000,000, and SBA’s guaranteed share across all of a borrower’s loans at $3,750,000 (13 CFR 120.151). Long-life buildings with a smaller equipment share sometimes fit the 504 program better; start from SBA loans.

How to Apply as a Farm

  1. Find your NAICS code and check its size standard, or run the net worth and net income test.
  2. Gather three years of farm tax returns (Schedule F or entity returns), a balance sheet, and production or sales records.
  3. Price the project: land appraisal, building bids, equipment quotes.
  4. Ask two kinds of lender: one that makes SBA loans to agricultural borrowers and one that makes FSA-guaranteed loans, and compare terms and payment schedules.
  5. Expect weeks, not days. If a planting or harvest date drives the purchase, start early or bridge the machine with equipment financing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can you get an SBA loan for a farm?

Yes. Agricultural businesses are not on SBA's ineligible list in 13 CFR 120.110. The farm has to meet SBA's size standard and the usual credit tests, and should be operating the land rather than only leasing it out.

What is the SBA size standard for a farm?

It depends on the NAICS code. Most crop and livestock codes fall between $2.25 million and $5.5 million in average annual receipts, with exceptions such as cattle feedlots ($22.0 million) and egg production ($19.0 million), under 13 CFR 121.201.

Is an SBA loan or an FSA loan better for a farm?

FSA loans are built for production agriculture and beginning farmers, with direct limits of $400,000 for operating and $600,000 for farm ownership. SBA 7(a) goes to $5 million and also fits agricultural businesses that are not farms. Many growers compare both.

How long can an SBA farm loan run?

Up to 10 years for equipment and working capital, and up to 25 years for real estate or equipment with a useful life over 10 years, under 13 CFR 120.212.

Can I buy farmland with an SBA loan?

Farmland the operation itself farms can be financed as real estate. Land bought to lease to someone else looks passive, which SBA generally does not finance.

Can an SBA loan pay for a tractor?

Yes, but a single tractor usually finances faster through an equipment loan or dealer program. SBA fits better when the machine is part of a larger package.

Sources & Further Reading

  • 13 CFR 120.110: businesses ineligible for SBA loans — The list of business types SBA will not finance; agricultural enterprises are not on it.
  • 13 CFR 121.201: SBA size standards by NAICS code — Receipts limits for crop production (subsector 111) and animal production (112).
  • 13 CFR 121.301: size rules for SBA loans — The alternative size standard: $20 million tangible net worth, $6.5 million average net income.
  • 13 CFR 120.151: 7(a) loan and guaranty limits — The $5,000,000 maximum 7(a) loan and the $3,750,000 guaranty cap per borrower.
  • 13 CFR 120.212: SBA loan maturities — Ten years, or up to 25 years for real estate and long-life equipment.
  • 7 CFR 761.8: FSA farm loan limits — Direct operating ($400,000) and direct farm ownership ($600,000) limits; guaranteed limits indexed.
  • 7 CFR 764.254: FSA operating loan terms — Term operating loans run the useful life of the security or 7 years, whichever is less.
