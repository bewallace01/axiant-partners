Food and Beverage Business Financing for Producers, Brands and Distributors

Purchase orders, retailer receivables, production lines and plants: what each costs, and the food rules lenders ask about

← Back to Manufacturing Business Financing

Updated November 14, 2026

Quick answer

As of November 2026, food and beverage producers usually finance in pieces: purchase order financing for a big retail order (commonly 1.5-6% of supplier cost a month), factoring for grocery and distributor invoices (about 1-5% per invoice with 80-95% advanced), and equipment or SBA loans for lines and plants. Lenders also ask about FDA registration and, for produce buyers, PACA trust payables. Axiant Partners, a business-loan broker in Boca Raton, compares these lenders. Typical ranges, not a quote.

Who This Page Is For

Businesses that make, pack or distribute food and drinks for someone else to sell: emerging consumer brands, co-packers and private-label plants, bakeries and commissaries selling wholesale, craft beverage producers, specialty food importers and regional distributors. If you serve meals to guests, the products and numbers are different; see restaurant business financing.

The common thread is the cash cycle. Ingredients, packaging and co-packer runs are paid for weeks before a retailer or distributor pays the invoice, and growth makes the gap bigger: a new chain account can double the cash tied up before the first payment arrives.

Worked Example: A First Chain Order

Illustrative assumptions, not a quote. A sauce brand lands a $200,000 wholesale order from a regional grocery chain. Ingredients, jars and the co-packer run cost $120,000. Purchase order financing pays the co-packer at an assumed 3% a month for two months; when the goods ship, a factor advances 85% of the invoice, pays off the PO funder, and charges an assumed 3% fee when the chain pays. The chain deducts an assumed $6,000 for promotions and short shipments. Computed by script.

StepAmount
PO fee (3% x 2 months)$7,200
Factor advance (85%)$170,000
Cash to brand at shipment$42,800
Factoring fee (3%)$6,000
Rebate after deductions$18,000
Gross margin$80,000
Left after all costs$60,800

Financing cost $13,200, about 17% of the gross margin, and the order that the brand could not have funded from cash still left about $60,800. The test before signing is the same one in the example: take the fee for the months the money is out, subtract expected deductions, and see what margin is left. Thin-margin orders rarely survive it.

Deductions, Slotting and Seasonality

  • Deductions. Retailers and distributors short-pay invoices for promotions, spoilage, late or short shipments and compliance fines. Factors usually take these from the reserve, so a brand with heavy deductions gets less back than the advance rate suggests. Track deductions by customer before you price a deal.
  • Slotting and launch costs. Fees or free fills to win shelf space are spent before any sales and are not collateral, so asset-based lenders will not fund them directly. They come from equity, a line of credit or a term loan.
  • Seasonality. Grilling sauces, holiday baking, beverages and produce-driven products build inventory months ahead. Lenders want to see last year's monthly sales so a seasonal dip is not read as decline.
  • Shelf life. Perishable finished goods lend at lower advance rates than shelf-stable ones, and many lenders exclude raw perishables entirely.

Food Rules Lenders Ask About

  • FDA facility registration. A facility that manufactures, processes, packs or holds food for U.S. consumption must register before it starts and renew between October 1 and December 31 of each even-numbered year (21 CFR 1.230). Lenders, and factors buying a co-packer's invoices, check that it is current.
  • Food safety plan. Most facilities fall under the FSMA preventive controls rule (21 CFR part 117). A recall can stop collections, so expect questions about the plan and product liability and recall insurance.
  • PACA trust. A business that buys fresh fruit and vegetables holds that produce, the products made from it and the sale proceeds in trust for unpaid produce sellers (7 U.S.C. 499e(c)). Trust claims can come ahead of a lender's lien, so lenders ask how much is owed to produce suppliers and often reserve for it.
  • Alcohol. Breweries file a brewer's notice with TTB (27 CFR 25.62); wineries, distillers and wholesalers hold federal basic permits under 27 CFR part 1. Lenders confirm them before funding a beverage producer.

Equipment and Plants

Fillers, cappers, ovens, mixers, conveyors and packaging lines are financed like other plant machinery, secured by the equipment; see conveyor system financing and equipment financing. Refrigerated delivery is on refrigerated truck financing.

For buying or expanding a plant, SBA 504 and 7(a) offer long fixed or prime-based terms. For 504 loans approved October 1, 2026 through September 30, 2027, SBA waives its upfront and annual fees for manufacturers in NAICS 31-33 and for listed food supply chain businesses; details are on SBA 504 loan fees. Wider options for plants are on manufacturing business financing.

Steps to Get Funded

  1. Sort the needs: orders, receivables, inventory, equipment, plant. Each has its own product.
  2. Gather the file: 3-6 months of bank statements, an accounts receivable aging, a deductions history by customer, purchase orders and supplier quotes, and your FDA registration.
  3. Price the order with fees and deductions before you accept it.
  4. Check how products stack: a factor and a PO funder must agree who is paid first, and an existing blanket lien can block both.
  5. Compare offers on total cost per order, not the headline rate.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do food and beverage companies get financing?

Usually with several products: purchase order financing for large orders, invoice factoring or a line of credit for receivables, inventory financing for stock, and equipment or SBA loans for lines and plants.

How much does purchase order financing cost for a food brand?

Commonly about 1.5-6% of the supplier cost per month the money is outstanding, depending on the order size, the retailer's credit and how reliable the supplier is. Ranges, not a quote.

Can I factor invoices from grocery chains and distributors?

Yes. Factors commonly advance 80-95% of the invoice and charge about 1-5%. Retailer deductions usually come out of the reserve, so heavy deductions reduce what you get back.

What is the PACA trust and why does a lender care?

Under 7 U.S.C. 499e(c), businesses that buy fresh produce hold it and its proceeds in trust for unpaid produce sellers. Those claims can come ahead of a lender's lien, so lenders ask about produce payables.

Do lenders check FDA registration?

Often, yes. Food facilities must register with FDA before operating and renew every even-numbered year between October 1 and December 31 (21 CFR 1.230), and lenders and factors confirm it is current.

Is restaurant financing the same as food and beverage financing?

No. Restaurants are financed on card sales and kitchen equipment. Producers, brands and distributors are financed on orders, receivables, inventory and plant machinery.
