Business Loan vs Line of Credit: Key Differences, Costs and When to Use Each

Lump sum or revolving limit: how each works, what each costs on $50,000, and how to pick.

Home › Working Capital Loans › Articles › Business Loan vs Line of Credit

Updated November 20, 2026

Quick answer

A business loan gives you a lump sum up front that you repay on a fixed schedule, with interest on the whole balance. A business line of credit gives you a limit, from about $10,000 to $2 million or more, that you draw from as needed; you pay interest only on what you use and can borrow again as you repay. Use a loan for a one-time cost like inventory or an expansion, and a line for recurring gaps like payroll while customers pay. For a 6-month, $50,000 need at 12% APR, a line costs about $1,765 in interest; a 12-month loan costs $3,309.

What is the difference between a loan and a line of credit?

The short answer is how you get the money and what you pay interest on. A loan hands you the full amount on day one, and you pay it back in fixed installments whether you spent it all or not. A line of credit is a limit you can tap: draw $20,000 for payroll this month, pay it back when invoices clear, then draw again next quarter for inventory. You only pay interest on what is out.

Feature Term loan Credit line
How you get the money One lump sum Draw what you need, up to a limit
Interest On the full balance Only on what you have drawn
Repayment Fixed schedule: 6-24 months for short-term working capital, 12-84 months for term loans Repay and draw again
Amounts $10,000 to $5 million+ Limits from $10,000 to $2 million+
Time to fund About 3-5 days for working capital; 5-10 business days for larger term loans About 1-2 weeks to open, then same-day draws
Credit 550+ for many programs 600+ for most lines
Best for A defined, one-time need Recurring or unpredictable gaps

How much does each cost on $50,000?

Say you need $50,000 for about six months and both options price at 12% APR. With a 12-month loan you carry the full amount, and pay interest on it, for a year. With a line, you draw $50,000 and pay it back over the six months you actually need it.

Option Payment Interest
Term loan, $50,000 at 12% APR over 12 months $4,442 a month $3,309
Line of credit, $50,000 drawn at 12% APR and repaid over 6 months $8,627 a month $1,765

The line saves about $1,545 here, but only because you paid it off fast. If you leave a line drawn for a year, it costs about the same as the loan. Real offers will differ on rate and fees, so run your own numbers in the business loan calculator.

When is a business loan the better choice?

Pick a loan when you know the amount and the purpose, and you want a fixed payment you can budget around. Typical uses: a bulk inventory buy, a marketing push, a new location, a large contract that needs upfront labor, or covering one known revenue gap. A working capital loan covers short operating needs; a longer term loan suits projects that pay back over several years.

When is a line of credit the better choice?

Pick a line when the need repeats or you cannot predict its size: payroll while customers take 30-90 days to pay, seasonal slowdowns, vendor payments, or a standing cash cushion. Lines are best opened before you need them, since setup takes a week or two. Secured lines backed by receivables or inventory usually carry higher limits and lower rates but require a UCC filing and periodic reporting. See line of credit requirements.

Which is easier to qualify for?

Both look at time in business (1-2+ years preferred), steady revenue, credit and cash flow. Many working capital and term loan programs start around 550 credit. Most lines want 600 or higher and about $10,000 a month in revenue with clean bank statements, because a revolving limit depends on steady behavior. Established businesses with 2+ years often see lines of $50,000 to $500,000 with decisions in 3-5 business days.

Can you use both?

Yes. A common setup is a term loan for the project and a line for day-to-day swings. The mistake to avoid is the reverse: using a line to buy something that should be paid off over years, which leaves no room on the line when a slow month hits. If you are weighing an SBA loan against a line, see SBA loan vs line of credit; for the term-loan angle, term loan vs line of credit.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a business loan and a line of credit?

A business loan pays you a lump sum once and you repay it on a fixed schedule with interest on the full balance. A line of credit is a revolving limit: you draw what you need, pay interest only on that amount, and can draw again as you repay.

Which is cheaper, a business loan or a line of credit?

It depends on how long you need the money. For a short or uneven need, a line usually costs less because you only pay interest on what is drawn: $50,000 repaid over 6 months at 12% costs about $1,765, versus $3,309 on a 12-month loan at the same rate. If you need the full amount for the full term, the loan's fixed payment is easier to plan around.

How much would a $50,000 business loan cost?

At 12% APR over 12 months, a $50,000 loan runs about $4,442 a month and $3,309 in total interest. A shorter term raises the payment and cuts the interest; a higher rate raises both. Use a loan calculator to test your own rate and term.

When should you use a line of credit instead of a loan?

Use a line for needs that come back again and again: payroll while customers pay, seasonal inventory, vendor bills. Use a loan for a defined, one-time project such as a bulk inventory buy, a new location or a large contract.

Is a line of credit harder to get than a business loan?

Often a little. Many working capital and term loan programs start around 550 credit, while most lines want 600 or higher, steady deposits and $10,000+ in monthly revenue. Secured lines backed by receivables or inventory can offer higher limits.

Can a business have both a loan and a line of credit?

Yes, and many established businesses do. A term loan pays for a specific project and the line covers day-to-day swings, so you are not drawing down the line for something that should be paid off over years.
