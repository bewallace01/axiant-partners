MCA vs Working Capital Loan

Speed, cost, structure — the actual numbers behind a merchant cash advance vs a working capital loan

Speed, cost, structure — the actual numbers behind a merchant cash advance vs a working capital loan

Updated April 10, 2026

Quick answer

A merchant cash advance funds in 1-3 days at a factor rate of 1.20-1.50 — equivalent to a 40-100%+ APR depending on term. A working capital loan funds in 3-10 days at an APR of 9-30%. Same cash; the MCA typically costs 3-5x more for the same dollars. MCAs win on speed and approval rate; working capital loans win on cost, every time. The right move 90% of the time is to apply for the working capital loan first and only fall back to an MCA if declined and the cash is genuinely time-critical.

The MCA-vs-working-capital decision is the most expensive financial choice most small businesses make in their first 5 years. The cost difference between the two products is real and large; the speed difference is real but smaller than most owners think. This page walks the actual math, the structural differences, and when each product fits. For broader context see working capital loans and revenue-based financing.

Real Cost Comparison: $50K of Cash

Take the same $50,000 of working capital. Two offers come in:

  • MCA: $50K advance, 1.30 factor rate, 6-month term, daily ACH ~$540/business day. Total payback: $65,000. Cost of capital: $15,000. Equivalent APR: ~80-100%.
  • Working capital loan: $50K, 18% APR, 12-month term, monthly P&I ~$4,580. Total payback: $54,950. Cost of capital: $4,950. Actual APR: 18%.

Same dollars, $10,000 cost difference. The MCA is 3x more expensive. This isn't edge math — this is the median outcome on real offers.

Why Factor Rates Mislead

A 1.30 factor rate “sounds” cheaper than a 30% APR because it's a smaller number. The trick is that factor rates are flat — they do not annualize. A 1.30 factor over 6 months is the same dollar cost as a 1.30 factor over 12 months from the customer’s view, but the equivalent APR doubles when the term halves. MCA brokers price short terms because the dollar number stays the same while the effective APR — the actual cost of capital — explodes. Always convert factor rate to APR before comparing.

Quick conversion: (factor - 1) / term in years × 1.8 approximates the APR for a daily-payback structure. A 1.30 factor on a 6-month term: 0.30 / 0.5 × 1.8 = 108% APR.

When Each Product Actually Fits

Working capital loan wins when

  • You have 600+ FICO and 6+ months in business and can wait 3-10 days for funding
  • You need $25K-$500K for a defined use of funds
  • You can support fixed monthly payments from operations
  • The use of funds won't produce returns higher than the working capital APR

MCA fits when

  • You have been declined for a working capital loan — not before that
  • You need cash within 48 hours for a genuinely time-critical use
  • Your credit is sub-600 and your bank statements are strong
  • The use of funds will produce more than the equivalent APR in incremental cash — rare but possible (filling a large PO, taking a deeply-discounted inventory deal, etc.)

The MCA Stacking Trap

The fastest way to a cash crisis is taking a second MCA on top of a first to cover the daily ACH from the first. Each new MCA brings its own daily debit; two stacked MCAs eat 4-8% of daily revenue, three eats 8-12%. At that level the business can't generate enough cash to keep up, defaults follow, and refinancing into a single working capital loan becomes nearly impossible because lenders see the MCA stack and decline. If you have one MCA, the right move is to refinance to a working capital loan or term loan as soon as you qualify — not stack a second.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between an MCA and a working capital loan?

An MCA buys a share of your future sales repaid via daily or weekly remittances; a working capital loan is borrowed money repaid on a fixed schedule. The loan is usually cheaper and more transparent; the MCA is faster and easier to qualify for.

Why do factor rates mislead?

A factor rate is not an APR. A 1.35 factor sounds modest but, repaid over a few months, translates to a very high annualized cost. Always convert to total dollars and an effective APR to compare fairly.

Which is cheaper, an MCA or a working capital loan?

A working capital loan, in almost every case where you qualify, because it's structured and priced as a loan. The MCA’s convenience and speed come at a materially higher effective cost.

What is the MCA stacking trap?

Taking a second or third MCA to cover the first. Each advance adds another daily remittance and raises the total revenue share, quickly choking cash flow — the fast lane into a cycle that is hard to escape.

Is an MCA cheaper than a working capital loan?

Almost never. A 1.30 factor rate on a 6-month MCA equates to roughly 70-90% APR. A working capital loan at 9-30% APR is typically 3-5x cheaper for the same dollars. MCAs win on speed and approval rate, not cost.

What is a factor rate vs an APR?

A factor rate is a flat multiplier on the principal (e.g., 1.30 means $50K becomes $65K total payback). An APR is annualized and reflects total cost over time. A 1.30 factor rate over 6 months equals roughly 80% APR; over 12 months, roughly 40% APR. The shorter the term, the higher the equivalent APR.

How fast does each fund?

MCAs typically fund in 24-72 hours from a complete application. Working capital loans fund in 3-10 business days. The speed difference is real but the cost difference is larger.

Do MCAs check credit?

Yes, but loosely. Most MCA underwriting is based on bank statements (revenue and deposit consistency) rather than FICO. Sub-600 borrowers who can't get a working capital loan often qualify for MCAs.

When does an MCA make sense?

An MCA makes sense when you need cash in 48 hours, have been declined for a working capital loan, and the use of funds will produce more than the factor rate's effective APR. Stacking MCAs on top of each other is the fastest way to a debt spiral.
