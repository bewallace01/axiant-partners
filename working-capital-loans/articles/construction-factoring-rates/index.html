Construction Factoring Rates: What Subcontractors Really Pay

Fees, advance rates and why retainage changes the math, with a progress-billing example you can rerun on your own pay application

Retainage, commonly 5-10%, is usually excluded, and slow payers push you up the range. Typical Construction Factoring Costs

We don't publish a separate construction price, because factors quote this work case by case. The table below applies the ranges on our invoice factoring rates page to situations a contractor can recognize.

Your receivableLikely fee zoneWhy
Strong GC, pays near net 30About 1-2%Good credit and fast pay, with steady volume
Mixed GCs, average payAbout 2-3.5%Moderate volume and more verification
Slow GC, net 60-90About 3.5-5%More tiers while the invoice is open
Pay-if-paid, thin file4-5% or more, or a declineHigher chance that payment never comes due

Many factors won't do construction at all, so expect fewer quotes than a staffing company would get. That is one reason a contractor can't assume the bottom of the range. Advance rates commonly run 80-95% of what's factored, with the rest held as a reserve until the customer pays.

Why Retainage Changes the Math

The percentage on the proposal applies to less than you billed. If a factor excludes retainage, your advance starts from the currently payable amount, so compare quotes on cash received, not on the headline fee. The mechanics are covered in our construction invoice factoring guide. This page is about the price.

Watch for the money sitting in two places: retainage held by the GC, and the reserve held by the factor. On a long job, both can be outstanding while your payroll runs. If retainage is the bigger problem, see our guide to the retainage cash flow gap.

Worked Example: A $100,000 Progress Billing

Illustrative numbers, not a quote. A subcontractor bills $100,000 on an approved pay application with 10% retainage. The factor advances 85% of the payable amount and charges 3.5% for 60 days.

ItemMathAmount
BilledApproved pay application$100,000
Retainage held10%$10,000
Payable amountBilled less retainage$90,000
Advance today85% of $90,000$76,500
Fee at 60 days3.5% of $90,000$3,150
Reserve released later$90,000 less advance and fee$10,350

So you get $76,500 now on a $100,000 billing, or about 76.5 cents on the dollar. Annualized, $3,150 on $76,500 for 60 days is about 25%, which is useful for comparing with a line of credit. That figure is only arithmetic. It doesn't include setup, wire or minimum fees. Rerun it at net 90 under tiered pricing, because the fee may rise as the invoice stays open.

What Moves Your Rate

  • Who pays you. A factor relies on the GC or owner's credit more than yours, so a strong payer helps most.
  • Billing quality. Signed pay applications are easier to fund than billings still open to adjustment.
  • Contract language. Pay-if-paid is riskier than pay-when-paid because it can decide whether you're paid at all.
  • Lien discipline. Preliminary notices and clean waivers protect the collateral behind the receivable.
  • Concentration. One GC on most of your billing can lower the advance or raise the fee.
  • Volume. Steady monthly volume generally earns better tiers than one-off billings.

Federal Jobs Have Their Own Rules

Factoring a federal receivable has an extra step. The Assignment of Claims Act lets money due on a federal contract be assigned to a financing institution if the contract doesn't forbid it. It goes to one party, and written notice must go to the contracting officer, any surety and the disbursing official. Primes need that paperwork, and it adds time, so ask about it before pricing the deal. Subcontractors bill the prime, not the government, and FAR 52.232-27 sets payment timing for subs on federal jobs, which can make the receivable easier to judge. Bonded private jobs raise a similar question, so tell the factor if a surety is involved.

Fees to Ask About Beyond the Rate

  • Setup or due diligence. A one-time charge to open the account, which may be higher for construction review.
  • Tier schedule. The exact fee at 30, 45, 60 and 90 days, not just the first tier.
  • Minimums and exit. Monthly minimum volume and the penalty for leaving early.
  • Recourse. When an unpaid invoice comes back to you, and how fast.
  • Disputes. What happens when a GC back-charges or adjusts a billing after you've been advanced.
  • Retainage. Whether it's excluded, discounted or financed separately, and on what terms.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are typical construction factoring rates?

Expect fees within the general 1-5% per invoice range, with advances of 80-95% on the approved, payable part of a progress billing. Slow payers, pay-if-paid clauses and thin files push toward the top of that range. These are typical ranges across factors, not a quote.

Does a factor advance against retainage?

Usually not. Retainage is paid later and depends on completion, so most factors exclude it or discount it heavily. Some offer separate retainage financing.

How much cash do I get on a $100,000 progress billing?

With 10% retainage and an 85% advance, about $76,500 up front. After a 3.5% fee on $90,000, about $10,350 of reserve comes later. That example is illustrative.

Why do many factors avoid construction?

Progress billings can be adjusted or disputed, pay-if-paid clauses can make a payment contingent, and lien rights and waivers complicate collateral. Factors that do this work build those risks into their terms.

Can I factor a federal construction contract?

Often, with an assignment under the Assignment of Claims Act if the contract doesn't forbid it, and with written notice to the required officials. It takes extra time, so raise it early.

Is a line of credit cheaper than construction factoring?

Often, if you qualify, since factoring can be expensive when annualized. Factoring leans on your customer's credit, so it can fit younger contractors who can't get a large line yet.
