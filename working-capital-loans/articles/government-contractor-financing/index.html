Government Contract Financing: How Contractors Fund the Gap Before the Agency Pays

Factoring, mobilization, PO financing and credit lines for contractors who perform first and get paid later.

Home › Working Capital Loans › Articles › Government Contract Financing

Updated November 20, 2026

Quick answer

Government contract financing covers the costs you carry between starting work on a contract and getting paid by the agency. The usual tools are government receivables factoring, which advances 80-90% of an accepted agency invoice within days; mobilization financing for the startup costs of a new award; purchase order financing for supplier costs, at about 1.5-6% a month; and a line of credit sized to one or two months of contract costs. Federal agencies generally aim to pay within 30 days of a proper invoice, but the real gap often runs one to three months.

What is government contract financing?

Government work has one great feature and one painful one. The great part: your customer is a federal, state or local agency, about as reliable a payer as exists. The painful part: you pay to perform long before that money comes back. You staff up, buy materials, mobilize and pay subcontractors, then bill through the agency's system and wait. Government contract financing is the money that carries you through that wait.

The gap grows as you win. Every new award means another round of upfront cost, so a contractor on a winning streak can be the one shortest on cash. The payer is not the problem; the timing is. For the basics of the product category, see working capital loans.

How long does the government take to pay?

Federal agencies work under the Prompt Payment Act, which generally targets payment within 30 days of a proper invoice. “Proper” carries a lot of weight there. The clock starts only after you have performed, billed correctly through the right system and had the invoice accepted; an error sends it back to the start. Add milestone or progress billing on bigger contracts, retainage on some, and no payment history on a new award, and the real gap between spending and collecting often runs one to three months. State and local agencies vary more and can be slower.

What you pay for before the agency pays you

  • Payroll: W-2 staff and contract workers are paid on schedule whether or not the invoice has cleared.
  • Mobilization: setup, travel, site work and equipment to start a new contract.
  • Materials and subcontractors, who expect payment on their own terms.
  • Compliance: SAM registration, certifications, bonding and the overhead of meeting contract requirements.
  • Insurance required to hold and perform the contract.

On a large new award, that upfront cost can reach six or seven figures before the first payment arrives.

Most contractors combine two of these: mobilization or PO financing to start a contract, then factoring or a line to carry payroll while invoices are paid. To compare the two most common revolving structures, read business loan vs line of credit.

Government receivables factoring

Factoring fits government work unusually well because the factor is relying on the agency to pay, and the agency is the most creditworthy payer in the economy. That is why small and newer contractors can often get funded this way. You submit an accepted invoice, receive an advance (often 80-90% or more) within days, and get the rest, minus the fee, when the agency pays. On a $200,000 accepted invoice, an 80% advance puts $160,000 in your account while you wait.

One federal wrinkle: the Assignment of Claims Act governs how payments on federal contracts can be assigned to a financing company, so use a factor that has done government work and handles the notices. For the general mechanics, see what invoice factoring is and invoice factoring rates.

Purchase order financing for supply contracts

If the contract is for goods you buy from a supplier and deliver, purchase order financing can pay the supplier directly, up to 100% of the supplier cost on strong deals. It costs more than a bank line, commonly around 1.5-6% of the supplier cost per month, so it fits orders with healthy margins.

How much financing can a government contractor get?

Amounts scale with contract value and invoice volume, typically from $50,000 into the millions. Factoring capacity grows with what you bill rather than sitting at a fixed cap. Lines of credit and working capital are commonly sized to one or two months of contract costs. Because underwriting leans on the agency and the award, a small business with a solid contract can often get meaningful capacity quickly. These are illustrative ranges, not quotes; for more on sizing, see how much working capital you can qualify for.

What lenders look at

  • The agency: federal and established state and local agencies are the foundation of the collateral.
  • Contract type: firm-fixed-price, time-and-materials and cost-reimbursement contracts bill differently.
  • Billing accuracy: clean, system-compliant invoices with few rejections show the receivables will pay.
  • Registration and past performance: active SAM registration, valid awards and a track record.
  • Labor and subcontractor structure, which drives when cash goes out.

To prepare a file, see what lenders look for in a working capital application.

How do you get government contracts, and when should financing be in place?

To hold a federal contract, your business has to be actively registered in SAM.gov, and many contracts also require specific certifications, bonding and insurance. Those are the same items lenders check, so getting them in order helps on both fronts.

The moment that stalls most contractors is winning an award bigger than their cash can carry. Payroll and mobilization for the larger contract hit weeks or months before the first payment. Contractors with factoring or mobilization financing lined up can ramp into the award; those without it sometimes decline work they fought to win. Arrange capacity while you bid, not after the award lands.

What to avoid

The common mistake is covering the gap with expensive short-term money, such as daily-payment advances, on contracts that were priced tight to begin with. Factoring and mobilization financing match the perform-first, get-paid-later cycle much better. Keep billing system-compliant so invoices are not rejected, handle Assignment of Claims correctly on federal work, and watch your exposure if one contract is most of your revenue. If you are already carrying expensive advances, read how to get out of business debt.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is government contract financing?

It is financing that covers payroll, materials, mobilization and subcontractors while you wait for a government agency to pay. The most common forms are receivables factoring, mobilization financing, purchase order financing and a business line of credit. The agency is a strong payer, so lenders lean on the contract and the invoices.

What is government receivables factoring?

You sell accepted invoices you have billed to a federal, state or local agency to a factor, which advances 80-90% or more within days and pays the rest, minus its fee, when the agency pays. Because the payer is the government, the receivables are strong collateral. On federal work the Assignment of Claims Act sets how payments can be assigned, so use a factor that knows the paperwork.

How long does the government take to pay contractors?

Federal agencies operate under the Prompt Payment Act, which generally targets payment within 30 days of a proper invoice. The clock starts only after the invoice is accepted, and rejected invoices start over. Counting performance, billing and milestones, the practical gap often runs one to three months, and state and local agencies vary more.

Can a small or new government contractor get financing?

Often, yes. Factoring and contract financing lean on the agency's credit and the awarded contract more than on how long you have been in business. A newer contractor with a valid award and clean billing can often qualify before a bank would offer a line.

How much financing can a government contractor get?

Amounts typically run from $50,000 into the millions, scaling with contract value and invoice volume. Factoring grows with what you bill instead of sitting at a fixed cap. Lines of credit and working capital are often sized to one or two months of contract costs.

What is mobilization financing?

Mobilization financing pays the upfront cost of starting a new contract, such as hiring, travel, site setup, equipment and materials, before you can submit the first invoice. It covers the tightest cash period, right after the award. Once you are billing, many contractors move to factoring or a line.
