Why Restaurants Run Short Even Though They Are Paid Daily

Most businesses that need working capital are waiting on customers who pay in 30 or 60 days. A restaurant isn’t: card sales settle within days and cash comes in every night. The gaps come from somewhere else.

  • Seasons. Rent, salaried staff, insurance and loan payments stay the same in a slow month while sales drop. A beach town or a college town can lose half its volume for a season.
  • Repairs that can’t wait. A walk-in cooler, a hood system or a dish machine fails on a Friday and has to be fixed before service.
  • Inventory runs on short terms. Food suppliers commonly want payment weekly or on delivery, so a menu change or a holiday stock-up is paid before it sells.
  • Openings and remodels. Hiring and training a crew, opening stock and weeks of reduced sales come before the dining room is full.
  • Money that isn’t yours. Sales tax collected and tips owed to staff sit in the same account and can make the balance look larger than it is.

Because the gap is about time and seasons rather than receivables, invoice factoring rarely fits a restaurant. Revolving credit and planned borrowing do. Catering and contract food service, which do bill on terms, are the exception.

Worked Example: Funding a Four-Month Off-Season

Illustrative assumptions, not a quote. A seasonal restaurant sells $150,000 a month in season and $60,000 a month for four slow months. Food and beverage cost is assumed at 30% of sales, slow-season labor $30,000 a month, and rent and other fixed costs $22,000 a month. Each slow month then runs $10,000 short ($60,000 less $18,000, $30,000 and $22,000), and the month before reopening adds $20,000 for stock and hiring.

MonthWhat happensLine of credit balance
1-4Draw $10,000 each slow month$10,000 rising to $40,000
5Draw $20,000 to restock and hire$60,000
6Season opens; repay $30,000$30,000
7Repay the rest$0

At an illustrative 12% APR, charged at 1% a month on each month’s balance, the interest on that schedule is about $1,900. The same $60,000 from a merchant cash advance at a 1.30 factor costs $18,000, and its holdback comes out of card sales during the slow months, when there is least to spare. In this example a peak month leaves about $35,000 after costs (at an assumed $48,000 of in-season labor), so repaying the line in two months is realistic. Put in your own numbers before you size a line.

Card Sales, Holdbacks and Why the Choice Matters

Restaurants get offered merchant cash advances more than almost any other business, because card sales are easy to see and easy to collect from. The advance is repaid from a share of each day’s card receipts, so it is quick to get and hard to live with in a slow stretch. Two checks before signing anything: ask for the total repayment in writing, and convert it to an APR over the expected payback period. Our MCA for restaurants guide covers holdbacks in detail. The FTC has warned providers against misrepresenting these costs.

If you already carry advances that are squeezing the slow season, see restaurant debt relief before adding another one.

What Lenders Check on a Restaurant File

  • Bank and card processing statements, commonly three to six months, and a full year if you are seasonal, so the lender sees the slow months as well as the good ones.
  • The lease. Years left on the lease, and whether it runs past the loan term, matter because the location is the business.
  • Existing daily or weekly debits. Advances already on the account limit what a bank will add.
  • Time open and ownership. Two or more years under the same owner opens more bank and SBA options.
  • Personal credit of the owners, since most restaurant loans carry a personal guarantee.

How to Line Up Working Capital Before the Slow Season

  1. Map last year month by month: sales, food cost, labor and fixed costs. The low point is the size of the line you need.
  2. Apply in season, while deposits are strong. A bank line is far easier to get in July than in January.
  3. Keep sales tax and tips separate so they are never spent as working capital.
  4. Finance equipment as equipment. Don’t drain the line for a replacement cooler that a lease or loan can cover.
  5. Set a repayment rule, such as repaying the line within two peak months, so it is clear by the next slow season.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best working capital option for a restaurant?

For seasons and repairs, usually a business line of credit, commonly about 8-13% APR at a bank and 14-30%+ online, because you draw only what you need and repay in busy months. An SBA 7(a) loan suits a larger planned need. These are typical ranges, not a quote.

Why do restaurants need working capital if they are paid daily?

Because costs don't fall with sales. Rent, salaried staff and insurance stay the same in a slow month, repairs can't wait, suppliers want payment weekly or on delivery, and openings and remodels cost money before sales arrive.

Is a merchant cash advance a good idea for a restaurant?

Usually only for a short, small emergency. Factor rates of 1.15-1.50 repaid from daily card sales often work out to roughly 40-100%+ in effective APR, and the holdback bites hardest in slow months. Compare the total repayment with a line of credit first.

Can a restaurant use an SBA loan for working capital?

Yes. Working capital is an allowed use of SBA 7(a) proceeds, and the SBA caps the rate, for example at the base rate plus 6.0% on loans of $50,001-$250,000. It is slower than other options, so it fits a planned need better than an emergency.

How much working capital does a restaurant need?

Enough to cover the deepest point of your slow season plus a repair reserve. Map last year month by month; in the example on this page, four slow months and a restock add up to $60,000.

When should a restaurant apply for a line of credit?

During the busy season, while deposits are strongest. Lenders read recent bank statements, so applying in the slow months makes the business look weaker than it is.
