What Is a Working Capital Loan, and When Is It the Wrong Tool?

What it pays for, how the terms work, what it costs, and the cases where another loan is the better call.

Home › Working Capital Loans › Articles › What Is a Working Capital Loan?

Updated November 20, 2026

Quick answer

A working capital loan is short-term business financing for day-to-day operating costs, such as payroll, inventory, rent and the gap while customers pay, rather than for long-term assets. Amounts run from about $10,000 to $5 million or more. Short-term versions are repaid over 6-24 months, often with daily or weekly payments, and usually fund within 48 hours to a week. SBA working capital loans can run up to 10 years but take 30-60+ days. Most lenders want 6+ months in business and $10,000+ in monthly revenue.

What is a working capital loan?

A working capital loan pays for running the business, not for buying something that lasts for years. Think payroll during a slow month, inventory before your busy season, materials for a big job, or rent while a large customer takes 60 days to pay. You borrow a set amount, use it within a few weeks or months, and pay it back from the cash the business brings in.

That is the difference from an equipment loan or a mortgage. Those are tied to an asset and paid back over many years. A working capital loan is usually unsecured or lightly secured, shorter, and priced higher because of it. For the full product range, see working capital loans.

How does a working capital loan work?

Most lenders look at your bank deposits, time in business and credit, then offer a lump sum or a revolving limit. The main versions:

Type Amount Term Speed Best for
Short-term working capital loan $10,000 and up 6-24 months, daily or weekly payments Funds in 48 hours to 1 week One-time needs: inventory, a project, a short gap
Larger working capital program Up to $500,000 Up to 60 months Decision in 2-5 business days Established companies with steady revenue
Business line of credit Revolving limit Draw, repay, draw again 1-2 weeks to open Recurring, unpredictable gaps
SBA working capital loan Up to $5 million (7(a) program maximum) Up to 10 years 30-60+ days Lower cost when you can wait

A short-term loan gives you the money once with a fixed payment schedule. A line of credit lets you draw, repay and draw again, paying interest only on what you use. If you are choosing between the two, read business loan vs line of credit.

What is the main purpose of working capital?

Working capital is the cushion between paying your costs and collecting from customers. On paper it is current assets (cash, receivables, inventory) minus current liabilities (bills, payroll and debt due within a year). A business with $180,000 in current assets and $120,000 in current liabilities has $60,000 of working capital.

More is usually better, up to a point. Too little and one late customer means a missed payroll. A very large balance sitting idle may mean cash that could be paying down expensive debt or funding growth. A working capital loan is for the first problem: a temporary gap that the business will close from its own revenue.

What does a working capital loan cost?

Short-term working capital usually prices at about 10-30% APR, depending on credit, time in business and how fast you need it. A $50,000 loan at 15% APR over 12 months runs about $4,513 a month. SBA working capital costs less but takes 30-60 days or more. The business loan calculator shows the payment for your own amount and term.

Who qualifies?

  • Time in business: at least 6 months for most programs.
  • Revenue: about $10,000 a month or more.
  • Credit: many lenders accept 550+, and 680+ usually gets better rates. See credit score for a working capital loan.
  • Bank statements showing steady deposits and few overdrafts.

For the full list, see business loan requirements, and for sizing, how much you can qualify for.

When is a working capital loan the wrong tool?

Using short-term money for a long-term purchase is a common and expensive mistake. The payments are too high for what the asset earns each month, and you end up refinancing under pressure. Eight cases where something else usually fits better:

  1. Buying commercial real estate. Office, industrial, retail or medical property belongs in an SBA 504 or 7(a) loan or a commercial mortgage, with 20-25 year amortization and lower payments.
  2. Buying heavy equipment. Construction equipment, machinery, trucks and farm equipment fit equipment financing, where the asset is the collateral and pricing is usually better than unsecured working capital.
  3. Needing long-term capital. If you need 10-25 years to repay, look at SBA financing. Working capital is a liquidity tool, not permanent capital.
  4. Unstable or falling revenue. Lenders want consistent deposits and a stable cash flow trend. If revenue is sliding, approval is harder, limits are lower, and the payment adds risk.
  5. A heavy existing debt load. Lenders check your debt service coverage ratio. If you already carry several loans, restructuring them is usually the better first step; see how to get out of business debt.
  6. Small, recurring gaps. If you need a cushion you dip into every few months, a line of credit is more efficient than taking a new lump sum each time.
  7. Cost matters more than speed. If you can wait 30-60+ days for SBA underwriting, you will usually pay less.
  8. Buying a business. Acquisitions and partner buyouts fit acquisition financing, with longer terms and underwriting built for it.

The short version:

If you need Use instead
To buy commercial property SBA 504, SBA 7(a) real estate, or a commercial mortgage (20-25 year amortization)
To buy equipment or vehicles Equipment financing or leasing
10-25 years to repay An SBA loan
To buy a business SBA acquisition financing
Draw-as-needed cash A business line of credit

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a working capital loan?

It is a short-term business loan for operating costs such as payroll, inventory, rent and the wait while customers pay. Short-term versions run 6-24 months, often with daily or weekly payments, and amounts start around $10,000. It is not meant for buying buildings, heavy equipment or another business.

What is the main purpose of working capital?

Working capital is the cash that keeps the business running between paying your costs and collecting from customers. It covers payroll, suppliers and rent while invoices are outstanding. A working capital loan tops it up when the gap is bigger than your cash.

How do you calculate working capital?

Subtract current liabilities from current assets. A business with $180,000 in cash, receivables and inventory and $120,000 in bills and debt due within a year has $60,000 of working capital. Lenders look at the trend in your bank deposits as much as this number.

How long are working capital loan terms?

Short-term working capital loans usually run 6-24 months. Larger programs go up to 60 months, and SBA working capital loans can run up to 10 years. Shorter terms fund faster but cost more per year.

What credit score do you need for a working capital loan?

Many lenders accept 550 or higher, and 680+ usually gets better rates and terms. Lenders also want at least 6 months in business and about $10,000 a month in revenue. Steady deposits matter as much as the score.

When is a working capital loan the wrong choice?

When you are buying real estate, heavy equipment or a business, when you need 10-25 years to repay, when revenue is falling, or when you already carry heavy debt. In those cases an SBA loan, equipment financing, a commercial mortgage or a debt restructure usually costs less.
