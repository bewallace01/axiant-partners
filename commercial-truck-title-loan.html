A commercial truck title loan is business-purpose financing secured by a commercial vehicle you already own: the lender places a lien on the title and advances a percentage of the truck's value, while you keep possession and keep the truck in revenue service.

How a Truck Title Loan Actually Works

If you own a truck outright, that truck is a parked asset with cash locked inside it. A title loan unlocks that cash without taking the truck off the road. The mechanics are straightforward:

  • You establish the value. The lender values the unit from year, make, model, mileage, engine, condition, and recent comparable sales. This is a wholesale or liquidation number, not what a dealer would ask for the same truck on a lot.
  • The lender advances a percentage of that value. Not the whole number — a portion, sized so the lender stays covered if it ever has to sell the unit.
  • A lien goes on the title. The lender becomes lienholder of record. You remain the owner and the registered operator.
  • You keep driving. The truck stays in service, generating the revenue that services the loan. Lenders generally require you to keep insurance in force naming them as lienholder, and most require ACH payments and a GPS unit.
  • You pay it down over a term. Structured in months, on a fixed schedule, not as a balloon due in 30 days.

The reason owner-operators and small fleets reach for this instead of an unsecured business loan is simple: the collateral does the heavy lifting. When a lender can value and recover the asset, your credit file matters far less than it would on a signature loan.

This Is Not a Consumer Car Title Loan

The phrase "title loan" carries baggage, and it's worth being precise, because two very different products share the name.

Title Loan or Refinance? They Are Not the Same Move

Owners searching for a truck refinance loan and owners searching for a title loan often want the same outcome — cash, or a lower payment — but the right structure depends on whether there is still a lender on the title.

Your situationThe structureWhat you get
Truck paid off, clean titleTitle loan against the unitThe largest cash advance, simplest closing
Still owe, but the truck is worth well moreCash-out refinancePays off the old lender, returns the surplus to you
Still owe, payment is too highRate-and-term refinanceLower or restructured payment, no cash out
Owe close to what it's worthNeither worksNo equity to draw against

That last row matters. A truck that is upside down, or barely above water, has nothing to lend against. If that's where you're, the productive conversation is about working capital or fixing the payment, not pulling equity that's not there.

What Trucks and Trailers Qualify

Lenders in this space want titled, commercially used units with an active resale market. Broadly:

  • Sleeper and day cab tractors — the core of the market. Newer units with lower mileage and mainstream drivetrains move easiest.
  • Box and straight trucks — widely financed, including for last-mile and delivery operations.
  • Vocational units — dump trucks, tow trucks and wreckers, service trucks, vacuum trucks, water trucks, and roll-offs.
  • Trailers and reefers — dry vans, flatbeds, drop decks, lowboys, dump trailers, and refrigerated units, valued on age, type, and for reefers, engine hours.

What usually stops a deal: a rebuilt, rebranded, or salvaged title; unclear or contested ownership; an open bankruptcy; sizeable unresolved tax liens; or a unit so old, so high-mileage, or so specialized that there's no dependable resale market for it. Engine make and model matter more than owners expect — some drivetrains are much harder to place than others, and that shows up directly in what a lender will advance.

How Much Can You Raise?

Two owners with the same truck model can get very different numbers. What drives it:

  • Mileage and year — the single biggest lever on a tractor's value.
  • Engine make and model — resale demand varies sharply by drivetrain, and lenders price that in.
  • Condition and maintenance records — documented service history raises both the value and the advance rate against it.
  • Title status — clean and lien-free supports the most; an existing payoff reduces what is left for you.
  • Your business — bank deposits and time in business affect the size of the loan the cash flow can carry, even when the collateral supports more.

Because lenders advance against liquidation value not retail asking price, the honest first step is finding out what the unit is worth to a lender. When It Makes Sense — and When It Does Not

It tends to make sense when: you own a truck free and clear and need capital for something that earns; you're covering a repair, an insurance renewal, or an IFTA bill that would otherwise park the unit; you're funding a down payment to add a second truck; or you are replacing expensive short-term debt with a secured loan at a lower cost of capital.

It tends not to make sense when: the truck is your only asset and revenue is already unstable, because you're putting the thing that earns your living behind a lien; the amount you need is small enough that a line of credit would serve better; or you're borrowing to cover a shortfall that will simply recur next month. Adding a secured payment to an operation that cannot carry the payments it already has makes the problem worse, not better.

If you're carrying merchant cash advances and thinking about using truck equity to clear them, that specific move has its own tradeoffs. See using asset equity to pay off MCAs.

What You Will Need to Apply

  • The truck's title, VIN, and current registration
  • Year, make, model, mileage, engine make and model, and transmission
  • Photos and, where available, recent maintenance and service records
  • Proof of insurance
  • Recent business bank statements, typically the last three months
  • If there is an existing lender, a current payoff quote

Deals in this category move quickly when the paperwork is complete. They stall when the title isn't in hand, ownership is unclear, or the payoff figure is a guess.

Other Ways to Raise Cash From Equipment You Own

A title loan is one route. Depending on what you own, others may raise more or cost less:

  • Sale-leaseback — sell owned equipment to a lessor for a lump sum and lease it back, keeping it in service.
  • Equipment refinancing and collateral loans — borrow against machinery and keep the title, or restructure existing equipment debt.
  • Real estate secured business loans — if you own a terminal, shop, yard, or other property, that equity is most of the time the cheapest capital on your balance sheet.
  • Invoice factoring — if the pressure is slow-paying brokers rather than a lack of assets, factoring the receivable may solve it without a lien on the truck.

Owners who hold both trucks and property often do best combining them, because more collateral in the deal generally means a better structure on the whole package.

What Your Truck Is Actually Worth to a Lender

Owners almost always start from the wrong number. The figure in your head is typically a dealer's asking price or what you paid. A lender works from what it could recover in a quick sale, which is a materially lower number, and then advances a percentage of that.

A rough sense of how the gap compounds on a typical late-model sleeper:

FigureWhat it representsDirection
Dealer asking priceRetail, reconditioned, with a warrantyHighest
Clean trade / book valueWhat a dealer would allow youLower
Orderly liquidationSold over a reasonable windowLower again
Forced liquidationAuctioned quicklyLowest
Your advanceA percentage of the liquidation figureLower still

That's not a lender being unreasonable. It's what a repossessed unit with unknown maintenance history fetches at auction. The practical takeaway: get the value established before you build a plan around a number. Our guide to equipment and machinery appraisal explains fair market, orderly liquidation, and forced liquidation value and why lenders use the lower ones.

What moves your number up: complete service records, a mainstream engine with an active resale market, lower mileage for the model year, a clean and lien-free title, and photographs that show the unit has been looked after. What moves it down: an engine that is hard to place, deferred maintenance, an accident history, or a title with anything unusual on it.

Title Loan, Sale-Leaseback, or Just Sell It?

If the goal is cash and you own the truck, three structures compete. They differ mainly in whether you keep the unit and whether you keep the title.

Title loanSale-leasebackOutright sale
Keep driving itYesYesNo
Keep the titleYesNo, with buy-backNo
Cash raisedPercentage of valueOften moreFull market value
Ongoing paymentYesYes, leaseNone
Best whenYou need the unit and the cashYou want maximum cash and can leaseThe unit is surplus or idle

The one owners overlook is the third column. If a truck is sitting because you can't crew it, financing it to raise cash means paying interest on an asset that isn't earning. Selling it and keeping the proceeds is frequently the better answer, and no lender will volunteer that. Tell us the situation and we will say which of the three raises the most net of cost.

What Happens After You Apply

The process is short when the paperwork is in order, and the steps are predictable:

  1. Value the unit. Year, make, model, mileage, engine, condition, and photos. Usually same-day.
  2. Confirm the title. Clean and in your name, or a payoff quote if a lender is still on it.
  3. Light underwriting. Typically the last three months of business bank statements. The collateral carries most of the decision.
  4. Offer and terms. Advance amount, payment, and term, with any GPS or documentation requirements stated up front.
  5. Lien and funding. The lender records against the title and funds. You keep the truck the entire time.

Deals stall for boring reasons: the title isn't physically in hand, ownership doesn't match the business name, or a payoff figure turns out to be a guess. Sorting those before you apply is the difference between days and weeks. Start the review and we will tell you what's missing before it costs you time.

Commercial Truck Title Loan FAQs

What is a commercial truck title loan?

It's business-purpose financing secured by a commercial vehicle you already own. The lender places a lien on the truck's title and advances a percentage of its value; you keep possession and keep the truck working. It raises capital from equity already sitting in the equipment over financing a purchase.

Is a commercial truck title loan the same as a car title loan?

No. A consumer car title loan is a short-term personal loan, often due in 30 days, and is capped, restricted, or banned in many states. A commercial truck title loan is a business-purpose loan to a company or owner-operator, secured by a revenue-producing titled asset, underwritten on both the truck's value and the business's ability to repay, and amortized over months. Different products, different rules.

Do I need to own my truck free and clear?

Not always. A clean, lien-free title produces the largest advance and the simplest closing. If you still owe, the usual route is a cash-out refinance that pays off the existing lender and returns the surplus equity to you — which only works if the truck is worth meaningfully more than the payoff. If you owe close to what it is worth, there is nothing to draw against.

How much can I borrow against my semi truck?

A percentage of the truck's value, and lenders lend against liquidation value and not retail asking price. Year, mileage, engine make and model, maintenance records, condition, and resale demand all move the number. A newer, low-mileage sleeper with a mainstream engine and a clean title supports far more than a high-mileage unit with a rebuilt title.

Can I keep driving the truck?

Yes — that's the whole point of the structure. The lender records a lien on the title as security, but you keep possession and keep the unit in revenue service. Expect to maintain insurance naming the lender as lienholder, and expect most lenders to require ACH payments and a GPS unit.

Can I get one with bad credit?

Often, yes. Because the loan is secured by a titled asset the lender can value and recover, credit carries less weight than on an unsecured business loan. Lenders in this space do work with challenged credit, thin files, start-ups, and discharged bankruptcies. Credit still affects how much you can draw. An open bankruptcy, sizeable unresolved tax liens, or a rebuilt or salvaged title will stop a deal regardless of score.

What kinds of trucks qualify?

Titled commercial units with an active resale market: sleeper and day cab tractors, box and straight trucks, dump trucks, tow trucks and wreckers, service and vacuum trucks, and in many cases trailers and reefers. Rebuilt or salvage titles, unclear ownership, and units with no dependable resale market are generally excluded.

How much can I get for a title loan on a semi truck?

A percentage of the truck's liquidation value instead of its retail price, which is why the figure as a rule comes in below what owners expect. Year, mileage, engine make and model, condition, maintenance records, and whether the title is clean and lien-free all move it. A newer low-mileage sleeper with a mainstream drivetrain supports substantially more than a high-mileage unit with a hard-to-place engine.

Do I have to give up my truck during a commercial title loan?

No. You keep possession and keep the truck in revenue service for the whole term. The lender records a lien against the title as security only. Expect to maintain insurance naming them as lienholder, and expect most lenders to require ACH payments and a GPS unit on the vehicle.

Can I get a title loan on a truck I am still paying for?

Only if there is real equity. The usual structure is a cash-out refinance that pays off the existing lender and returns the surplus to you, which requires the truck to be worth meaningfully more than the payoff. If you owe close to what it's worth, there is nothing to draw against and the productive conversation is about the payment instead.

What paperwork do I need for a commercial truck title loan?

The title, VIN, and current registration; year, make, model, mileage, engine and transmission details; photos and any maintenance records; proof of insurance; recent business bank statements, usually three months; and a current payoff quote if a lender is still on the title. Having the title physically in hand is the single biggest determinant of speed.
