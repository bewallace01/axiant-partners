Avoid Payment Delays from Change Orders: Contractor Checklist

A practical change-order payment checklist for U.S. contractors to reduce draw delays, dispute risk, and financing stress.

Avoid Payment Delays from Change Orders: Contractor Checklist
← Back to Construction Financing

Updated March 31, 2026

Quick answer

Change orders slow payment when scope is agreed verbally and priced later: the cost hits payroll and suppliers weeks before the approval clears. Document scope, price and sign-off before the crew mobilizes, and keep the undisputed base contract billing on its own schedule. Underwriters read the same paperwork, and denials usually follow a pattern of loose documentation rather than one catastrophic event.

1. Why This Pain Point Shows Up in U.S. Contracting

Contractors in the U.S. operate inside a cash cycle that is naturally uneven. Labor, insurance, fuel, permits, mobilization costs, and supplier deposits are often due before project cash arrives. Even healthy companies can feel squeezed when multiple jobs overlap and draw timing drifts. The issue gets worse when change orders, inspections, and owner approvals stretch beyond expected dates. Lenders understand this pattern, but they still need evidence that your business manages timing risk instead of reacting to it. If your file looks disorganized, approvals slow down. If your file shows predictable controls, you usually get stronger options. In real-world contractor workflows, this usually means coordinating project managers, accounting, and owners around one shared view of timing risk. When these teams use different assumptions, the financing file becomes inconsistent and lenders lose confidence. A disciplined cadence of weekly cash review, draw tracking, and document hygiene materially improves outcomes without adding unnecessary overhead.

Keep your language specific when talking to lenders. Avoid broad statements like "we need flexibility" and instead provide measurable details: amount needed, timing window, repayment source, and contingency plan if collections slip. This level of clarity helps underwriters move from "pending" to "approved" faster. It also helps you compare offers accurately, because you can evaluate whether structure, cost, and covenants truly fit your operating model.

2. How Lenders and Underwriters Read the Situation

Underwriters do not just ask whether your revenue is growing. They look at the shape of your revenue, the quality of your reporting, and whether debt obligations fit your operating rhythm. In contractor files, they often compare bank statement inflows to payroll cadence, AP pressure, and debt service patterns. They also evaluate concentration risk: one or two large customers can be positive for backlog but risky if payment timing is volatile. When the story is clear, underwriters can defend the approval internally. When the story is fuzzy, they request more documents, reduce limits, or decline. 3. Operational Mistakes That Turn Pressure into Denials

Most denials are not caused by a single catastrophic event. They are caused by repeated avoidable mistakes: applying late, mixing business and personal cash movement, poor follow-up on documentation requests, and borrowing structures that do not match the job cycle. Another frequent mistake is treating financing as a rescue tool instead of a planned operating tool. When a contractor only applies once payroll is in danger, lenders assume elevated risk and pricing often moves against the borrower. Strong operators apply earlier, size correctly, and preserve optionality. 4. Financial Controls Contractors Should Put in Place

You do not need a large accounting department to present a fundable file. You need consistency. Keep monthly financials current, standardize your job-cost reporting, and maintain a short narrative for each major funding request that explains use of funds and repayment source. Reconcile accounts quickly and avoid unexplained owner draws during tight periods. Build a rolling 13-week cash forecast that includes payroll dates, known supplier obligations, and expected draw receipts. This single practice improves both internal decision-making and lender confidence. 5. Practical Checklist Before You Apply

Before submitting any application, run a checklist. Confirm your legal entity records are current. Prepare clean bank statements and recent P&L and balance sheet reports. Build a concise explanation of why funds are needed now, how amount was calculated, and when repayment events occur. Identify whether your need is recurring or one-time. Recurring needs usually fit a line structure; one-time project gaps often fit targeted working capital. If equipment is involved, include equipment details and expected utilization.
