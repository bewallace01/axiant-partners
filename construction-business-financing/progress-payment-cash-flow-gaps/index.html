Contractor Cash Flow Between Draws: 7 Things Stopping You (and Fixes)

Progress payments are predictable. So are the cash flow gaps. Here’s how contractors stay liquid between draws.

Contractor Cash Flow Between Draws: 7 Things Stopping You (and Fixes)
← Back to Construction Financing

Updated April 10, 2026

Contractors usually go cash-negative between draws because payroll and materials are paid weekly while progress payments arrive later and often include retainage. The same construction-company cash-flow squeeze opens up between jobs, too — the payment timing on one project’s final draw and retainage often lands weeks after the next job has already started pulling cash. The fastest fixes are matching financing to timing gaps (line of credit for recurring gaps, project-sized working capital for one-off gaps) and tightening billing/change-order workflows. FAQ Quick Hits

Why do contractors run out of cash between draws?

Contractors pay for labor, materials, subs, and mobilization before progress payments arrive. Retainage, slow approvals, change-order delays, and mismatched payment terms create predictable cash flow gaps between draws.

What is the best financing for cash flow between progress payments?

The best option depends on your situation. Many contractors use a business line of credit for recurring gaps, working capital loans for specific projects, and equipment financing to avoid draining cash on machinery purchases. The key is matching terms to your draw schedule.

How do contractors cover payroll while waiting on a draw?

Common approaches include a line of credit, short-term working capital sized to payroll cycles, and improving billing/collections speed. Contractors also stabilize cash flow by aligning payables with draw timing and keeping a cash buffer.

How does retainage affect contractor cash flow?

Retainage withholds a portion of each payment until completion, which reduces available cash during the project. On larger jobs, retainage can create a major working capital gap that needs to be planned for in pricing and financing.

Does equipment financing help contractor cash flow?

Often, yes. Financing equipment spreads costs over the equipment’s useful life and preserves working capital for payroll and materials. Many contractors use equipment financing instead of paying cash for excavators, skid steers, or trucks.

Can retainage alone cause cash flow problems on profitable jobs?

Yes. Even profitable projects can run tight when 5-10% retainage is withheld across multiple active jobs.

Is a line of credit or short-term loan better between draws?

A line often fits recurring monthly gaps, while short-term/project financing can fit one large temporary gap with a clear payoff event.

Draw-Cycle Execution Framework for Contractors

Most construction cash issues are execution mismatches, not profitability failures. Costs move weekly while pay applications move on slower approval cycles, and small process gaps compound into major cash pressure. High-performing contractors manage this with a draw-cycle operating model: pre-billed milestones, submission calendars, and disciplined change-order controls tied to funding triggers.

A practical framework is to map every active job into one of three states: front-loaded (mobilization-heavy), steady-state (predictable draw cadence), or closeout (retainage-heavy). Each state should have a specific cash strategy. Front-loaded jobs need short-cycle liquidity and supplier term alignment. Steady-state jobs need tight billing discipline. Closeout jobs need retainage forecasting and negotiated release milestones.

  • Billing control: submit pay apps on a fixed internal deadline before owner deadlines
  • CO discipline: avoid self-funding change work without signed authorization path
  • Vendor strategy: align supplier terms to expected draw timing where possible
  • Liquidity rule: keep dedicated buffer for payroll-to-draw timing gaps

This guide is designed to be practical. You’ll see the most common reasons contractors go cash-negative between progress payments, how to diagnose which one is hitting you, and the financing and operational fixes that align with how construction actually gets paid.

Why contractor cash flow breaks between progress payments

Construction cash flow is “front-loaded.” The costs show up early (labor, materials, subs, equipment), while revenue is delayed (billing cycles, inspections, approvals, pay apps, retainage). Even when the job is profitable, timing alone can create a cash crunch.

Most contractors experience cash gaps in three predictable windows:

  • Mobilization to first draw: payroll and materials start immediately, but the first payment may be 30—60+ days out.
  • Between draws: costs remain weekly, but cash arrives monthly (or slower).
  • Closeout: retainage and punch list delays hold back final cash.

Quick self-diagnosis: which gap is hitting you?

Before picking a financing product, identify the real gap. These are the most common patterns:

  • Payroll gap: you’re paying labor weekly but getting paid monthly.
  • Materials gap: supplier terms are tighter than your owner/GC payment terms.
  • Retainage gap: 5—10% withheld creates a slow bleed on bigger jobs.
  • Change-order gap: you’re performing work before it’s signed/approved/paid.
  • Growth gap: you’re winning more work than your balance sheet can float.

Once you identify the gap, the “right” fix becomes clearer.

1) Mobilization costs before the first draw

The first draw is the most dangerous part of a job. You might have 2—6 weeks of payroll before the first pay app is approved. Add materials deposits, equipment rentals, permits, and the job can go cash-negative fast.

What stops you: Contractors often price the job for profit, but not for float. The job is profitable at the end, but the company can’t carry the early costs.

Fixes that work:

  • Structure the contract for mobilization: negotiate a mobilization payment or materials deposit when possible.
  • Use a line of credit for recurring gaps: a revolving line is designed for “in and out” funding needs.
  • Use working capital for a specific project: when the gap is tied to one job, a project-sized working capital loan can fit.

See working capital loans and business lines of credit for product-level details.

2) Payroll is weekly; draws are monthly

Payroll is the #1 reason contractors take bad financing. You can delay some vendor payments. You can’t delay payroll without risking crews, productivity, and compliance.

What stops you: the mismatch between weekly outflows and monthly inflows. Even a “healthy” gross margin doesn’t help if the timing is wrong.

Fixes that work:

  • Right-size your payment: avoid taking a 12-month loan to solve a 45-day gap. Match term to reality.
  • Build a payroll buffer: a dedicated reserve reduces dependence on expensive short-term products.
  • Use a revolving line for recurring payroll gaps: draw, pay payroll, repay when the draw hits.

Contractor-specific guidance: see line of credit for contractors. Subcontractors who invoice the GC have different cash flow patterns; see working capital for subcontractors between invoices.

3) Materials and supplier terms don’t match your pay terms

Materials can break cash flow even when you’re “busy.” If suppliers require payment on delivery (or net-10/net-15) but the owner pays net-30+ after approval, you’re floating the job.

What stops you: materials timing + volume. The bigger the job, the bigger the early materials hit.

Fixes that work:

  • Negotiate supplier terms: even moving from net-10 to net-30 changes the cash curve.
  • Schedule procurement to match billing milestones: avoid buying everything at once if staging is possible.
  • Use working capital for materials-heavy phases: especially when the gap is predictable for a single project.

4) Retainage creates a slow bleed

Retainage is a quiet cash flow killer. When 5—10% is held back on every draw, you’re effectively lending money to the project until closeout. On larger jobs, retainage can represent months of payroll.

What stops you: Many contractors don’t price for retainage or they scale up and discover retainage is tying up working capital across multiple jobs at once.

Fixes that work:

  • Price the float: retainage impacts cash flow even if profit is fine.
  • Track retainage across all jobs: treat it like a receivable with expected release dates.
  • Use recurring liquidity tools: for ongoing retainage float, a revolving line is often a better match than one-off debt.

5) Change orders get performed before they get paid

Change orders are where contractors lose both profit and cash flow. The work happens now. The paperwork and approval happen later. Payment can lag even further.

What stops you: A crew is on site; you don’t want delays. So you perform change work and hope the paperwork catches up. That can create a “free financing” problem for the owner/GC.

Fixes that work:

  • Institute a change-order discipline: even a simple policy (signed T&M ticket or email approval) helps.
  • Bill change work clearly and fast: reduce the lag between work and pay app inclusion.
  • Don’t finance bad process with expensive debt: fix the paperwork timing first, then use financing as needed.

6) You bought equipment with cash (and broke your working capital)

Buying equipment with cash feels responsible until it empties your reserves. Many contractors end up cash-poor with a paid-off machine. That’s the worst trade when payroll and materials are due weekly.

What stops you: Cash equipment purchases reduce your ability to float projects. Then you’re forced to use high-cost short-term financing for payroll.

Fixes that work:

  • Use equipment financing for equipment: spread the cost over the useful life. Preserve cash for operations.
  • Match term to the asset: excavators, skid steers, and trucks often finance 36—84 months depending on age and profile.
  • Don’t mix equipment and working capital in one request: finance the asset with an asset-backed product; keep liquidity tools for cash flow.

See equipment financing and the construction equipment guide construction & heavy equipment financing.

7) Growth outpaces your cash conversion cycle

Winning more work is great—until you can’t fund it. Many contractors fail at the moment they “level up” into bigger jobs. The reason is usually working capital, not skill.

What stops you: Your working capital needs scale faster than your balance sheet. A single new crew means more payroll, more tools, more materials, and bigger float between draws.

Fixes that work:

  • Forecast cash weekly: not monthly. Weekly forecasting catches shortfalls early.
  • Right-size jobs to liquidity: take bigger jobs when you have the capital to float them.
  • Use structured liquidity: a line of credit for recurring gaps, working capital for project spikes, and equipment financing for machines.

Which financing options fit “between draws” cash flow?

Contractors tend to get into trouble when they pick the wrong tool. Here’s a simple mapping:

Common contractor scenarios (and the best-fit fix)

Here are real-world patterns that show up across trades. Use these as a quick “this is me” matcher.

You’re profitable, but the account hits zero before every draw

This is usually a timing and buffer problem. Your margins may be fine, but you’re operating with no cushion. One late pay app approval and you’re scrambling.

  • Fast fix: stabilize the cash buffer and reduce “deposit then drain” weeks.
  • Financing fit: a line of credit sized to your recurring gap tends to fit better than stacking short-term loans.

You have one big job that’s starving everything else

One large contract can consume working capital across your whole company. Payroll and materials for that job drain the account, then smaller jobs suffer because you can’t buy materials or keep crews staffed.

  • Fast fix: separate the project cash plan and track weekly burn vs expected draws.
  • Financing fit: project-sized working capital, plus disciplined billing and change-order control.

You’re buying materials earlier than you’re billing for them

This is common when procurement happens up front but billing is milestone-based. The result is a cash gap that looks like “we’re busy but broke.”

  • Fast fix: align procurement to billing milestones and negotiate supplier terms.
  • Financing fit: working capital for the materials-heavy phase.

Retainage is building up across multiple jobs

Retainage is manageable on one project. It becomes dangerous when you have five jobs running and each one is withholding 5—10%. That cash is “earned” but not usable.

  • Fast fix: track retainage as a separate receivable with release dates and closeout tasks.
  • Financing fit: revolving liquidity (line of credit) is often more practical than one-off debt.

How to stop the cash gap from repeating (process upgrades)

Financing keeps you liquid. Process keeps you from needing expensive liquidity. These upgrades often deliver the fastest permanent improvement:

  • Weekly cash forecasting: Forecast receipts and outflows weekly, not monthly. Contractors get hurt in the weeks between draws.
  • Clean pay apps: Incomplete pay apps create avoidable delays. Use a checklist so you aren’t missing backup, lien waivers, or approvals.
  • Change-order discipline: If you do change work without a signed path to payment, you are financing the project for the owner/GC.
  • Separate equipment from working capital: Financing equipment with cash creates operational stress. Use equipment financing so your cash stays available for payroll and materials.
  • Build a minimum cash buffer: Even a small buffer changes everything. It reduces NSFs, reduces panic decisions, and improves lender terms.

What lenders look for (when contractors apply for cash-flow financing)

Contractor approvals are usually driven by a few simple signals. Knowing them helps you package your file and avoid delays:

  • Deposit stability: consistent deposits and fewer “zero weeks” improve confidence.
  • Bank statement cleanliness: NSFs/overdrafts and thin balances commonly reduce approvals or increase cost.
  • Existing obligations: heavy daily/weekly debits and multiple liens can restrict options.
  • Use-of-funds clarity: “Payroll between draws” is clear; “general needs” is less clear.

Contractor cash flow checklist (before you borrow)

Financing helps, but the fastest improvement often comes from tightening the process around draws.

  1. Know your draw schedule: dates, approval steps, and typical lag.
  2. Track retainage separately: treat it like a receivable with release dates.
  3. Speed up pay apps: submit clean, complete pay apps early.
  4. Control change orders: don’t let change work become unbilled work.
  5. Match financing to the gap: revolving for recurring; term for project spikes; asset-backed for equipment.

Final Thoughts

Cash flow between draws is a timing problem—and timing problems need timing-aligned solutions. The goal isn’t to “borrow more.” The goal is to keep your business liquid while you grow: payroll covered, materials purchased, and crews working without panic financing.
