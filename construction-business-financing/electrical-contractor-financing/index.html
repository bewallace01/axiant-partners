Electrical Contractor Financing and Funding: Rates, Terms and Options

Payroll weekly, pay apps monthly, retainage at closeout - which funding fits service work, project work and new vans, and what each typically costs

← Back to Construction Business Financing Articles

Updated October 27, 2026

Quick answer

Electrical contractors usually fund two different needs two different ways. Payroll and materials between pay apps go on a line of credit (commonly about 8-13% APR at banks, 14-30%+ online) or invoice factoring (advances of commonly 80-95% of approved invoices). Vans and bucket trucks go on equipment loans, commonly about 6-15% over 36-72 months. These are typical ranges across lenders, not a quote.

Service Work and Project Work Need Different Money

Service work (repairs, panel upgrades, maintenance contracts) turns fast. Tickets are small, customers pay within days or weeks, and the cash need is a steady float for parts and payroll. A revolving line fits because the same small gap opens and closes every week.

Project work (new construction, tenant improvements, commercial fit-outs) turns slowly. You buy wire, gear and fixtures up front, bill a monthly pay application, wait for the general contractor to be paid by the owner, and then have part of every payment held back as retainage. The gap is larger, longer and tied to specific jobs.

Lenders judge service revenue on deposit consistency and project revenue on backlog and receivables concentration, so show the two streams separately. For the project gap, see cash flow gaps between progress payments.

Retainage, Pay Apps and Federal Work

Retainage is the share of each progress payment held until the job is complete, commonly 5-10%. On one job it is a nuisance. Across several jobs running at once it becomes a large balance you have earned but cannot spend, released only at closeout and sometimes later. Our retainage guide covers how to price and plan for it.

Federal jobs follow fixed timing rules. Under FAR 52.232-27, a federal agency's construction progress payment is due 14 days after it receives a proper payment request, and the prime contractor must pay its subcontractors within 7 days of being paid. Federal construction contracts over $150,000 also require payment bonds under the Miller Act, which gives unpaid subs and suppliers a claim against the bond rather than the project. Private and state work follows state prompt-payment and lien laws, which vary.

Factoring and lines of credit both work around pay-app timing, but check two things first: whether your subcontract prohibits assigning receivables, and whether a blanket UCC filing from an earlier lender already covers them. Subcontractors waiting on a general contractor should also read working capital for subcontractors between invoices.

Vans, Bucket Trucks and Field Equipment

Vehicles are the largest asset most electrical firms buy, and they should be financed as assets, over their working life, not out of the operating line.

  • Cargo and service vans commonly run about $35,000-$75,000 before upfit. Used vans often trade 25-45% below comparable new pricing. See cargo van financing.
  • Bucket trucks commonly run $100,000-$200,000 or more, with the chassis and aerial device financed together. Used units commonly cost 30-50% less. See bucket truck financing.

Equipment financed this way is generally depreciable, and Section 179 may let you deduct much of the cost in the first year; check IRS Publication 946 and your accountant for the current limits.

Worked Example: A Commercial Fit-Out

Illustrative numbers, not a quote. An electrical subcontractor wins a five-month commercial fit-out with monthly pay applications of $80,000 and 10% retainage. Field payroll runs $18,000 a week.

  • The first pay app goes in at the end of month one. If the general contractor pays about 30 days later, the firm carries roughly eight weeks of payroll, about $144,000, before any money arrives, plus the material buys.
  • Each payment arrives as $72,000, because $8,000 is held as retainage. By the last pay app, $40,000 is held and is not released until closeout.

A sensible structure here is a line of credit sized to the payroll gap, drawn as the job ramps and paid down as pay apps clear, or factoring on approved pay apps if the line is too small.

What Lenders Check in an Electrical File

  • Bank statements, usually 3-6 months, for deposit consistency and existing payments.
  • AR aging and backlog, including who your general contractors are and how much of your receivables sit with one of them.
  • A debt schedule covering vehicle notes, equipment loans and any merchant cash advances.
  • License and insurance in good standing, which lenders read as a sign jobs will keep closing and paying.
  • A specific use of funds, tied to jobs, hires or vehicles, rather than "working capital" alone.

Apply before the squeeze shows; the contractor financing mistakes guide lists the most common delays.

How to Choose and Apply

  1. Name the bottleneck. A recurring timing gap, a one-off project ramp and a vehicle purchase are three different loans.
  2. Size it from your numbers. Use weekly payroll, your average days to payment and the retainage you are carrying, not a round figure.
  3. Check your contracts for assignment clauses before counting on factoring.
  4. Gather the file: bank statements, AR aging, backlog, debt schedule and quotes for any vehicles.
  5. Compare total cost, including fees, guarantees and prepayment terms, not just the rate.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best funding for an electrical contractor?

It depends on the need. A line of credit usually fits recurring payroll and material gaps, invoice factoring fits slow commercial invoices, a working capital loan fits a one-off project ramp, and equipment financing fits vans, bucket trucks and field equipment.

What interest rate do electrical contractors pay?

Commonly about 8-13% APR on bank lines of credit and 14-30%+ online, and about 6-15% on van and bucket truck loans. Factoring is priced as a fee of about 1-5% of invoice value. These are typical ranges across lenders, not a quote.

Can an electrical subcontractor factor pay applications?

Often, if the pay app is approved and the subcontract does not prohibit assigning receivables. Funders commonly advance 80-95% and look mostly at the general contractor's and owner's ability to pay.

How does retainage affect an electrician's cash flow?

Retainage holds back commonly 5-10% of each progress payment until the job is complete. Across several jobs it becomes a large balance you have earned but cannot use, so plan financing around it.

How fast must a federal prime contractor pay an electrical sub?

Under FAR 52.232-27, within 7 days of receiving payment from the agency. The agency's own progress payment is due 14 days after it receives a proper payment request.

Can electrical contractors finance bucket trucks and vans?

Yes. Vans and bucket trucks are commonly financed over about 24-72 months with 0-20% down, and the bucket truck's chassis and aerial device are financed together.
