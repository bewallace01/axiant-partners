Quick answer

Asset financing is a loan or lease used to buy a business asset, such as equipment, a truck or technology, where the asset itself secures the deal. Rates follow your credit: about 7-9% APR at 720+ with 2+ years in business, 10-13% at 680-719, 13-16% at 640-679 and 16-22% at 580-639, over 2-7 years with 0-25% down. Approvals usually take 24-72 hours and funding 5-10 business days. Borrowing against assets you already own, like receivables or inventory, is asset-based lending, a different product.

How does asset financing work?

You pick the asset and get a quote or invoice from the seller. The lender pays the seller, and you pay the lender back in fixed monthly payments. With a loan, the asset is yours from day one. With a lease, the lender owns it, and at the end you decide whether to buy it, return it or renew. Either way the asset is the collateral, so lenders care about what it's worth and what it will earn you, not only about your credit score.

The wider category, sometimes called corporate and asset finance, also covers asset-based lending, working capital and commercial real estate. If you already know which one you need, go straight to it below.

The four types of asset and corporate finance

1. Equipment finance

Loans and leases to buy machinery, trucks, technology or other equipment, secured by the equipment. Approvals often come in 24-72 hours and terms run 24-84 months. See equipment financing and leasing →

2. Asset-based lending (ABL)

Borrowing against assets you already own (receivables, inventory or equipment) to free up cash. The line grows with the value of the collateral rather than resting only on your credit, which helps asset-rich, cash-tight companies. How asset-based lending works →

3. Working capital

Short- to medium-term money for payroll, materials, inventory and the gaps between getting paid, as a term loan, line of credit or revenue-based financing. Explore working capital options →

4. Commercial real estate

Loans to buy, build or refinance owner-occupied or investment property: SBA 504 and 7(a), conventional and bridge. Longer terms, larger amounts, and underwriting that leans on the property's cash flow (DSCR). Commercial real estate loans →

Asset finance vs. asset-based lending: what's the difference?

People use the two terms interchangeably, but they point in opposite directions:

  • Asset finance buys a new asset. You borrow or lease to get equipment or a vehicle, and that new asset secures the deal.
  • Asset-based lending raises cash from assets you own. A lender advances around 80-85% of eligible receivables and often about 50% of eligible inventory, recalculated as those balances change.

Plenty of businesses use both: asset finance for the equipment that earns revenue, then asset-based lending or a line of credit to smooth cash flow in between. Not sure which fits? Get matched →

What assets can you finance?

Most hard assets with resale value and a useful life can be financed, plus the current assets that asset-based lending borrows against:

  • Equipment and machinery: construction, manufacturing, medical, restaurant, agricultural.
  • Commercial vehicles and trucks: from a single box truck to a full fleet.
  • Technology and software: hardware, systems and some software.
  • Commercial real estate: owner-occupied and investment property.
  • Receivables and inventory: through asset-based lending or invoice financing.

Is asset finance a good idea?

It usually is when the asset makes money or saves it. A new truck that adds a route, or a machine that takes work in-house, can cover its own payment. The reasons it works:

  • Your cash stays in the business. With 0-10% down on good credit, you keep reserves for payroll and slow months.
  • The payment is fixed and matched to how long the asset lasts.
  • Approval is easier because the asset secures the loan, so lenders can say yes to files a bank would decline.

The trade-offs are real too. The lender can repossess the asset if you stop paying, lower credit tiers pay 16-22% with 15-25% down, and a term longer than the asset's working life leaves you paying for something you've stopped using. If you need cash rather than a new asset, look at working capital or asset-based lending instead.

Who qualifies for asset financing?

Most established U.S. businesses qualify for at least one product. Equipment finance is the easiest to get (600+ opens most lenders and decisions are fast), SBA-backed options want 650-680+ and take longer, and asset-based lines depend on the quality of your receivables and inventory. Asset financing FAQs

What is asset financing?

A loan or lease used to buy a business asset, such as equipment, a vehicle or technology, where that asset secures the deal. If payments stop, the lender can take the asset back and sell it, so lenders look at what you're buying as well as your credit. That's why it usually closes faster than a bank or SBA loan.

What assets can a business finance?

Equipment and machinery, commercial vehicles and trucks, technology and some software, and commercial real estate. Receivables and inventory you already hold can be borrowed against through asset-based lending or invoice financing. Most hard assets with resale value and a useful life qualify.

What are typical asset financing rates?

On equipment, about 7-9% APR with 720+ credit and 2+ years in business, 10-13% at 680-719, 13-16% at 640-679 and 16-22% at 580-639. Terms run 2-7 years, longer for stronger credit, and down payments 0-25%.

Is asset finance a good idea?

It usually is when the asset earns money or saves it, because the asset secures the loan, the payment is fixed and your cash stays in the business. The trade-offs: the lender can repossess the asset if you default, and a loan longer than the asset's working life leaves you paying for something you no longer use.

What is the difference between asset finance and asset-based lending?

Asset finance pays for a new asset, and that asset secures the loan. Asset-based lending borrows against assets you already own, usually around 80-85% of eligible receivables and often about 50% of eligible inventory, to free up cash.

What are examples of long-term asset financing?

Commercial real estate loans and SBA 504 loans, which run up to 25 years, are the long end. Equipment loans and leases are medium-term at 2-7 years, matched to how long the equipment lasts.
