USDA Business Loans: How B&I Guaranteed Loans Work for Rural Businesses

Up to $25 million, terms to 40 years, for buildings, equipment, acquisitions, hotels and working capital outside the big cities. What qualifies and what lenders ask for

← Back to SBA Loans

Updated November 13, 2026

Quick answer

A USDA Business & Industry (B&I) loan is a bank loan with a USDA guarantee for businesses in rural areas, generally outside towns of more than 50,000 people. It can pay for real estate, buildings, equipment, inventory, acquisitions and working capital, up to $25 million per borrower. Lenders commonly lend up to about 80% of collateral value, and their appetite for size varies. Axiant Partners, a broker in Boca Raton, matches rural projects with B&I lenders. These are program rules and typical ranges, not a quote.

How a B&I Loan Works

USDA doesn't lend the money. A bank, credit union or other approved lender makes the loan, and USDA Rural Development guarantees part of it against loss. Under the FY2026 annual notice the guarantee is 85% on loans under $5 million and 80% from $5 million to $25 million. The cap is $25 million per borrower, counting any B&I balance you already have.

The guarantee is what lets a lender go bigger and longer than it would on its own. Terms can run to the useful life of what you're financing, up to 40 years, and the loan must fully amortize with no balloon. The rate is negotiated with the lender, with no USDA rate sheet. For the numbers, see USDA B&I rates, fees and terms.

Where It Works: The Rural Test

The project has to be in a rural area: not in a city or town of more than 50,000 people and not in the urbanized area attached to one, by the latest census. That rules out most suburbs but leaves in thousands of county seats, highway towns and resort areas, including some fairly large towns that stand alone.

Check the exact address on the USDA eligibility map before anything else. USDA eligible areas walks through the map and the narrow exceptions.

Equity, Collateral and Who Can Borrow

The rule sets minimum equity at loan closing. A borrower meets one test in its row:

BorrowerBalance sheet equityOr cash into the project
Existing business10% (or 9:1 debt to equity)10% of eligible project cost
New business, sales contracts10%10%
New business, pre-completion25% (or 3:1)25%
Any other new business20% (or 4:1)25%

USDA can raise these for riskier loans. Beyond the rule, these are common lender practice:

  • Collateral. Lenders discount appraised values and commonly lend up to about 80% of collateral value; personal guarantees are standard. Going-concern value comes out of the appraisal before discounting (5001.203).
  • Cash flow. Lenders want history that covers the new payment, often at 1.25 times or better.
  • Feasibility study. Required for a new business borrowing over $1 million (5001.306), and possible on other loans.
  • Who can borrow. Companies, cooperatives, tribes, public bodies and individuals. An individual must be a U.S. citizen or a green-card holder (5001.126), and the collateral must be fixed assets in the U.S.

Worked Example: A Rural Plant Expansion

Illustrative, not a quote or an offer. A food processor that has run for years in a town of about 9,000 adds a building ($3,800,000), equipment ($1,900,000) and permanent working capital ($500,000). It meets the existing-business test by putting in 10% of project cost. Fees use the FY2026 B&I rates.

A real lender would likely set a longer term on the building and shorter ones on the equipment and working capital, so the payment above is a blend. How to Apply

  1. Confirm the address is rural on USDA's map.
  2. Find a lender that does B&I. Fewer banks use it than SBA, and appetite for size, industry and hotels varies.
  3. Build the file: three years of financials, interim statements, a debt schedule, the project budget, and a pro forma balance sheet as of closing.
  4. The lender applies to USDA. Expect appraisals, an environmental review and, if needed, a feasibility study.
  5. Conditional commitment, then closing. USDA issues a conditional commitment, the loan closes, and the loan note guarantee follows once the conditions are met.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a USDA business loan?

Usually it means a Business & Industry guaranteed loan: a loan from a bank or other lender, partly guaranteed by USDA Rural Development, for a business in a rural area.

What is the maximum USDA B&amp;I loan?

$25 million per borrower, including existing B&I balances. Cooperatives doing value-added agricultural processing can go higher with the Secretary's approval.

Does my town have to be under 50,000 people?

The project must be outside any city or town of more than 50,000 people and outside the urbanized area attached to it. Check the address on USDA's eligibility map.

Can a USDA B&amp;I loan buy a business?

Yes, if it's an entire business, not a partial stock purchase, and jobs are created or saved. Buyouts between existing owners qualify under conditions in the rule.

How much do I need to put down?

The rule's minimum is 10% for an existing business and 20-25% for most new ones, by the tests in 7 CFR 5001.105(d). Lenders may ask more.

Can USDA B&amp;I fund working capital?

Yes, as a permanent working capital term loan. Lines of credit are not eligible.
