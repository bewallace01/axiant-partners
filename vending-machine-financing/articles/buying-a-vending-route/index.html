How to Finance Buying a Vending Route

What you are really buying, what to verify before you borrow, and how route purchases are structured

← Back to Vending Machine Financing Articles

Updated October 26, 2026

Quick answer

A vending route is machines plus the location contracts and cash flow that come with them, so it is financed as a business acquisition, not just equipment. Common structures are an SBA 7(a) loan (with at least 10% equity injection for a change of ownership), an equipment loan on the machines plus a seller note, or both. What You Are Actually Buying

The asking price for a route is usually well above what the machines would sell for on their own. The difference is what you are paying for the placements and the revenue they produce:

Part of the routeWhat it is worth to a lender
MachinesLiquidation value; can secure an equipment loan
Location agreementsThe revenue, if they transfer to you
GoodwillNo collateral value; needs SBA or seller financing
InventorySmall; usually bought at cost
VehicleTitled; financed separately if included

Buyer and seller report the price split between equipment, goodwill and other assets to the IRS on Form 8594. The split matters to you twice: it decides what you can depreciate, and it shows a lender how much of the price has collateral behind it.

Verify Before You Borrow

A lender will check these, and you should check them first:

  • Sales by machine. Telemetry or card-reader reports per machine and location. A route sold on the seller's word is very hard to finance.
  • Deposits that match. Vending still takes cash. Bank deposits and tax returns should line up with the sales the seller reports.
  • Location agreements. Whether each one is in writing, how long it runs, whether it can be assigned to a new owner, and the commission. Handshake placements can walk away after a sale.
  • Liens on the machines. Vending machines have no title; a lender's lien is a UCC-1 filed with the secretary of state. A UCC search shows whether the seller's machines are already pledged.
  • Machine age and payment hardware. Older cash-only units may need card readers or replacement soon after you buy.

How Route Purchases Are Structured

  • SBA 7(a). The usual route for a purchase with meaningful goodwill. SBA's rules require an equity injection for a complete change of ownership - at least 10% of the project cost - and a seller note counts toward it only on full standby and only for part of it. See business acquisition financing.
  • Equipment loan plus seller note. An equipment lender finances part of the machines' liquidation value, and the seller carries the rest. Faster than SBA, but the equipment loan covers only the machines.
  • Seller financing alone. Common on small routes. Make sure the note's terms are written and that a UCC search shows no other lender on the machines.
  • Working capital. Keep some aside. Inventory, fuel and early repairs come before the first full month of sales.

A Worked Example

Illustrative arithmetic, not a quote. A buyer agrees $120,000 for a route of established machines with written, assignable location agreements:

StructureFigure
Purchase price$120,000
Equity injection (10%)$12,000
SBA 7(a) loan$108,000
Payment, 11.5% / 10 yrsAbout $1,518

The underwriting question is whether the route's verified cash flow, after product, commissions, fuel and the buyer's own pay, covers $1,518 comfortably. Closing costs and the SBA guarantee fee are extra.

When a Route Purchase Is Hard to Finance

  • Placements are verbal. Without written agreements, there is nothing to show the revenue will stay.
  • Sales are unverifiable. No telemetry, no matching deposits.
  • The price is mostly goodwill on a small route. Too small for SBA to be worth the paperwork, too little collateral for an equipment lender; seller financing is usually the answer.

After the purchase, growing the route is an equipment question again: see typical vending machine financing rates and micro-market financing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I get a loan to buy a vending route?

Yes. Routes are financed as small business acquisitions - commonly with an SBA 7(a) loan, an equipment loan on the machines plus a seller note, or seller financing alone on small routes.

How much do I need to put down to buy a vending route?

With an SBA 7(a) loan for a complete change of ownership, SBA requires an equity injection of at least 10% of the project cost. Other structures vary with the lender and the seller.

Can a seller note count toward my down payment?

Under SBA rules, only if the note is on full standby for the life of the SBA loan, and only for part of the required injection.

What should I check before buying a vending route?

Sales by machine, bank deposits that match, written and assignable location agreements, a UCC lien search on the machines, and the age of the machines and payment hardware.

Will an equipment loan cover the whole route price?

Rarely. An equipment lender lends against the machines' liquidation value, which is usually well below a route's price. The rest needs SBA financing, a seller note or your own cash.

Sources & Further Reading

  • SBA SOP 50 10: Lender and Development Company Loan Programs — SBA's operating rules for 7(a) loans, including the equity injection required when buying a business and when a seller note can count toward it.
  • IRS Form 8594: Asset Acquisition Statement — How buyer and seller of a business allocate the price between equipment, goodwill and other assets - which decides what can be depreciated.
  • UCC § 9-310: When Filing Is Required to Perfect a Security Interest — Vending machines carry no title; a lender records its lien by filing a UCC-1, which is what a lien search on a route turns up.
