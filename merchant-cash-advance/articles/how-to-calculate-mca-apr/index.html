How to Calculate the Real APR of Your MCA (Factor Rate to APR)

A factor rate hides the true cost of an advance. Here's how to convert your factor rate and payback term into an effective APR — with the formula, a worked example, and why fast repayment makes it worse

MCAs are quoted in factor rates, not APRs—and that's exactly why so many owners underestimate the cost. A "1.3 factor" sounds like 30%. The true annualized cost is usually multiples of that. This page shows you how to convert your own factor rate and term into an effective APR so you can compare the advance honestly against a term loan or line of credit. Factor Rate vs APR

A factor rate is a flat multiplier applied to the amount funded. Borrow $100,000 at a 1.30 factor and you owe $130,000—period. It doesn't accrue or change with time. An APR (annual percentage rate) is the cost expressed as an annualized interest rate that accounts for how long you take to repay. The disconnect is the whole problem: a factor rate ignores time, while an MCA is repaid in months, not years. Annualize a fixed cost over a short window and the APR balloons.

The Calculation, Step by Step

You need three inputs: the amount funded, the factor rate, and the payback period (how long until the advance is fully repaid). Then:

  1. Total payback = amount funded × factor rate
  2. Dollar cost = total payback − amount funded
  3. Simple cost rate = dollar cost ÷ amount funded
  4. Annualize: multiply the simple cost rate by (12 ÷ number of months in the payback period) for a rough effective annual rate

This gives a clear, conservative estimate. A precise APR accounts for the fact that you're paying down the balance throughout (which pushes the true APR even higher than this simple annualization), but the quick method is enough to see the order of magnitude.

Same $30,000 cost, same 1.30 factor—but the effective APR roughly triples as the payback window shrinks. (These are the simple annualized figures; the precise APR, accounting for the declining balance, runs higher still.) That's the counterintuitive truth of MCAs: faster repayment makes the annualized cost worse, not better.

Why the Real APR Is So High

Two features stack up. First, the cost is fixed by the factor, not accrued over time—so there's no reduction for paying sooner. Second, the daily or weekly debit structure means you repay the whole thing in months. Annualizing a fixed dollar cost over a short period is what produces triple-digit APRs. And because most agreements don't reduce the factor for early payoff, the very speed that feels like progress is what pushes the effective rate up. This is the engine behind the MCA cycle.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you calculate the APR of a merchant cash advance?

Amount funded times the factor rate gives the total payback; total payback minus amount funded is the dollar cost. Divide the dollar cost by the amount funded for the simple cost rate, then annualize it over the actual payback period. Because MCAs repay quickly, annualizing turns a modest factor into a high effective APR.

What is the difference between a factor rate and an APR?

A factor rate is a flat multiplier (e.g. 1.30) applied to the amount funded to set the total payback; it doesn't change with repayment speed. An APR is an annualized rate that accounts for the timeline. Because an MCA repays fast, the same factor translates into a much higher APR than the factor number suggests.

Why is my MCA's real APR so high?

The cost is fixed by the factor, but you repay it over a short period through frequent debits. Annualizing a fixed dollar cost over a few months produces a high APR, and paying off early usually doesn't reduce the fixed factor — so fast repayment can raise the effective APR rather than lower it.

Does paying off an MCA early lower the cost?

Often not by much. Because the cost is a fixed factor rather than accruing interest, early payoff usually still owes most or all of the factor unless the agreement offers a specific early-payoff discount. Some funders do discount early payoffs, which helps when you refinance — always ask for a written payoff quote.

What APR should make me refinance an MCA?

There's no single threshold, but if your effective APR is well into the double or triple digits and you can qualify for a term loan or line of credit at a far lower rate, refinancing usually improves both cost and cash flow. Calculate your real APR, compare it to available refinance rates, and weigh any early-payoff discount.
