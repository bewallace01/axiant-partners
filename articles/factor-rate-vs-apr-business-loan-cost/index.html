Factor Rate: What It Is and What It Costs in APR

A 1.3 factor rate sounds cheaper than a 30% APR loan. Usually it costs more. Here is how to turn any factor rate into an APR you can compare before you sign.

Quick answer

A factor rate is the multiplier a merchant cash advance or short-term lender uses to set your total payback: a $50,000 advance at a 1.30 factor means you repay $65,000. The $15,000 fee is fixed, so the faster you repay, the higher the real cost. Paid back by daily debits, a 1.30 factor works out to about 55% APR over 12 months and 109% over 6 months. What is a factor rate?

A factor rate is a decimal multiplier, usually between 1.1 and 1.5, that tells you the total amount you'll repay. It's the pricing model used by merchant cash advances and many short-term working capital products. The size of the advance is a separate question, set mostly by your monthly card sales or deposits; see how much you can qualify for with a merchant cash advance.

The math is simple: advance × factor rate = total payback. Borrow $50,000 at a 1.3 factor rate and you repay $65,000. The $15,000 difference is the entire cost, fixed the day you sign. There's no separate interest accruing month to month, which is exactly what makes it feel cheaper than it is.

For a smaller example: $20,000 at a 1.30 factor means $26,000 back. Spread over 12 months of daily debits, that is about $103 every business day, or roughly $2,167 a month.

Factor rate vs. interest rate: what’s the difference?

APR (annual percentage rate) expresses cost as a yearly percentage, including fees. It's the standard for term loans, lines of credit, and SBA loans, and it's powerful for one reason: it accounts for time. A 30% cost paid back over three years is very different from a 30% cost paid back over six months, but a factor rate hides that difference. APR exposes it. Interest on a normal loan also shrinks as you pay the balance down; a factor-rate fee does not.

The trap: a factor rate ignores how fast you repay. The faster the payback, the higher the real cost, because you're paying the same fee for far less time with the money. A 1.3 factor over 6 months is roughly twice as expensive, annualized, as the same 1.3 factor over 12 months.

How do you convert a factor rate to APR?

Three steps, using a $50,000 advance at a 1.30 factor:

  1. Find the fee. Advance × (factor − 1). Here, $50,000 × 0.30 = $15,000.
  2. Get the simple rate. Fee ÷ advance ÷ years. Over 6 months: 30% ÷ 0.5 = 60%.
  3. Adjust for daily payments. Daily debits mean you hand the money back from day one, so on average you only have about half of it for the term. The true APR lands close to double the simple rate: about 109% over 6 months.

How to compare any two offers fairly

Convert everything to APR.

Ask each lender for the APR in writing. If they only quote a factor rate or a "cents on the dollar" cost, do the conversion yourself with the table above or the calculator below.

Compare total dollars, not the rate alone.

A higher APR on a small, short loan can cost fewer real dollars than a lower APR on a big, long one. Look at the total fee and the payment you'll actually make.

Account for payment frequency.

Daily and weekly debits hit cash flow harder than a monthly payment of the same monthly total.

Read the payoff terms.

With a factor rate, paying early usually doesn't save you anything unless the contract includes a discount. Confirm before signing.

When does a factor-rate product still make sense?

Higher cost doesn't always mean wrong choice. A factor-rate advance can be the right call when the money buys something worth more than the fee: filling a large order you couldn't otherwise fund, covering payroll to keep a contract, or bridging a genuine emergency when speed matters more than price. The danger is using expensive, fast money for ongoing needs that a cheaper line of credit or term loan should cover, or stacking advances until the daily payments choke cash flow.

A 30-second gut check before you sign

If you remember nothing else, run this quick test on any offer. Take the total of all payments you'll make, subtract the amount you're actually receiving, and you have the true dollar cost of the money. Divide that cost by the amount funded, then divide by the number of years (or fraction of a year) you'll have the funds. That's a rough annualized rate you can compare apples-to-apples against any APR quote. If a lender resists giving you the numbers to do this math, that reluctance is itself the answer. Cheap, transparent capital is happy to be measured; expensive money hides behind a friendly-sounding factor rate.

Frequently asked questions

What is a factor rate?

A factor rate is a decimal multiplier, commonly 1.1 to 1.5, that merchant cash advances and some short-term loans use to set total payback. Borrow $50,000 at a 1.3 factor rate and you repay $65,000. Unlike interest, the fee is fixed up front and usually does not shrink if you pay early.

Is a 1.3 factor rate the same as 30% APR?

No. A 1.3 factor is a 30% total fee, but you repay it in months, not years, and usually by daily debit. Over 12 months of daily payments that is about 55% APR, and over 6 months about 109%. The shorter the term, the higher the APR for the same factor.

How do I convert a factor rate to APR?

Multiply the advance by the factor minus 1 to get the fee, divide by the advance, then divide by the term in years for a simple rate. With daily payments, the true APR is close to double that simple rate: a 1.30 factor over 6 months is 60% simple and about 109% true APR. Always ask the lender for the APR in writing.

Why do lenders quote factor rates instead of APR?

A factor rate makes the cost look smaller: 1.3 sounds modest next to a 60% APR. Merchant cash advances are also structured as purchases of future revenue, not loans, so they are not always required to disclose an APR. That is why you should convert to APR yourself before comparing.

Does paying off a factor-rate loan early save money?

Usually not. The fee is fixed by the factor rate rather than accruing as interest, so paying early typically means you owe the same total, which makes the effective APR even higher. Some lenders offer early-payoff discounts, so ask before you sign.

How much does a $20,000 advance cost at a 1.3 factor rate?

You repay $26,000, so the cost is $6,000. Over 12 months of daily debits that is about $103 per business day and roughly 55% APR. The same $6,000 fee over 6 months is about 109% APR.
