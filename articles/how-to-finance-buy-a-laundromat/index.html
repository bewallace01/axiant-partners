Acquisition financing for coin and card laundromats

This guide is about buying an existing laundromat: how the purchase is structured, what a lender checks in a coin or card store, and how the lease and the machines shape the loan. For financing an equipment upgrade or a store you already own, see laundromat financing.

Important: Axiant Partners is not a lender; we connect businesses with financing sources. Offers depend on underwriting, program rules, and verification. This guide is educational only and not credit, legal, or tax advice.

Which Loan Fits Which Laundromat Deal

The deciding question is whether you are buying the building. Most laundromats operate in leased storefronts, so the price is mostly machines and goodwill, which is 7(a) territory. When the real estate is included, a 504 loan can carry the building on a long fixed rate while a 7(a) or the seller covers the rest.

A 7(a) loan can be as large as $5 million (13 CFR 120.151), well above most single-store purchases. Equipment financing is rarely the acquisition loan itself, but it is the usual way to replace machines after you own the store.

What a Lender Looks For in a Laundromat

A laundromat sells machine time, so underwriting comes down to three things: how much wash and dry volume the store really does, how long it can stay at its address, and how much life is left in the equipment. Stores with mostly attended drop-off or wash-and-fold service add labor, which lenders read like any service business; self-service stores with a part-time attendant read closer to an asset-heavy, low-labor operation. Either can be financed when the records support the price.

Coin, Card and Cash: Proving the Revenue

Coin-only stores are the hardest to underwrite because the seller’s cash count is the only record. Card and app payment systems leave a transaction trail, and many lenders give more weight to revenue they can tie to processor statements and bank deposits. Expect the underwriter to:

  • Match two to three years of tax returns against the store’s collection reports and deposits.
  • Compare water, gas and electric bills over the same months. Utility use should move with wash volume; a store reporting rising sales on flat water use invites questions.
  • Separate self-service revenue from wash-and-fold, dry cleaning drop-off and vending, which carry different margins.
  • Recast the seller’s cash flow with a market wage for the attendant hours the seller covered personally.

If the seller reports more cash than the records support, lenders usually size the loan on what can be documented, and the price has to come down or a seller note has to cover the gap.

Lease Length vs Loan Term

Under 13 CFR 120.212, SBA loans run up to 10 years unless they finance real estate or equipment with a longer useful life, with a 25-year maximum. For a leased-space laundromat that usually means a 10-year loan, and lenders commonly want the remaining lease plus renewal options to cover it. Before you sign a letter of intent, check:

  • Years left on the lease and the renewal options, and whether the landlord will consent to assignment.
  • Rent steps and who pays for water and sewer, which can be one of the largest bills.
  • Whether the landlord will sign a lender’s collateral access or landlord waiver letter for the machines.
  • Any exclusive-use clause that stops a competing laundromat opening in the same center.

Machine Age and the Replacement Reserve

Commercial washers and dryers wear out on a cycle, and a store whose machines are near the end of it will need a large capital outlay soon after closing. Ask for the purchase dates and model list, get an independent condition report, and price the replacement before you agree on a price. Lenders handle it one of three ways: a reserve funded inside the 7(a) loan, a lower price, or a plan to finance new machines separately. Card readers and payment systems on older machines are part of the same question.

Down Payment and Seller Notes on Standby

SBA lenders commonly require the buyer to inject at least 10% of the project cost on a change of ownership; conventional loans for business purchases typically ask for more, often 20-30%. A seller note can help close the gap. When the seller agrees to full standby, meaning no payments for a period set by SBA rules, part of the note may count toward the injection. Ask the lender how it will treat the note before the purchase agreement is final. Lenders also review your personal credit, commonly looking for 650-680+, and your personal financial statement.

Worked Example: A $600,000 Leased-Space Store

Illustrative assumptions, not a quote. A buyer purchases a self-service laundromat in a leased storefront with eight years left plus two five-year options. The rate assumes prime at 7.00% plus 2.75%. Payment computed with the standard amortization formula, payment = loan × r ÷ (1 − (1 + r)−n), with r = 9.75% ÷ 12 and n = 120.

DSCR is cash flow available for debt service divided by annual debt service: $140,000 ÷ $93,213 ≈ 1.50. Many lenders commonly look for about 1.25x or more, so this deal has room, and it still clears that level if documented cash flow comes in 15% lower. If the store could only document $110,000, coverage would fall to about 1.18x, and the buyer would need a lower price, a larger injection or a seller note on standby.

Financing a Machine Refresh After You Buy

Many buyers keep the acquisition loan lean and replace washers and dryers once they know the store. Equipment financing is secured by the new machines and commonly runs 36-72 months. At an assumed 9% over 60 months, $120,000 of new machines is about $2,491 a month, or $29,460 of interest over the term. The case is stronger when you can show what the old machines cost in repairs and lost turns. See equipment loan vs lease to compare structures.

Steps to Buy and Finance a Laundromat

  1. Read the lease first. Years left, options, assignment and water costs decide whether a lender can make a 10-year loan.
  2. Collect the records. Tax returns, card-system and processor reports, deposits and 24 months of utility bills.
  3. Inspect the machines and price a replacement plan.
  4. Recast the cash flow with a wage for the work the seller does, then check coverage at your price.
  5. Choose 7(a), 504 or a mix, and agree on any seller note and its standby terms.
  6. Compare lenders using how to compare business loan offers, then expect 30-60+ days to close an SBA loan once the file is complete.

Next Steps

A laundromat purchase gets financed when three things line up: revenue the records prove, a lease that outlasts the loan and machines with life left or a plan to replace them. Frequently Asked Questions

Can you use an SBA loan to buy a laundromat?

Yes. Laundromats are eligible small businesses, and buyers commonly use SBA 7(a) when the store sits in a leased space, or SBA 504 when the building is part of the purchase. A 7(a) loan can be up to $5 million (13 CFR 120.151). Plan on an equity injection of at least 10% of the project.

How long can a laundromat acquisition loan run?

Under 13 CFR 120.212, SBA loans generally run up to 10 years unless they finance real estate or equipment with a longer useful life, with a 25-year maximum. A leased-space laundromat purchase is usually a 10-year loan; one that includes the building can stretch toward 25 years on the real estate portion.

Does the store lease matter to the lender?

Yes, a lot. A laundromat's value depends on staying in its location, so lenders commonly want the lease, with renewal options, to run at least as long as the loan. A lease with three years left on a 10-year loan is a common reason a deal stalls.

How do lenders check a laundromat's revenue?

They compare what the seller reports with what the store can document: tax returns, card-system and payment-processor reports, bank deposits, and water and gas bills, which should rise and fall with wash volume. Coin-only stores with thin records get less credit for their stated cash.

What if the washers and dryers are old?

Expect the lender to ask for a condition report and to size the loan with a reserve or a plan to replace machines. Many buyers fund the replacement later with equipment financing, which keeps the acquisition loan smaller. In this page's example, $120,000 of new machines at an assumed 9% over 60 months is about $2,491 a month.

Can a seller note count toward the down payment?

Sometimes. When the seller agrees to put the note on full standby, SBA rules can allow part of it to count toward the equity injection; the rest must come from the buyer. Ask your lender how much of the note it will count before you sign the purchase agreement.
