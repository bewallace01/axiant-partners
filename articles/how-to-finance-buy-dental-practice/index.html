Buying a Dental Practice: What It Costs and How to Finance It

What practices sell for, how dentists finance the purchase with little or nothing down, and the steps from valuation to closing.

Buying a dental practice is one of the easiest small-business purchases to finance. Collections are predictable and patients rarely leave overnight, so lenders treat dentistry as low risk and routinely fund acquisitions with little or no money down. The structure depends on whether you are buying an existing office, starting from scratch or expanding. For the full product list, see dental practice financing.

Important: Axiant Partners is not a lender; we connect dentists with financing sources. Final terms depend on underwriting, program rules, and verification. This guide is educational only and not credit, legal, or tax advice.

How much does it cost to buy a dental practice?

Most practices are priced as a percentage of annual collections, frequently 60% to 80%, or on a multiple of adjusted earnings, often 2 to 3 times EBITDA or owner discretionary earnings. The percentage is a quick benchmark; the earnings method shows true profit once the seller’s salary and perks are normalized. Here is what that range means for price and payment if you finance the full price at an assumed 11.5% over 10 years:

The price is not the whole bill. Budget for working capital, commonly enough to cover three to six months of operating expenses, because insurance credentialing can take 60 to 120 days and claims may be slow in that window. Old chairs or imaging you will replace soon after closing belong in the budget too.

How do dentists finance buying a practice?

Three paths dominate. SBA 7(a) acquisition loans are the workhorse: they allow high loan-to-value, finance goodwill and amortize over long terms. Specialty practice-acquisition lenders, banks and finance companies with dedicated dental divisions, compete hard for healthy practices and sometimes advertise 100% financing. Conventional bank loans suit larger groups, multi-location DSOs or buyers who want to avoid SBA paperwork and personal guarantees on every owner, and usually ask for 10–20% down.

The SBA doesn’t lend directly. It guarantees part of a loan made by a participating bank, which lowers the bank’s risk and gets you better terms. Qualified dentists with clean credit and relevant clinical experience can often finance roughly 90% to 100% of the price, including the large goodwill share typical in dentistry. Terms commonly run 10 years for the business and up to 25 years when the building is part of the deal, and the 7(a) cap is $5 million, enough for most single-location purchases. Expect a rate tied to prime plus a spread, a personal guarantee from any owner of 20% or more, and assignment of life insurance. See the SBA loans overview for the program basics.

You can layer in equipment financing for chairs and imaging, commercial real estate loans if you are buying the building, and working capital loans for the transition. Most dental lenders bundle several of these into one closing so you have one payment.

Should you buy an existing practice or start a de novo?

Buying an established practice gets you a patient base, trained staff, active recall and cash flow from day one. Underwriting is easier because the lender can size the loan against real collections. The trade-off is paying for goodwill and inheriting someone else’s systems, equipment and lease.

A de novo startup gives you a clean slate on layout, technology and location, but it starts with zero collections, so it carries more ramp-up risk. Dental-focused lenders still fund de novos, often packaging the build-out, equipment, signage and several months of working capital together. They lean on your credit, your production history as an associate and a demographic study of the ZIP code. Plan for a slower revenue ramp and keep a personal cash cushion through the first year.

What do lenders look at in a dental practice?

Numbers alone don’t set the price or the loan. Lenders and buyers check the active patient count (typically patients seen in the last 18 months), the recall and retention rate, hygiene revenue as a share of production, and the payer mix: a fee-for-service or PPO-heavy practice usually commands more than one that depends heavily on Medicaid. Equipment condition, the length of the lease and how much production depends on the departing owner all move the multiple. A practice where the seller produces 90% of revenue is riskier than one with associates and a deep hygiene program.

On your side, dental lenders expect new owners to carry student debt and weigh your earning power alongside your credit. Typical expectations are a personal credit score around 650 to 680 or higher (stronger scores price better), a clean recent payment history, a license in good standing and demonstrated production as an associate or in residency. Student loans are usually treated as normal for a dentist, as long as projected practice cash flow covers every obligation with a comfortable debt service coverage ratio.

Financing equipment and the build-out

Clinical technology is a major line item: operatory chairs and delivery units, CEREC and other CAD/CAM milling systems, digital panoramic and 3D cone-beam imaging, intraoral scanners, sterilization and practice-management software can run into the hundreds of thousands of dollars. You can fund these through dental equipment financing or, for high-end radiology, medical imaging equipment loans, which spread the cost over the asset’s useful life and may offer Section 179 tax advantages.

Build-out costs, such as plumbing for each operatory, cabinetry, nitrous lines and compliant electrical, are often rolled into the acquisition or startup loan rather than financed separately. That keeps your debt service in one place.

How much working capital do you need for the transition?

Cash is tightest in the weeks around closing. You may keep the seller on for a transition period, retain staff at their current pay, wait on insurance credentialing and absorb a dip in production while patients get to know you. Building working capital into the loan, commonly enough for three to six months of operating expenses, keeps you from draining personal reserves. Credentialing alone can take 60 to 120 days, so plan the runway conservatively. Owners adding locations later can look at medical practice business financing that underwrites group cash flow.

How do you buy a dental practice, step by step?

  1. Define the deal: acquisition, de novo or expansion. Each routes to different lenders and structures.
  2. Pull your credit and gather documents: tax returns, license, student-loan statements and a personal financial statement.
  3. Get the target valued: request the seller’s production and collections reports and a third-party valuation.
  4. Pre-qualify before you commit: see how to prequalify for a business loan to gauge terms without a hard credit pull.
  5. Compare offers side by side: rate, term, fees, prepayment penalties and guarantee scope. Read how to compare business loan offers.
  6. Close and fund: coordinate the lender, escrow and the seller’s transition, typically 30 to 60+ days.

Next steps

For most dentists the question is not whether you can get funded but whether you get the right structure at the right cost. Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to buy an established dental practice?

Most practices sell for 60–80% of annual collections or 2–3 times adjusted earnings, so a practice collecting $1 million usually prices around $600,000–$800,000. Financed in full at an assumed 11.5% over 10 years, that is about $8,436–$11,248 a month. Add working capital for three to six months of expenses.

Can you use an SBA loan to buy a dental practice?

Yes. The SBA 7(a) program is one of the most common ways to finance a dental practice purchase. Strong, credentialed buyers can often finance roughly 90–100% of the price, including goodwill, over 10 years for the business portion and up to 25 years when real estate is included. The 7(a) loan cap is $5 million.

How much down payment do you need to buy a dental practice?

Down payments commonly range from 0 to 10 percent on SBA-backed dental purchases for qualified buyers, and roughly 10 to 20 percent on conventional bank loans. Some specialty practice-acquisition lenders advertise 100 percent financing for dentists with solid credit and a healthy target practice, but you should still keep working-capital reserves.

How long does it take to buy a dental practice?

Once you have a signed agreement and a complete file, an SBA-financed purchase typically takes 30 to 60 days or more to close. Getting the valuation, the seller's production and collections reports and your own documents together first is what keeps that timeline. Insurance credentialing can take another 60 to 120 days after you take over.

Can you finance a dental startup or de novo build-out?

Yes. Lenders that focus on dentistry finance de novo startups, often bundling the build-out, equipment and working capital into one package. A startup has no collections history, so underwriting leans on your credit, experience, business plan and demographic data, and may require a larger personal investment than buying an established practice.

How are dental practices valued?

Commonly as a percentage of annual collections, frequently 60 to 80 percent, or on a multiple of adjusted earnings, often 2 to 3 times EBITDA or owner discretionary earnings. Patient count, recall rate, payer mix, hygiene revenue, equipment condition and lease terms all affect where a specific practice lands.
