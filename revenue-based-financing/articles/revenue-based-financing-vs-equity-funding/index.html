Revenue-Based Financing vs Equity: Is RBF Dilutive?

RBF lets you fund growth without selling ownership—equity trades permanent shares for cash you never repay. How they compare on dilution, cost, control, and fit

Quick Facts

RBF is non-dilutive: no ownership given up, no warrants, finite cost, defined end. Equity = permanent ownership sold, no repayment, open-ended future cost + control implications. RBF for revenue-generating growth; equity for big, long-horizon bets.

Quick answer

No—revenue-based financing is non-dilutive. You give up no ownership, there are typically no warrants, you repay the advance plus a flat fee from a percentage of revenue, and then it's over—you still own everything you owned before. Equity funding is the opposite: you sell a permanent stake for cash you never repay, diluting your ownership and often adding investors to your cap table and board. RBF has a known, finite cost and a defined end; equity has no repayment but an open-ended ownership (and sometimes control) cost. RBF fits revenue-generating growth; equity fits big, long-horizon or pre-revenue bets.

"Should I raise money or borrow it?" is one of the biggest decisions a founder makes—and the answer hinges on what each option really costs. The headline question, is RBF dilutive, has a clean answer: no. But the deeper comparison—cost, control, and fit—deserves a real look. This page lays it out. Dilution: The Core Distinction

With equity, you sell shares. The cash never has to be repaid, but you permanently give up a slice of ownership—and the value of that slice grows as the company grows. You may also add investors with information rights, board seats, or approval over major decisions.

With RBF, you keep 100% of your equity. You repay a fixed total (the advance plus a flat fee) out of revenue, and when it's paid, the relationship ends. No shares change hands, no board seats, no permanent claim on your upside. That's what "non-dilutive" means in practice.

The Real Cost of Each

RBF's cost is explicit and capped: the advance plus a flat fee. Equity's cost is implicit and open-ended—the true price is the future value of the ownership you sold, which can dwarf the cash you raised if the company succeeds. For a growing, profitable business, paying a known RBF fee can be far cheaper than handing over shares that compound in value. But equity has a crucial advantage where RBF can't compete: there's nothing to repay, which matters when near-term revenue can't service a repayment. See typical RBF rates to frame the known cost.

When Each One Makes Sense

  • Choose RBF when you have revenue, want to fund growth (inventory, marketing, hiring) that pays back, and want to keep full ownership and control. See RBF requirements and fits like SaaS and D2C.
  • Choose equity when you're pre-revenue, capital-intensive, or need large amounts you couldn't repay from near-term revenue—or when investors bring strategic value beyond cash. Know RBF's limits: when RBF is not the right option.

Use Both Strategically

It's not all-or-nothing. Many companies raise equity for the big, long-horizon bets and use revenue-based financing for revenue-generating growth that pays back quickly—so they don't dilute further to fund spending the revenue will cover. Putting non-dilutive capital where it fits can meaningfully reduce how much equity you ultimately sell. Explore the broader startup financing options.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is revenue-based financing dilutive?

No. RBF is generally non-dilutive: you don't give up ownership or board control, and there are typically no warrants. You repay the advance plus a flat fee from a percentage of revenue, then the obligation ends and you still own everything you owned before. That non-dilutive nature is why founders with revenue often choose RBF over an equity round.

What is the difference between RBF and equity funding?

RBF is capital you repay from revenue and then you're done, keeping full ownership and control. Equity is selling a permanent stake for cash you don't repay, which dilutes ownership and usually adds investors to your cap table and sometimes board. RBF has a defined cost and end date; equity has no repayment but a permanent ownership and potential control cost.

Is RBF cheaper than giving up equity?

It can be, especially for a growing, profitable business, because the equity you'd sell today could be worth far more later. RBF has a known, finite cost, while equity's true cost is the future value of the ownership you gave up. For pre-revenue or capital-intensive companies that can't service repayment, equity may still be the better or only fit.

When should I raise equity instead of using RBF?

Equity tends to fit pre-revenue companies, deeply capital-intensive businesses, and cases where you need large amounts you couldn't repay from near-term revenue, or where investors bring strategic value beyond money. RBF fits revenue-generating companies that want to fund growth, smooth cash flow, or extend runway without selling ownership.

Can I use RBF alongside equity?

Yes. Many companies combine them: raise equity for big, long-horizon bets and use RBF for revenue-generating growth like inventory or marketing, so they don't dilute further for spending that pays back quickly. Using non-dilutive capital where it fits can reduce how much equity you ultimately sell.
