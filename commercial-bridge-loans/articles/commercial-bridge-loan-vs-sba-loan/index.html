Commercial Bridge Loan vs SBA Loan

Speed vs. cost — how the two compare on funding time, rate, term, and use case, and how to use a bridge now and refinance into SBA later

Quick answer

A commercial bridge loan and an SBA loan solve different problems. A bridge loan is short-term, fast, and costlier — it can close in days to a couple of weeks, runs 6–36 months at a higher rate, and is built to act fast or cover a gap until permanent financing. An SBA 7(a) or 504 loan is long-term, slower, and cheaper to carry — it takes weeks to a couple of months to close but offers low rates and 10–25-year terms. Choose the bridge when timing wins the deal; choose SBA when you want the lowest long-term cost. A very common play is to bridge now and refinance into SBA once the property or business is stabilized. Figures are illustrative, not quotes.

Borrowers weighing a commercial bridge loan against an SBA loan are usually really asking one question: do I need speed, or do I need the cheapest money? The two products sit at opposite ends of that trade-off, and the right answer depends entirely on your deadline and your plan to exit. This guide compares them head to head and shows when each wins.

The pattern is consistent: the bridge loan trades a higher cost for speed and flexibility, while SBA trades time and paperwork for low long-term cost. Neither is "better" in the abstract — they fit different moments in a deal.

When a Bridge Loan Wins

A bridge loan is the right tool when the clock is the constraint. Typical situations: a time-sensitive purchase where a slower lender would lose the deal; an auction or competitive offer that needs proof of fast funds; buying before you sell an existing property; or a property that does not yet qualify for permanent financing (needs lease-up, renovation, or seasoning) and must be stabilized first. In each case, the higher cost of the bridge is justified because it captures an opportunity or prevents a larger loss. For the deeper decision framework, see when should you use a commercial bridge loan and how fast you can close a bridge loan.

When an SBA Loan Wins

SBA financing wins when you have time and want the lowest long-term cost. It is built for acquisitions, owner-occupied commercial real estate, and long-term holds, with low rates and amortization stretching to 25 years on real estate. The trade-off is the process: full financials, a business plan, personal guarantees, and a closing measured in weeks to a couple of months. If your deal has no hard deadline — or you can use a bridge to hold the deadline — SBA is usually the cheaper way to own the asset over time. Compare the SBA programs in SBA 7(a) vs 504 and the timeline in how long SBA approval takes.

Using Both: Bridge Now, Refinance into SBA Later

The two products are not mutually exclusive — the most powerful play often uses them in sequence. You close fast with a bridge loan to win the deal or stabilize the property, then refinance into an SBA 7(a) or 504 loan once the asset or business cash flow supports permanent financing. This captures the bridge's speed and the SBA's low long-term cost. The keys are to confirm up front that the SBA program allows the specific refinance, that the stabilized numbers will actually qualify, and that the bridge's term gives you enough runway to close the SBA takeout. Model the carry cost of the bridge against the rate savings of the SBA before committing.

The Real Cost Difference

On a rate basis, the bridge is almost always more expensive: higher interest plus origination points and fees, because it is short-term and fast. SBA is cheaper to carry, but it is not free either — expect guarantee fees, packaging, and a slower path. The honest way to compare is total cost for your hold period and exit plan: a few months of bridge cost can be trivial if it wins a deal you then own cheaply via SBA, but an unplanned long bridge hold gets expensive fast. Frequently Asked Questions

What is the main difference between a commercial bridge loan and an SBA loan?

Speed versus cost. A commercial bridge loan is short-term financing that can close in days to a couple of weeks at a higher rate, designed to act fast or cover a gap. An SBA 7(a) or 504 loan is long-term, lower-rate financing that takes weeks to a couple of months to close because of fuller underwriting and the government guarantee. Bridge buys speed; SBA buys cheaper, longer-term capital.

When should you use a bridge loan instead of an SBA loan?

Use a bridge loan when timing matters more than rate: a time-sensitive property purchase, an auction or competitive deal, buying before you sell, or stabilizing a property that does not yet qualify for permanent financing. SBA loans are slower, so they rarely fit a hard deadline. Many borrowers bridge now and refinance into SBA or conventional debt once the deal is stabilized.

Can you refinance a bridge loan into an SBA loan?

Yes, and it is a common strategy. You close fast with a bridge loan to win or stabilize the deal, then refinance into an SBA 7(a) or 504 loan once the property or business cash flow supports permanent financing. This captures the speed of the bridge and the low long-term cost of SBA. Confirm the SBA program allows the specific refinance and that the numbers work before committing to the bridge.

Is a bridge loan more expensive than an SBA loan?

Usually yes on a rate basis. Bridge loans carry higher interest plus points and fees because they are short-term and faster to fund. SBA loans typically have lower rates and long amortization, so they are cheaper to carry over years. The bridge can still be the right call if speed wins the deal or avoids a larger loss, but you should plan an exit (sale or refinance) rather than hold a bridge long-term.
