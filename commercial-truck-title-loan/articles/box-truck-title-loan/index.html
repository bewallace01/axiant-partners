What a Box Truck Is Worth to a Lender

Box trucks span four weight classes, and the class decides both the price and who can buy it. New prices, as published on our box truck financing page:

Size and classTypical new priceWhat it means for a loan
10-16 ft, Class 3-4$35,000-$75,000Easy to sell; a used one may support only a small advance
16-22 ft, Class 5-6$75,000-$90,000The sweet spot: no CDL needed, wide buyer pool
22-26 ft, Class 6-7$90,000-$100,000+Over 26,000 lb GVWR needs a CDL, so fewer buyers

Used box trucks typically cost 30-50% less than new equivalents, and large delivery and rental fleets cycle units into the used market continuously. That keeps resale liquid but keeps prices low, which is why the advance on a box truck is usually a smaller number than owners hope.

Loan-to-Value: Why the Dollar Amount Is the Limit

Lenders commonly advance roughly 50-80% of forced liquidation value on owned trucks. That range is conventional across lenders, not anyone's quote. A box truck can sit in the upper part of it, because the market is deep and the trucks are easy to sell, and still produce a small loan, because the value underneath it is small.

Two things specific to box trucks move it:

  • The 26,000-pound line. Under federal rules, a single vehicle rated over 26,000 lb GVWR needs a Class B CDL. A Class 6 box truck can be driven by anyone with a regular license; a Class 7 cannot. More buyers means a faster sale, which is what a lender prices.
  • Stop-and-go mileage. Delivery miles are hard miles: brakes, transmission and idling hours wear faster than on a highway truck with the same odometer reading.

Because closing costs are largely fixed - title work, filing, any inspection - they take a bigger share of a small advance. Run the arithmetic before you start.

A Worked Example (Illustrative)

Take a used 24-foot Class 6 box truck, owned outright, whose forced liquidation value is put at $40,000. The figures are illustrative, not a quote:

  • 50-80% of $40,000 is $20,000-$32,000
  • No payoff, so that is the gross advance
  • Less title work, lien filing and any inspection

Compare that with the same owner's two- or three-truck fleet pledged together. One facility against several titles spreads the fixed costs and usually makes a more useful number - see owner-operator versus fleet terms.

Who Lends on a Box Truck

  • Equipment and commercial-vehicle finance companies that refinance owned trucks and pay out the equity. Some have a minimum loan size, which a single older box truck may not reach.
  • Fleet and delivery-business lenders that will take several titles in one facility, often alongside vans.
  • Banks and credit unions, mostly for existing customers; a smaller box truck is sometimes handled like any other business vehicle loan.
  • Consumer car-title lenders. A Class 3 cutaway looks close to a pickup, and some will take one, but under consumer rules and for a personal borrower. For business-purpose money, a commercial lender is the right home.

When a Title Loan Is the Wrong Tool

  • The truck is leased or on a rental contract. You do not hold the title, so there is nothing to lend against.
  • The advance is too small to be worth the cost. If a single older truck supports only a few thousand dollars after fees, a business line of credit or working capital loan is usually cheaper per dollar.
  • The gap is a delivery contract that pays late. Net terms from a shipper or platform are a receivables problem; financing against the invoices matches it better.
  • You need the truck replaced, not refinanced. If the box truck is near the end of its life, the cash is better raised as a down payment on an equipment loan for the next one.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I get a title loan on my box truck?

Yes, as a business-purpose loan secured by the title. Lenders advance against the truck's forced liquidation value, commonly in the range of 50-80% of it, less any payoff, and you keep using the truck.

How much can I borrow against a box truck?

Usually a modest amount, because the truck's value is modest. New box trucks run roughly $35,000 to $100,000 or more, used ones less, and a lender advances a percentage of what the used truck would fetch sold quickly.

Does it matter if my box truck needs a CDL?

It can. A single vehicle over 26,000 lb GVWR needs a Class B CDL, so fewer buyers can use it. A Class 6 truck under that line sells to a wider pool, which a lender pricing a quick sale counts in its favor.

Can I borrow against several box trucks at once?

Yes, and it often makes more sense. One facility against several titles spreads the fixed closing costs and produces a more useful number than a single older truck.

Can I get a title loan on a leased box truck?

No. A title loan needs a title you own; on a leased or rental truck the leasing company holds it. The equity question only arises once the truck is yours.

Sources & Further Reading

  • OCC Comptroller's Handbook: Asset-Based Lending — The federal bank examiners' handbook on collateral lending, including orderly and forced liquidation value - the bases lenders advance against instead of market value.
  • U.S. Department of Energy: Vehicle Weight Classes and Categories — The Class 1-8 gross vehicle weight bands used to describe box trucks, from Class 3 cutaways to Class 7 straight trucks.
  • 49 CFR 383.91: Commercial Motor Vehicle Groups — The federal rule that puts a single vehicle over 26,000 pounds GVWR in the Class B CDL group - the line that splits the box truck resale market in two.
  • IRS Publication 946: How To Depreciate Property — The authority on Section 179 and depreciation for business vehicles, which is what determines a truck's book value against its market value.

Figures above describe ranges commonly seen across lenders and reflect published guidance as of the date on this page. Confirm current terms with the cited source or your lender before acting.
