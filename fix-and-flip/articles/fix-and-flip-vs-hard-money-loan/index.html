Hard Money Loans to Flip Houses vs Structured Fix-and-Flip Loans

How hard money works on a flip, what it costs, what lenders want, and when a structured fix-and-flip loan is the better deal

Quick answer

A hard money loan to flip a house is short-term private money secured by the property: usually 6-12 months, interest-only, with 1-3 or more points up front, and it can close in 1-3 weeks. Lenders typically cap the loan at about 75% of after-repair value (ARV) and fund up to 100% of the rehab in draws, so you bring 10-20% of the purchase price plus closing costs. Structured fix-and-flip lenders, which price in the low-to-mid teens, are usually cheaper if you have 620+ credit and a clear rehab plan.

How Does a Hard Money Loan to Flip a House Work?

A hard money loan is short-term, asset-backed money from private individuals, small lending groups or local funds. The lender cares mostly about the house and your equity in it, not your tax returns, so it can move fast and say yes to deals a bank will not touch: distressed purchases, quick closings, investors who do not fit traditional lending.

On a flip, the loan usually covers part of the purchase and the rehab. You pay interest only, rehab money is released as work gets done, and you pay the loan off when you sell, typically within 6-12 months. Expect a higher rate than a structured program, 1-3 or more points at closing, and extension fees if the project runs long. A structured fix-and-flip loan is the same basic tool from an institutional lender, with standardized underwriting, 6-18 month terms and inspection-based draws. Structured fix-and-flip programs commonly price in the low-to-mid teens, interest-only, plus points. Hard money usually costs more on both, in exchange for speed and loose credit. For investors doing several flips a year, the cheaper, repeatable structure usually wins; for one odd deal that has to close this week, hard money often does.

How Much Will a Hard Money Lender Lend on a Flip?

Lenders use three ceilings at once: up to about 80% of the purchase price, up to 100% of the rehab, and a total loan of no more than about 75% of ARV (some programs also cap total cost at 90%). First-time flippers are often held to 65-70% of ARV. Here is a typical deal:

The $40,000 down payment is 20% of the purchase price, at the top of the usual 10-20% range. Interest is lower while the rehab is being drawn, and the costs that eat profit are usually the extra months, not the rate. True 100% financing is rare; lenders want your money in every deal. What Is the 70% Rule for Flipping Houses?

It is a buying guideline: your purchase price plus rehab should not exceed 70% of the after-repair value. On a $360,000 ARV, that means $252,000 for purchase and rehab combined. The other 30% covers closing and selling costs, holding costs and your profit. Lenders like deals that pass it, and first-time flippers should aim lower, around 65-68%, to build margin and improve approval odds. For where flip profit actually goes, see house flipping costs.

Is It Hard to Get a Loan to Flip a House?

Not if the deal is good. Hard money lenders approve mainly on the property: purchase price, rehab scope, ARV and comparable sales, plus enough cash for the down payment and reserves. Credit and income matter less than at a bank. Structured fix-and-flip programs look harder at you: most want 620+ credit, and first-timers are often asked for 700+, cash reserves and a detailed, line-item rehab budget. Both close in about 1-3 weeks once the appraisal and documents are in. The full checklist is in hard money loan requirements.

What Is the Best Loan for Flipping Houses?

Use hard money when you need to close fast, the property is rural or unusual, your credit is weak, it is a one-off project, or the deal structure will not fit a standard program.

Use a structured fix-and-flip loan when you plan several flips a year, want predictable leverage and draws, are working larger deals, or want lower pricing. First-time flippers often do better with a structured lender because the terms and draw schedule are clearer; see fix and flip loans for beginners. On larger or commercial projects, a commercial bridge loan can replace both.

Before you sign either, read the term sheet for total dollars, not just the rate: points, draw and inspection fees, extension costs and prepayment terms. Get matched with flip lenders to compare both on the same deal.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is it hard to get a loan to flip a house?

Not with a good deal. Hard money lenders approve mainly on the property, its after-repair value and your down payment, with minimal credit and income requirements. Structured fix-and-flip programs usually want 620+ credit, and often 700+ for first-time flippers.

What is the 70% rule for flipping houses?

Your purchase price plus rehab should not exceed 70% of the after-repair value. On a $360,000 ARV, that is $252,000 combined. The remaining 30% covers transaction and holding costs and your profit.

What is the best loan for flipping houses?

A structured fix-and-flip loan is usually cheapest if you have 620+ credit and a clear rehab plan, especially for repeat flips. Hard money is better for fast closings, weak credit, unusual properties or one-off deals.

Is it hard to qualify for a hard money loan?

Usually not. Hard money lenders focus on the property and your equity, not tax returns. Expect to bring 10-20% of the purchase price, closing costs and reserves, and to pay a higher rate and points than a structured loan.

How much do hard money lenders charge to flip a house?

Expect interest-only payments at a rate above structured fix-and-flip programs, which commonly run in the low-to-mid teens, plus 1-3 or more points at closing. Watch for draw, inspection and extension fees, which add up if the project runs long.

Can you get a hard money loan to flip houses with no money down?

Rarely. Lenders typically fund up to about 80% of the purchase and 100% of the rehab, capped near 75% of ARV, so most flippers bring 10-20% of the purchase price plus closing costs.

Can a first-time flipper get a hard money loan?

Yes. Lenders approve first-timers on the strength of the deal, but often cap them at 65-70% of ARV instead of 70-75% and want stronger credit and reserves. A structured program with clear draw schedules is often the easier first loan.
