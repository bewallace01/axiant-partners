House Flipping Costs: What a Flip Really Costs and What Kills the Profit

Every cost line on a real flip, how much cash you need to start, and the three costs that turn a good deal into a thin one

← Back to Fix and Flip Articles

Updated November 20, 2026

Quick answer

House flipping costs fall into five buckets: the purchase, the rehab, holding costs while you work (loan interest, taxes, insurance, utilities), financing costs such as points, and selling costs of roughly 8% of the sale price. On a $200,000 house with a $60,000 rehab and a $350,000 resale, a 4-month plan at $3,500 a month of holding costs leaves about $43,600. A 30% rehab overrun and three extra months cut that to about $15,100.

What Does It Cost to Flip a House?

More than the purchase and the rehab. The costs that decide your profit are the ones that run by the month and the ones that come off the top at the sale. Take a typical flip: bought at $200,000, a $60,000 rehab budget, and an after-repair value (ARV) of $350,000. The plan was four months. The rehab ran 30% over and the project took seven.

CostPlanActual
Purchase$200,000$200,000
Rehab$60,000$78,000 (30% over)
Holding costs at $3,500/mo$14,000 (4 months)$24,500 (7 months)
Selling and closing, ~8%$28,000$28,000
Loan points, 2% of $220,000$4,400$4,400
Total cost$306,400$334,900
Profit on a $350,000 sale$43,600 (14.2%)$15,100 (4.5%)

Nothing went badly wrong, and the profit still fell from $43,600 to $15,100, from about 14% of total cost to about 4.5%. The purchase price did not change. The overrun, the three extra months of carry and the fixed selling costs did the damage; they are the line items flippers most often underestimate. Figures are illustrative.

How Much Do Holding Costs Add?

Loan interest, property taxes, insurance, utilities and any HOA dues run every month the house is not sold. In the example they were $3,500 a month, so each extra month took $3,500 straight out of profit. Budget 2-6+ months of holding costs with a buffer, and run your numbers as if the project takes longer than planned. A draw schedule that releases rehab money as work is done helps, because you only pay interest on what has been drawn.

How Much Should You Budget for Rehab Overruns?

Rehab budgets routinely run 20-40% over, from structural, mechanical and permit surprises behind the walls. Get contractor bids, walk the property with your contractor before you close, and add a 10-20% contingency on top of the bid. On a $60,000 rehab that is $6,000-$12,000; this example's overrun was $18,000. For the other common traps, see fix and flip mistakes to avoid.

What If the ARV or the Sale Price Comes In Low?

If you overestimated the after-repair value with the wrong comps, a market shift or improvements the neighborhood will not pay for, the sale price falls short and every dollar comes out of profit. Use conservative comps and do not over-improve. Selling costs of about 8% come off the top regardless, about $28,000 on a $350,000 sale. Price realistically from the start, stage the house, and have a backup plan, such as renting it, if the market softens. If a lower ARV would break the deal, the loan will shrink too, since lenders cap it at about 75% of ARV.

How Much Do Loan Points and Fees Cost on a Flip?

Most flip loans charge 1-3 or more points at closing, plus interest-only payments that commonly run in the low-to-mid teens on structured programs. In the example, 2 points on a $220,000 loan cost $4,400 before any work started. Watch for draw, inspection and extension fees and for prepayment terms. Compare lenders on total dollars, not the rate alone; see fix and flip loan red flags.

What Is the 70% Rule in Flipping?

It is a guideline for what to pay: purchase plus rehab should not exceed 70% of ARV. On a $350,000 ARV that is $245,000. The example deal, at $260,000, missed it by $15,000, and that thin cushion is why a normal overrun and a few extra months hurt so much. First-time flippers should aim lower, around 65-68%.

How Much Capital Do You Need to Start Flipping Houses?

With a fix-and-flip loan covering up to 80% of the purchase and 100% of the rehab, the example deal needed this much of the flipper's own cash:

Cash itemAmount
Down payment, 20% of purchase$40,000
Loan points, 2%$4,400
Holding costs, 4 months$14,000
Rehab contingency, 10-20%$6,000-$12,000
Total before closing costs$64,400-$70,400

Add purchase closing costs and the appraisal on top. Most lenders want 10-20% down; true 100% financing is rare. See how much down payment a fix-and-flip loan needs and fix and flip loans for beginners.

How Do You Protect the Margin?

  • Buy on the numbers. Run the deal at a lower ARV, a longer timeline and a 10-20% rehab overrun. If it still works, buy it.
  • Underwrite the timeline honestly. Every extra month is holding cost; a faster, cleaner scope often beats a bigger one.
  • Match the loan to the project. A draw schedule keeps interest off money you have not used, and the term should cover a realistic timeline plus a buffer. Compare hard money and fix-and-flip loans.
  • Know your exit before you buy. A conservative ARV from actual comps keeps the whole model honest.

On larger projects, a commercial bridge loan can replace flip financing. When you have a deal, get matched with fix-and-flip lenders and compare total cost.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the costs of flipping a house?

The purchase, the rehab, holding costs while you work (loan interest, taxes, insurance, utilities, HOA), financing costs such as points, and selling and closing costs of roughly 8% of the sale price. Holding costs and rehab overruns are the ones most often underestimated.

How much capital do I need to start flipping houses?

On a $200,000 house with a $60,000 rehab financed with a fix-and-flip loan, about $64,400-$70,400 before closing costs: a $40,000 down payment, $4,400 in points, four months of holding costs and a 10-20% rehab contingency.

What is the 70% rule in flipping?

Purchase price plus rehab should not exceed 70% of the after-repair value. On a $350,000 ARV, that is $245,000. First-time flippers should aim for about 65-68% to leave room for overruns.

Why do holding costs hurt flip profit so much?

Every extra month adds loan interest, taxes, insurance and utilities while the house earns nothing. In the example, three extra months at $3,500 cost $10,500.

How much should I budget for rehab overruns?

Add a 10-20% contingency on top of the contractor bid. Rehab budgets routinely run 20-40% over because of structural, mechanical and permit surprises.

What if my ARV was wrong?

The sale price comes in lower than planned and the difference comes out of profit. Options are to hold and rent, cut the price to sell faster, or talk to the lender about an extension. Use conservative comps on the next deal.
