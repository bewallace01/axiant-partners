Hard Money Loan Requirements: What Fix-and-Flip Lenders Check Before Funding

The deal, the borrower and the exit: what a hard money or fix-and-flip lender needs to see, with a worked example

Home › Fix and Flip › Articles › Hard Money Loan Requirements
← Back to Fix and Flip Articles

Updated November 20, 2026

Quick answer

Hard money loan requirements center on the deal: a purchase price and rehab budget that keep the loan within about 70-75% of after-repair value (ARV), comps that support that ARV, and a realistic scope of work. On your side, lenders want 10-20% of the purchase price as a down payment, cash for closing costs, points and reserves, and a clear exit, usually a sale within 6-18 months. Pure hard money has minimal credit requirements; structured fix-and-flip programs usually want 620+ credit and prefer 2+ flips in the past 2 years.

What Are the Requirements for a Hard Money Loan?

A hard money lender underwrites the house first and you second. If the deal is strong, a borderline borrower can get funded; if the deal is weak, a strong borrower still gets declined. Here is the full list:

RequirementLenders want
LeverageLoan within about 70-75% of ARV; up to 80% of purchase and 100% of rehab; 90% of total cost
Down payment10-20% of the purchase price
CashClosing costs, 1-3+ points, an interest reserve and rehab contingency
CreditMinimal for pure hard money; 620+ for most structured programs; first-timers often 700+
Experience2+ flips in the past 2 years preferred; first-timers capped lower, about 65-70% of ARV
The propertyA real discount to market, ARV backed by comps, a feasible rehab
ExitSale within 6-18 months, or a refinance into a rental loan

Pure private hard money leans almost entirely on the property and asks little about credit or income. Structured fix-and-flip lenders use the same deal tests and add credit, experience and liquidity checks in exchange for lower pricing. Hard money loans to flip houses vs fix-and-flip loans compares the two.

What Do Lenders Check on the Property?

  • Purchase discount. Lenders want a property bought below market value; a thin discount leaves no room for mistakes.
  • ARV support. Comparable sales have to back the after-repair value. Aggressive ARV projections cut the loan or kill the deal. Many investors test the buy with the 70% rule: purchase plus rehab at or below 70% of ARV.
  • Rehab scope and budget. A detailed scope, contractor estimates and track record, a realistic timeline, and a clear split between structural and cosmetic work. Unrealistic budgets are among the most common red flags.
  • Location. Neighborhood strength and local resale demand decide how fast the house sells at the end.

How Much Do You Need for a Hard Money Loan?

Take a flip bought at $200,000 with a $50,000 rehab and a $320,000 after-repair value. The lender checks three caps and lends the lowest:

Lender capCalculationMax loan
Purchase + rehab80% of $200,000 + 100% of $50,000$210,000
After-repair value75% of $320,000$240,000
Total cost90% of $250,000$225,000
Loan offeredThe lowest of the three$210,000

You bring the $40,000 gap, which is 20% of the purchase price, plus closing costs, the appraisal, about $4,200 at 2 points, and an interest reserve. Rehab budgets routinely run 20-40% over, so plan a 10-20% contingency, here $5,000-$10,000. Notice the 70% rule: 70% of $320,000 is $224,000, but purchase plus rehab is $250,000. The deal fails it by $26,000, so expect the lender to question the ARV or the purchase price, and a first-time flipper may be held to 65-70% of ARV. Figures are illustrative, not a quote. What Credit Score and Experience Do You Need?

Credit: pure hard money has minimal credit requirements. Most structured programs want 620+, and stronger credit brings higher leverage, lower rates and fewer document requests. First-time flippers are often asked for 700+.

Experience: lenders prefer 2+ flips in the past 2 years. Newer investors still qualify, usually with stronger credit, more cash and a tighter rehab plan; see fix and flip loans for first-time flippers.

Liquidity: money for the down payment, closing costs and reserves for carrying costs and overruns. Running out of cash mid-project is one of the top reasons flips fail, so underwriters check it closely.

Why Does the Exit Strategy Matter?

A hard money loan is a bridge to something: usually a sale after renovation, sometimes a refinance into a long-term rental loan. Lenders underwrite to that exit, typically within 6-18 months. High leverage raises the bar, so a high-leverage request needs stronger ARV support, more experience or more cash. Pricing runs in the low-to-mid teens on most structured programs, plus points; see fix and flip loans for program terms.

Why Do Hard Money Loans Get Declined?

  • Inflated ARV or weak comparable sales
  • A thin purchase discount
  • An unrealistic rehab budget
  • Too little cash for the down payment and reserves
  • Credit below the program minimum
  • Weak local resale demand
  • Too much leverage across the borrower's other projects

To improve your odds, bring strong comps, a line-item rehab budget with contractor bids, proof of cash, any prior project history, and answer underwriting requests the same day. Complete files close in about 1-3 weeks. On larger deals, a commercial bridge loan can replace flip financing. When the file is ready, get matched with flip lenders.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the requirements for a hard money loan?

A deal that keeps the loan within about 70-75% of after-repair value, comps that support that value, a realistic rehab budget, 10-20% of the purchase price down, cash for closing costs and reserves, and a clear exit. Structured programs add 620+ credit and prefer prior flips.

How much do I need for a hard money loan?

Usually 10-20% of the purchase price plus closing costs, 1-3 or more points, an interest reserve and a rehab contingency. On a $200,000 purchase with a $50,000 rehab, the loan is about $210,000, so you bring about $40,000 plus those costs.

What credit score do I need for a hard money loan?

Pure hard money lenders have minimal credit requirements and focus on the property. Most structured fix-and-flip programs want 620+, and first-time flippers are often asked for 700+. Stronger credit brings higher leverage and lower rates.

What happens if you don't pay back a hard money lender?

The property secures the loan, so the lender can foreclose and sell it to recover what is owed, and a personal guarantee, if you signed one, puts your own assets at risk for any shortfall. If a project is running late, ask about an extension before the maturity date; extensions cost fees, but far less than a default.

Do you need experience to get a hard money loan?

No, but it helps. Lenders prefer 2+ flips in the past 2 years. First-timers can qualify with a strong deal, but often get lower leverage, about 65-70% of ARV, and need more cash and stronger credit.

Why do fix and flip loans get declined?

Usually an inflated ARV with weak comps, a thin purchase discount, an unrealistic rehab budget, or too little cash for reserves. Fixing any of these improves your odds.
