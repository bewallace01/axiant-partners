Equipment Appraisal Companies: Who Can Appraise Business Equipment for a Lender

Which appraisers and firms lenders accept, what the report has to show, what it costs, and who chooses the appraiser

← Back to Equipment Appraisal Articles
Home › Equipment Appraisal › Articles › Equipment Appraisal Companies

Updated November 20, 2026

Quick answer

Lenders accept equipment appraisal companies that are independent of the sale and the loan, hold a machinery and equipment credential (the American Society of Appraisers issues one) and write reports to USPAP. Many lenders pick the appraiser from their own panel. A dealer's trade-in offer, a price guide printout or your own estimate is not an appraisal. The report should state fair market, orderly liquidation and forced liquidation value; lenders lend against the liquidation figures, often 50-80% of forced liquidation value.

Why Won't a Lender Accept a Dealer's Number?

The reason a lender will not accept a dealer's number is not that it is wrong. It is that the person producing it has a position.

A dealer quoting a trade-in is quoting what they would pay, which is a bid, not a valuation. A seller's asking price is an aspiration. Your own figure, however well informed, is the borrower's opinion of the borrower's collateral.

An appraisal is a documented opinion from someone with no interest in the transaction, produced to a standard, with the reasoning written down. That is what makes it usable as the basis for lending, and it is why lenders often instruct the appraiser directly rather than accepting one you commissioned.

What Should an Equipment Appraisal Report Contain?

You can judge quality without being an appraiser. A usable report shows its working:

  • The basis, named explicitly: fair market, orderly liquidation or forced liquidation. A report that does not say is not usable; see orderly versus forced liquidation value.
  • The effective date. Values move; an undated opinion is worthless.
  • Identification: serial numbers, models, years, hours.
  • Method and comparables, so the number can be followed rather than taken on trust.
  • Scope: whether it was a desktop or an inspection, and what was assumed.
  • Signature and credentials, with a statement of independence.

A number on letterhead with none of that is a quote wearing a costume.

Who Chooses the Appraisal Company?

Usually the lender, and there is a reason worth understanding.

An appraisal commissioned by the borrower creates an obvious incentive question, however scrupulous the appraiser. Many lenders therefore instruct from their own panel, or will accept an existing report only if the appraiser agrees to address it to them, which makes the lender the client and the appraiser answerable to them.

The practical consequence: if you commission your own appraisal before approaching a lender, expect that it may be informative rather than decisive. It is often still worth having for your own planning, but do not assume the lender will simply adopt it.

How Much Does an Equipment Appraisal Cost?

Appraisal cost scales with effort rather than with the value of the asset: scope, travel, number of units and how obscure the equipment is. A single common machine assessed from records sits at one end; a multi-site inspection of specialised plant sits at the other.

The biggest lever is desktop versus on-site. A desktop appraisal works from serial numbers, specifications, hours and photographs; it is faster and cheaper but conservative, because condition is assumed rather than seen. An on-site inspection costs more and takes longer, and it is worth it when your equipment is in better shape than its age suggests. Above roughly $500,000 of equipment, expect the lender to want a formal third-party appraisal rather than a desktop figure. See desktop vs on-site equipment appraisal.

Two things worth establishing before instructing:

  • Who pays, and whether it is due regardless of whether the loan completes. It usually is; the appraiser did the work either way.
  • Whether the report is portable if you end up with a different lender. Sometimes it can be re-addressed; sometimes it cannot, and that is worth knowing before you pay for it twice.

How Do Appraisers Value Used Equipment?

An appraiser identifies the equipment (make, model, year, hours or mileage), judges its condition and maintenance history, and compares it with recent sales of similar machines. The things that move the number most are age and hours, condition and service records, brand and resale demand, how easily it can be resold, and whether you own it free and clear. A lender-grade report then states three values:

Value Assumes Example
Fair market (FMV)A willing buyer and seller with time to market the asset$200,000
Orderly liquidation (OLV)A sale over a limited but reasonable period$160,000
Forced liquidation (FLV)A quick, auction-style sale$120,000

The example is a contractor's paid-off excavator and skid steer. Lenders advance against the liquidation figures, not fair market value. An equipment refinance often advances 50-80% of forced liquidation value, which on $120,000 is $60,000-$96,000; a sale-leaseback lender working from the orderly liquidation value funded around $130,000-$150,000 on the same machines. See orderly vs forced liquidation value and how appraisal value sets loan-to-value.

How Do You Choose an Equipment Appraisal Company?

Before you hire one, ask:

  • Do you hold a machinery and equipment credential, and will the report be written to USPAP?
  • Have you appraised this type of equipment? Yellow iron, machine tools and medical imaging are different markets.
  • Will the report state fair market, orderly liquidation and forced liquidation value, with an effective date?
  • Desktop or on-site, and what does each cost and take?
  • Can the report be addressed to my lender, or re-addressed if I change lenders?
  • Do you carry professional indemnity cover, and do you have any interest in the sale or the loan?

If you are raising money against the equipment, ask the lender first. Many will name the firms they accept, and that saves you paying for a report twice. For the ways to turn appraised value into cash, see equipment appraisal and equipment financing.

Frequently Asked Questions

What equipment appraisal companies do lenders accept?

Independent firms with no interest in the sale or the loan, whose appraisers hold a machinery and equipment credential and write to USPAP. Many lenders keep their own panel and instruct the appraiser directly, so ask your lender which firms it accepts before you hire one.

How much does an equipment appraisal cost?

It depends on effort, not on the equipment's value: the number of units, travel, how specialised the equipment is, and whether it is a desktop review or an on-site inspection. Desktop appraisals are cheaper and faster; on-site costs more. The fee is usually due whether or not the loan closes.

How do you determine the value of used equipment?

Appraisers look at make, model, year, hours or mileage, condition and maintenance records, and compare recent sales of similar machines. A lender-grade report states fair market, orderly liquidation and forced liquidation value, and lenders lend against the liquidation figures.

Can a dealer's quote be used as an appraisal?

No. A dealer quoting a trade-in is stating what they would pay, which is a bid rather than an independent valuation. Lenders want a documented opinion from someone with no interest in the transaction.

What is USPAP?

The Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice, published by The Appraisal Foundation. Most lender-accepted appraisal work in the United States is written to it, and a report stating USPAP compliance tells a lender what standard it was produced under.

Does a real estate appraiser qualify to value equipment?

Generally not. Machinery and equipment appraisal is a separate specialism with its own credentials, and within it the markets differ — construction equipment, machine tools and medical imaging are not interchangeable expertise.

Can I use an appraisal I paid for myself?

Sometimes, but do not count on it. Many lenders instruct from their own panel, or will accept an existing report only if it is re-addressed to them so they become the client. Your own appraisal is often informative rather than decisive.

What makes a report unusable?

Most often, not naming the basis. A figure that does not say whether it is fair market, orderly liquidation or forced liquidation value cannot be lent against. An undated opinion, or one with no method or comparables shown, has the same problem.
