1. Underestimating the Appraisal—Or Relying on a Number That Has Not Been Verified

Lenders base loan amount and LTV on the lower of purchase price or appraised value. If you're counting on a certain value to hit your target LTV or loan size, and the appraisal comes in low, you may have to bring more equity to the table, renegotiate the purchase price, or lose the deal. Appraisal surprises are one of the leading causes of CRE closing delays and denials.

To reduce risk, do your homework before you go under contract. Review comparable sales and income approach with a broker or appraiser so you have a realistic value range. If the deal only works at the high end of that range, you're taking appraisal risk. Consider an appraisal contingency in your purchase contract so you can renegotiate or walk if the value is short. For SBA 504 or conventional loans, understand the lender’s LTV and minimum DSCR so you know what value and NOI you need. See how much down payment is required for a commercial property loan and what lenders look for in a commercial real estate loan.

2. Debt-Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR) Shortfalls

Lenders require the property’s net operating income (NOI) to cover debt service by a minimum multiple, often 1.20x to 1.35x or higher. If NOI is lower than projected (vacancy, lower rents, higher expenses) or the rate/term increases the debt service, DSCR can fall below the lender’s minimum. That can force a smaller loan, more equity, or a denial.

Model DSCR conservatively. Use realistic rents (not aspirational) and include vacancy and expense reserves. If you're buying a value-add property, understand whether the lender will underwrite to current or stabilized NOI; many conventional and SBA lenders underwrite to current. Lock rate and term as soon as possible so debt service does not move. If DSCR is tight, consider a larger down payment or a different loan structure. See credit score requirements for commercial real estate loans and SBA 504 vs conventional CRE loan for program differences.

3. Lease and Tenant Documentation Gaps

For income-producing property, lenders need to verify leases, rent rolls, and tenant credit. Missing or inconsistent lease documents, expired leases, or tenants in default can slow underwriting or cause the lender to haircut income. Single-tenant properties carry concentration risk; if that tenant has weak credit or a short lease term, the lender may require more equity or deny.

Gather all leases, amendments, and rent rolls before you apply. Ensure they match the rent and term you used in your NOI model. If a key lease is expiring soon, have a renewal or extension in hand or explain the plan. For multi-tenant properties, provide tenant financials or credit information if the lender requests it. Resolve any tenant disputes or arrears before closing. See owner-occupied vs investment commercial property loans for how owner-occupancy affects underwriting.

4. Incomplete or Inconsistent Financial and Entity Documentation

CRE underwriting requires business and personal financials, tax returns, entity documents, and sometimes rent rolls and operating statements. Incomplete or inconsistent documents—revenue that doesn't match tax returns, unsigned operating agreements, or outdated financials—cause underwriting to pause while the lender requests more information. Each round of requests can add a week or more.

Prepare a complete package before submission. Use a checklist from your lender or broker. Ensure entity formation documents are current, all owners with 20% or more are identified, and financials are consistent across tax returns, P&L, and bank statements. Respond to lender requests within 24–48 hours. If you need more time, communicate the date you'll deliver so the lender can plan. For timeline expectations, see how long it takes to close a commercial real estate loan.

5. Title and Environmental Surprises

Title issues (liens, easements, boundary disputes) or environmental concerns (phase I findings, contamination) can delay or kill a closing. Lenders require clear title and often require environmental review. If the title report or phase I reveals problems, you may need to clear liens, obtain endorsements, or remediate environmental issues before the lender will fund.

Order title and, if required, environmental work early in the process. Review the title commitment and phase I as soon as they're available. Resolve issues before the scheduled closing date. If you're on a tight timeline, consider a commercial bridge loan for speed, then refinance once permanent financing is in place; see how fast you can close a commercial bridge loan.

6. New Debt or Credit Events Before Closing

Lenders re-verify credit and debt close to closing. If you take on new debt, miss a payment elsewhere, or your credit score drops, the lender may reassess and reduce the loan, require more equity, or deny. Avoid applying for new credit, making large purchases, or missing payments between application and closing. If you have a material change, disclose it to the lender immediately.

7. Misaligned Timeline and Loan Type

Commercial real estate loan timelines vary. Conventional loans may close in 45–60 days; SBA 504 can take 60–90 days or more. If your purchase contract has a 30-day close and you applied for SBA 504, you'll likely miss the date. Choose a loan type that fits your timeline, or negotiate a long enough closing period to allow for the loan you want. See how long it takes to close a CRE loan and bridge loan vs SBA loan when speed matters.

8. Poor Communication With the Lender

Slow or unclear communication prolongs underwriting. If the lender has to ask multiple times for the same document or cannot reach you, the file stalls. Assign a single point of contact on your side, respond promptly to every request, and provide complete documents the first time. If you cannot meet a deadline, say so and give a new date. Proactive communication keeps the process moving and reduces the chance of a last-minute denial or delay.

Summary: Get Ahead of the Process

Most CRE loan delays and denials stem from appraisal, DSCR, documentation, or title/environmental issues. To avoid them: model value and DSCR conservatively, gather leases and financials upfront, order title and environmental early, and respond quickly to the lender. Avoid new debt or credit events before closing, and match your loan type to your closing timeline. Before you sign, also review CRE loan red flags (recourse, prepayment, balloon, closing costs) so your terms fit your exit plan. Frequently Asked Questions

What usually delays a commercial real estate loan closing?

Common delays include low appraisals, DSCR or debt coverage shortfalls, incomplete or inconsistent documentation, lease or tenant issues, title or environmental problems, and slow borrower or third-party response. Prepare early and respond quickly to lender requests.

Why would a CRE loan be denied at the last minute?

Denial late in the process can happen if the appraisal comes in below need, debt service coverage falls short of the lender's minimum, new material issues appear in title or environmental review, or the borrower's financials or credit change negatively. Lock in key assumptions and avoid new debt or negative events before closing.

How can I avoid CRE loan closing delays?

Order title and environmental early, get a solid appraisal and DSCR run early, gather all financial and entity documents upfront, ensure lease and tenant info is complete and consistent, and respond to lender requests within 24-48 hours. Align your timeline with the loan type (e.g., SBA 504 takes longer than conventional).
