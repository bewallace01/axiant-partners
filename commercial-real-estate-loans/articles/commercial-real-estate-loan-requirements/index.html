Quick Answer: The Core CRE Loan Requirements

  • DSCR / cash flow: many lenders target around 1.20x–1.35x+ (varies)
  • Down payment / equity: driven by LTV, program, and property type
  • Credit profile: often 680+ for many programs, plus clean history
  • Property: acceptable type, appraises well, marketable
  • Liquidity/reserves: funds for closing + post-close cushion
  • Documents: tax returns, financials, statements, and property package

Owner-occupied and investment properties differ. If you are comparing the two, see owner-occupied vs investment commercial property loans.

1) DSCR Requirements (The Main Underwriting Driver)

DSCR (debt service coverage ratio) is often the first number lenders look at. It answers: can the business comfortably make the payment after expenses and existing obligations?

In owner-occupied CRE, DSCR is often derived from the operating business’s cash flow (not just property cash flow). Underwriters may consider:

  • historical cash flow from tax returns and financials
  • add-backs (non-recurring expenses, depreciation, normalized owner comp)
  • global debt payments (business + guarantor obligations)

Many lenders target around 1.20x–1.35x or higher, but it varies by program, property type, and risk. A stronger DSCR can support better leverage, lower pricing, and faster approvals.

2) Down Payment Requirements (Equity Injection)

Down payment requirements are primarily a function of loan-to-value (LTV) and program guidelines. Your down payment also interacts with appraisal outcomes: if a property appraises below the contract price, the effective down payment can increase.

For specific guidance, read how much down payment is required for a commercial property loan. Also note that SBA and conventional programs can differ substantially, which is why many borrowers compare SBA 504 vs conventional CRE loans.

3) Credit Score and Credit Profile Requirements

CRE loans are longer-term obligations, so lenders care about credit discipline. Many programs prefer 680+ for key guarantors, but the full profile matters:

  • payment history (especially the last 12–24 months)
  • utilization and revolving debt
  • public records (liens, judgments)
  • depth and stability of credit

For a focused breakdown, see what credit score is needed for a commercial real estate loan.

4) Property Requirements (Type, Condition, and Marketability)

Lenders are underwriting an asset as collateral. Even with a strong borrower, a weak property can kill a deal. Common property considerations include:

  • Property type: industrial, retail, office, medical, and mixed-use each underwrite differently.
  • Special-use risk: niche/special-purpose properties can require more equity.
  • Condition: deferred maintenance can trigger repairs or holdbacks.
  • Marketability: lenders want collateral they could sell if needed.

Owner-occupied deals have their own rules. Some programs require a minimum owner-occupied percentage; investment properties are underwritten more on property cash flow and leases.

5) Appraisal and LTV Requirements

Appraisal is the lender’s third-party check on collateral value. LTV limits determine how much the lender will loan relative to appraised value. If the appraisal comes in low, you may need to:

  • increase the down payment
  • renegotiate the purchase price
  • change programs or structure

Appraisal timing also impacts closing timelines. If you need timeline expectations, see how long it takes to close a commercial real estate loan.

6) Liquidity and Reserves Requirements

Lenders want to see you have enough liquidity not only for down payment and closing costs, but also for post-closing reserves. Reserves protect the loan against surprises: slow months, unexpected repairs, or temporary revenue disruption.

Reserve expectations vary. Stronger liquidity can sometimes offset borderline DSCR or higher-risk property types. Underwriters may request bank statements, brokerage statements, and a personal financial statement to verify liquidity.

7) Documentation Checklist (What to Prepare)

CRE underwriting is documentation-heavy. A complete package speeds approval and reduces last-minute surprises. Common documents include:

  • Business tax returns: often 2–3 years
  • Personal tax returns: often 2–3 years for guarantors
  • Year-to-date P&L and balance sheet
  • Business bank statements: commonly 3–12 months
  • Debt schedule: all existing obligations
  • Personal financial statement (for guarantors)
  • Purchase contract (or payoff statement for refinance)
  • Entity documents: articles/operating agreement, EIN, ownership
  • Property information: address, use, size, occupancy, rent roll (if relevant)

SBA programs add additional forms and disclosures. Incomplete documentation is one of the main reasons CRE closings get delayed.

Owner-Occupied vs Investment CRE: Requirements Are Not the Same

Many borrowers get tripped up because they assume all commercial real estate loans are underwritten the same way. They are not. In owner-occupied lending, the operating business is usually the main repayment source, so lenders focus heavily on business cash flow and global debt. In investment CRE, the property’s leases and income are often the core repayment source, so lenders focus more on rent roll, leases, occupancy, and property-level cash flow.

If you want the detailed comparison, see owner-occupied vs investment commercial property loans. The practical takeaway: make sure your application clearly states how the property will be used and that the documentation matches the scenario.

Due Diligence Requirements (Appraisal, Environmental, and Property Review)

Commercial property loans typically require due diligence beyond a standard business loan. Depending on lender and property type, you may encounter:

  • Appraisal: establishes value for LTV and collateral underwriting.
  • Environmental review (Phase I ESA): common for many property types to identify contamination risk.
  • Property condition assessment: may be required for older buildings or properties with visible deferred maintenance.
  • Title, survey, and insurance: lender will require clear title, appropriate coverage, and sometimes updated surveys.

These steps are normal and are a major reason CRE timelines are longer than working capital or equipment loans. If you want timing expectations, see how long it takes to close a commercial real estate loan.

Closing Costs and Fee Expectations (What to Budget For)

Beyond the down payment, borrowers should plan for third-party costs and lender fees. Common items include appraisal fees, environmental reports, title and escrow/closing fees, legal review, and sometimes lender origination fees. Costs vary by deal size and property, but budgeting up front helps you avoid last-minute stress and prevents liquidity from becoming a surprise requirement.

Also watch for loan structure terms like prepayment penalties, balloon payments, and recourse requirements. Those terms can matter as much as the interest rate. If you want to avoid the biggest pitfalls, see CRE loan red flags.

Example: How Requirements Combine in a Real Owner-Occupied Deal

Suppose a business is purchasing an owner-occupied warehouse. The lender will look at the business’s cash flow (DSCR), the buyer’s equity and liquidity, and the property’s appraisal. If DSCR is strong but liquidity is tight, the lender may require additional reserves. If the appraisal comes in low, the down payment requirement may increase. If the property is special-use, the lender may reduce LTV even with strong borrower metrics. This is why a clean, complete file is so important: it lets the lender see the full picture and choose the right structure.

Common Approval Blockers (and How to Fix Them)

CRE deals fail for predictable reasons. The most common include:

  • Weak DSCR: cash flow doesn’t support the payment cushion.
  • Appraisal shortfall: value comes in below contract price.
  • Insufficient liquidity: down payment drains reserves too far.
  • Credit issues: recent delinquencies or unresolved public records.
  • Property risk: special-use, location issues, or condition concerns.
  • Documentation gaps: missing or inconsistent financials and entity information.

If you want to avoid the most expensive errors, also read CRE loan mistakes that delay or deny closing and CRE red flags to watch for.

Multi-State Borrowers and Nationwide Lending

Many CRE lenders operate nationwide, but geography still matters because commercial property is local. Underwriters will consider local market liquidity and property comparables. If your business operates across multiple states, you may need to clarify:

  • where the business is registered vs where it operates
  • where revenue is generated and deposited
  • how the property will be used (owner-occupied percentage)

The key is a consistent story across your application, entity documents, and bank statements.

Pre-Application Checklist

  1. Confirm DSCR: estimate the payment at a realistic rate/term and ensure cash flow covers it with cushion.
  2. Budget equity + costs: down payment plus appraisal/environmental/title and reserves.
  3. Assemble documents: tax returns, financials, statements, debt schedule, entity docs.
  4. Clarify occupancy: owner-occupied vs investment and the percentage of use.
  5. De-risk the property: address obvious condition issues and be ready for due diligence.

Doing these steps first is the fastest way to meet commercial real estate loan requirements and avoid delays once appraisal and third-party reports begin.

It also improves your negotiating position: when your financing file is ready, you can move with confidence on timelines, respond quickly to lender questions, and reduce the chances of a last-minute restructure. Speed and certainty close deals. In all markets.

Final Thoughts

Commercial real estate loan requirements are a combination of borrower strength and property strength. If you want the best outcome, focus on DSCR, equity, documentation readiness, and choosing a program that matches your property and use case. When the file is packaged cleanly, CRE financing becomes predictable rather than stressful.

What are the requirements for a commercial real estate loan?

Most lenders evaluate DSCR/cash flow, down payment (equity), credit profile, property type and valuation, liquidity/reserves, and documentation completeness. Owner-occupied loans often have different requirements than investment properties.

What DSCR do you need for a commercial real estate loan?

Many lenders target around 1.20x-1.35x or higher, depending on the program and property. Stronger DSCR can improve leverage and pricing.

How much down payment is required?

Down payment requirements vary by program and property type. SBA structures can be lower in some owner-occupied cases, while conventional CRE commonly requires more equity. The exact amount depends on LTV, appraisal, and risk.

What credit score is needed for a CRE loan?

Many programs prefer 680+ but requirements vary by lender and program. Underwriters also look at payment history, utilization, and public records.

What documents do you need to apply?

Commonly business and personal tax returns, financial statements, bank statements, a debt schedule, purchase contract or payoff, entity documents, and property information. SBA programs add additional forms and disclosures.
