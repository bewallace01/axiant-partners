How Much Money Do You Need to Start a Business?

There is no average that fits your business, but there is a formula that does:

Funding need = launch costs + (monthly expenses × runway months) + contingency buffer

Launch costs are the one-time items you need to open: equipment, opening inventory, buildout, licenses, deposits, tools.

Runway is the money that keeps the doors open while sales ramp up: payroll, rent, software, insurance, and the gap while customers take time to pay.

The buffer covers what goes wrong: a late opening, slower sales, a repair. Ten percent of the first two buckets is a common starting point.

Getting this number right matters more than most founders think. Ask for too much and the payment strains you in the months when revenue is thinnest. Ask for too little and you are back asking for money in six months, usually on worse terms.

How Many Months of Runway Should You Fund?

Most founders fund 3-6 months at first and raise more once sales prove the model. Where you land in that range depends on your cash cycle. If customers pay on 30- or 60-day terms, or the sales cycle is long, plan for the longer end. A business that gets paid at the register can carry less, but still needs the buffer for delays.

If you have no revenue yet, keep the first request tied to opening milestones. Startup loans with no revenue covers what lenders fund at that stage.

How Much of Your Own Money Do You Need?

Expect to fund part of the total yourself. On an SBA 7(a) start-up loan, the minimum equity is 10% of the total project cost, so the $78,650 plan above needs about $7,865 from you. If the equipment is financed on its own, lenders typically ask businesses under two years old for 10-20% down, or a personal guarantor; at 20% on the $18,000 of equipment, that is $3,600.

Splitting the plan this way often works better than one loan for everything: finance the equipment against the equipment (about 12-22% APR under two years in business), and use savings, a personal loan or an SBA microloan of up to $50,000 for the runway.

Can You Afford the Payment? Run the Fit Test

Before you apply, put the payment next to your slowest expected month. If the whole $78,650 were borrowed:

  • 12% APR over 60 months: about $1,750 a month.
  • 19% APR over 36 months: about $2,883 a month.

Build three versions of your first year: expected, conservative and stressed. If the payment breaks the conservative version, shrink the request or phase it. In this example, phase one could cover launch costs plus two months of runway and a 10% buffer, $51,150, which is about $1,138 a month at 12% over 60 months. Phase two comes after the business hits its first milestones. Try other rates and terms in the payment calculator, and see startup financing rates, fees and costs for what drives the rate.

Startup Cost Categories by Business Type

Different businesses have different cost shapes. Use these lists so you do not miss a line:

Service business: tools and equipment, software, payroll ramp, insurance, marketing you can measure, and a receivables buffer if you invoice.

Product or ecommerce: inventory, fulfillment, a returns buffer, packaging, vendor deposits, and marketing with a customer-cost assumption.

Storefront or location-based: buildout, deposits, equipment, opening inventory, staffing ramp, licenses, and a longer buffer for opening delays.

B2B contracts: payroll bridge, onboarding and implementation costs, and a buffer for 30- to 60-day payment terms.

Sizing Mistakes to Avoid

  • Optimistic timing: assuming cash arrives when the sale happens. Model when you get paid, not when you sell.
  • No buffer: one late delivery and the plan breaks.
  • Round numbers: asking for $50,000 or $100,000 with no table behind it.
  • Mixing everything into one ask: equipment, marketing and personal debt cleanup in a single loan.
  • Ignoring payment frequency: some products debit daily or weekly, not monthly. Check before you run the fit test.

How to Present Your Number to Lenders

Lead with the table, not the total. "Here is the breakdown, the timing and the quotes" gets further than "we need $100k." Add a one-page memo on why now and how the payment gets covered, and tie the buffer to specific risks (late receivables, a slower ramp, vendor delays) with a rule for when you will use it. A buffer explained that way reads as risk control. A buffer labeled "extra" reads as padding.

Then package the file with the startup financing application checklist, check any offer against the startup loan red flags, and compare startup funding options by stage. When your number is ready, get matched with lenders that fund new businesses.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much money do I need to start a business?

Add your one-time launch costs, your monthly expenses times the months of runway you want (most founders fund 3-6), and a contingency buffer, often 10%. A service startup with $21,500 in launch costs and $12,500 a month in expenses needs about $78,650 for four months of runway. There is no single number; the formula gives you yours.

How many months of runway should a startup fund?

Most founders fund 3-6 months, then raise more once deposits prove the model. Long sales cycles or slow-paying customers need the longer end. Fast-turnover businesses can carry less, but still need a buffer for delays.

How much of my own money do I need to put in?

Lenders want to see some. On an SBA 7(a) start-up loan the minimum is 10% of the total project cost, so a $78,650 plan needs about $7,865 from you. Equipment lenders typically ask newer businesses for 10-20% down on the equipment.

Should I ask for more than I need?

Usually not. A bigger request means a bigger payment and more skepticism from underwriters. Ask for what your worksheet supports, include a buffer you can explain, and phase anything optional.

What business can I start with little money?

The ones where the launch-cost bucket is close to zero: services you can deliver with tools and skills you already have, run from home before you take on rent. Your first costs are then mostly monthly expenses, which you can keep small until the first customers pay.

Is there a free startup cost worksheet or calculator?

Yes. Copy the worksheet on this page: list each launch cost with its quote, add monthly expenses times your runway months, then add a buffer. To see the loan payment on the total, run it through a payment calculator at a few rates and terms.
