What Are the Main Startup Funding Options?

Most lists boil down to three kinds of money: debt you repay, equity you sell, and grants you win. Your own savings and money from friends and family sit underneath all three, and lenders want to see some of it in the deal. Which of the rest you can actually get depends less on the product than on where the business is: no sales yet, early sales, or steady deposits.

Stage 1: Before Revenue

With no sales, a lender is underwriting you. Expect them to look at your personal credit (680+ is the usual floor, 700+ gets better terms), the cash you are putting in, collateral and how specific the plan is. Asks tied to a specific asset get approved more often than a round number labeled "working capital."

What works here: an SBA microloan for mixed startup costs up to $50,000, equipment financing for the truck, oven or machine (about 12-22% APR and 10-20% down under two years in business, or a personal guarantor), an SBA 7(a) start-up loan for bigger projects, and personal borrowing. On a 7(a) start-up loan you put in at least 10% of the total project cost, so a $300,000 project needs at least $30,000 of your own money. A personal loan ($5,000-$100,000 typical, 8-29% APR over 3-5 years) is approved on your income, which is why it is one of the few tools that works before revenue. Startup loans with no revenue goes deeper on this stage.

Stage 2: Early Revenue (First 6-12 Months)

Once deposits show up in your bank statements, lenders start reading them. At about 6 months, many equipment programs, working capital lenders and merchant cash advance providers will look at you, though working capital lenders commonly want $10,000 or more in monthly revenue. Some fintech lenders open a startup line of credit at 6-12 months with 680-700+ credit, often starting at $5,000-$50,000.

The risk at this stage is overcommitting. Size any payment to your slowest month, not your best one, and be careful with daily-debit products while revenue is still uneven.

Stage 3: Steady Revenue (1-2 Years and Up)

With consistent deposits, the question moves from "can I get funded" to "what is the cheapest structure for this job." Banks usually want 1-2 years or more before opening a line, and standard SBA loans typically want about 2 years. Revenue-based financing ($10,000 to $5,000,000+, funded in about 3-10 days, usually after 6-12 months) fits businesses whose sales swing month to month. This is also the point to refinance anything expensive you took on in stage 1 or 2.

What Kind of Funding Do Startups Get First?

For most small businesses it is the founder's own money plus one small, specific loan: equipment financing, a microloan or a personal loan. Venture capital and angel money go to a small slice of companies built for fast growth, and they cost you ownership. Grants exist but are narrow and competitive, so plan as if you will not get one.

How Much Does a $50,000 Startup Loan Cost?

Two examples inside the 12-22% range newer businesses usually see on equipment loans:

  • 12% APR over 60 months: about $1,112 a month, $16,733 in total interest.
  • 19% APR over 36 months: about $1,833 a month, $15,981 in total interest.

Total interest is close; the payment is not. Early on, the monthly payment is the number that matters, because it comes out of your savings until sales cover it. If you are still working out the amount, how much money you need to start a business walks through the math.

Can a New LLC Get Funding?

Yes. A new LLC can be the borrower, but with no track record the lender looks through to you: your credit, your cash in the deal and a personal guarantee. Forming the LLC helps with liability and starts a business credit file; it does not replace personal credit on the first loan. See what a new LLC can borrow.

Match the Money to the Job

Equipment and vehicles: finance them on their own; the asset secures the loan.

Recurring cash gaps (payroll before receivables land, inventory before a busy season): a line of credit, once statements support it.

Revenue-linked growth: revenue-based financing, where payments rise and fall with sales.

Long-term projects like a buildout or real estate: SBA, with its longer terms and lower payments.

If you would rather not sell shares at all, financing a new business without giving up equity covers the non-dilutive options.

Common Mistakes

  • Applying for products that need revenue you do not have yet. Every decline costs time, and some cost a hard credit pull.
  • Choosing on speed alone instead of whether the payment fits.
  • Using one loan for everything: equipment, payroll and marketing on the same short-term note.
  • Keeping an expensive early structure after the business qualifies for cheaper money.

Moving From One Stage to the Next

Keep a living financing file: current bank statements, formation and ownership documents, a one-page use-of-funds summary and a short note on how the business is performing. Set simple triggers ahead of time, such as "three straight months of stable deposits, then apply for a line of credit." When the trigger hits, you can requalify in days instead of rebuilding the file. The startup financing documents checklist and startup financing requirements list what goes in it. When you are ready, get matched with lenders for your stage, or start from the startup financing overview.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the main startup funding options?

Your own savings, friends and family, equity investors, grants and loans. On the loan side, the ones that work before revenue are SBA microloans up to $50,000, SBA 7(a) start-up loans, equipment financing and personal loans. Lines of credit, working capital loans and revenue-based financing usually wait for 6-12 months of deposits.

What are the three main types of startup funding?

Debt, equity and grants. Debt is borrowed and repaid with interest, and you keep ownership. Equity is cash from investors in exchange for a share of the company. Grants do not have to be repaid, but they are narrow and competitive.

Can a new LLC get funding?

Yes. A new LLC can be the borrower, but with no history the lender looks at the owner, usually wants 680+ personal credit, and asks for a personal guarantee. Equipment financing and SBA microloans are the most common first loans for a brand-new LLC.

When can a startup get a business line of credit?

Some fintech lenders open lines at 6-12 months in business with 680-700+ personal credit and steady deposits, often starting at $5,000-$50,000. Banks usually want 1-2 years or more. Before 6 months, options are narrow unless you have strong collateral.

How much would a $50,000 startup loan cost?

At 12% APR over 60 months, about $1,112 a month and $16,733 in total interest. At 19% over 36 months, about $1,833 a month and $15,981 in interest. Both sit inside the 12-22% range newer businesses usually see on equipment loans.

Should a startup borrow or give up equity?

Borrow when the money buys something that pays it back, like equipment or inventory, and the payment fits your cash flow. Equity makes more sense for long, uncertain build-outs with no payment capacity, but you give up a share of every future dollar. Many founders use both: their own cash and a small loan first, investors later if growth needs it.
