Line of Credit vs Term Loan: Which One Fits Your Business Now

How the two products work, what each one costs on $50,000, and which a newer business can actually qualify for.

← Back to Startup Financing Articles

Updated November 20, 2026

Quick answer

A line of credit is better for repeating, short gaps like payroll before invoices clear: you draw what you need, pay interest only on the balance, and reuse the limit. Lines run about 8-30% APR and usually renew every 12 months. A term loan is better for a one-time cost like a build-out or equipment: you get a lump sum at about 7-25% APR and repay it on a fixed schedule over 1-10 years. For a newer business, the term loan is often easier to get first.

What Is the Difference Between a Line of Credit and a Term Loan?

A term loan gives you one lump sum, and you pay it back in fixed payments over a set term. A line of credit gives you a limit. You draw what you need, pay interest only on what you have drawn, repay it, and the limit opens back up. One is built for a single purchase. The other is built for timing gaps that keep coming back.

That difference changes how the lender looks at you. On a term loan, they size one payment and ask whether you can make it. On a line, they are agreeing to risk you do not control yet, since you can draw the full limit any day. That is why lines usually want stronger credit and steadier deposits, and why a newer business often gets a term loan first.

Which Costs Less on $50,000?

It depends on how long the money stays out. Take $50,000:

  • Term loan at 12% over 36 months: about $1,661 a month. You pay about $5,208 in interest in the first year and $9,786 over the full term, plus any origination fee.
  • Line of credit at 14%, fully drawn all year: about $7,000 in interest for the year, plus a $1,000 draw fee if the lender charges 2%.
  • Same line, averaging $25,000 drawn: about $3,500 in interest for the year, plus draw fees each time you tap it.

The pattern holds at most sizes. If you will use the money once and keep it out for years, the term loan is cheaper. If you draw, repay within weeks and draw again, the line wins, because you only pay for the days the money is out. Add the fees to the rate before you compare: a 14% line with a 3% draw fee can cost more than an 18% line with none if you draw often.

When Does a Line of Credit Make More Sense?

When the need repeats and the cash comes back on its own. You invoice on net-30 or net-60 but pay payroll every two weeks. You restock inventory every quarter and sell through it. You want a cushion for a slow month. In each case you draw, the money cycles back through sales, and you repay.

A line is the wrong tool for something you will pay off over years. If you are using it for a build-out and sitting near the limit for months, you are paying line rates on term-loan money, and a line held above 90% for long stretches is one of the common reasons lenders cut or freeze it at renewal.

When Does a Term Loan Make More Sense?

When you can name the purchase and the amount: a build-out, opening inventory, a vehicle, a second location. The payment is fixed, so you can plan around it, and it is easier to underwrite because the lender knows exactly what the money does.

If the purchase is equipment, look at equipment financing before a general term loan. The equipment secures the loan, so a business under 2 years old can often qualify at about 12-22% APR with 10-20% down or a personal guarantor.

Which Is Easier to Get for a New Business?

Usually the term loan. Lenders typically want 680-700+ personal credit for a startup line of credit. Online lenders will look at 6-12 months in business if your deposits are consistent; banks usually want 1-2 years. Some term loan programs start at 6-12 months in business and $10,000+ in monthly revenue, with 550+ credit accepted and 680+ getting better pricing.

Before 6 months, a general line is hard to get unless you have collateral such as receivables. That is when most founders start with a defined term loan or equipment loan, then add a line once deposits settle. Business lines of credit for startups covers that path in detail.

Can You Use Both?

Yes, and many businesses do. A common order:

  1. Term loan first for the launch or a defined build step, which also starts your repayment history.
  2. Line of credit next, once you have several months of steady deposits, for timing gaps.
  3. Refinance or reprice both as the business gets older and the file gets stronger.

The SBA has its own line of credit program, CAPLines, with lines up to $5 million and terms up to 10 years, for businesses that qualify for SBA lending.

How to Choose in 15 Minutes

  1. Write down the top three things the money will pay for.
  2. Mark each one as one-time or repeating.
  3. Look at the last 3 months of deposits. Are they steady?
  4. Mostly one-time, and you can carry a fixed payment: term loan.
  5. Mostly repeating, and deposits are steady: line of credit.
  6. A mix: start with the term loan, add the line later.

Whichever you pick, compare the full cost: rate, fees, payment timing against when deposits actually land, and how the deal holds up in a slow month. Frequently Asked Questions

Is a line of credit better than a term loan?

Neither is better across the board. A line of credit is cheaper for short, repeating needs because you pay interest only on what you draw. A term loan is usually cheaper and safer for a one-time cost you will pay off over several years, because the rate is often lower and the payment is fixed.

Is a line of credit similar to a loan?

It is a loan, but a revolving one. Instead of one lump sum, you get a limit you can draw against, repay and draw again, and interest runs only on the outstanding balance. A term loan pays out once and you repay it on a fixed schedule.

How long can you keep a line of credit?

Most business lines run for about 12 months and then renew if the lender is happy with your account. At review, a lender can lower or freeze the line, for example if you have sat at 90%+ of the limit for months, filed financials late or broken a covenant.

Is a line of credit harder to get than a term loan?

For a new business, usually yes. Lenders often want 680-700+ personal credit for a startup line, and banks want 1-2 years in business, because they cannot control how the line gets used. Some term loan programs start at 6-12 months in business and 550+ credit, though 680+ gets better terms.

Which is better, a credit card or a term loan?

For a large, one-time cost, a term loan is usually better: about 7-25% APR with a set payoff date. Business credit cards run about 18-29% APR once any intro rate ends and have no set payoff date. A card works for small purchases you will pay off within an intro period.

What is the disadvantage of revolving credit?

The rate is usually variable, draw and annual fees add to the cost, and the lender can cut or freeze the line at review. It also makes it easy to fund long-term costs with short-term money. If you find yourself drawn to the limit for months, a term loan is probably the better tool.
