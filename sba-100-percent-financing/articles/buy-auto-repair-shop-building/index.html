Why a Shop That Already Rents Has an Edge

This guide is for an operating shop buying its own building, the case 100% SBA financing covers. If you are buying the business too, that is a change of ownership with a 10% minimum equity injection; see how to finance buying an auto repair shop.

A tenant shop has three things a lender likes. Its rent history shows it can carry an occupancy cost. It already uses the space, so the occupancy test is easy. And the lender can compare the new mortgage payment with rent the shop is already paying. The question becomes whether your cash flow covers the payment plus the taxes, insurance and repairs a landlord used to absorb.

The Environmental Report Comes First

Under SOP 50 10 8.1, the lender checks the property's current and past uses against the SBA's list of environmentally sensitive industries. Automotive repair and maintenance (NAICS 8111) is on it, so the investigation must begin with a Phase I regardless of loan amount. A Phase I is always required if the property sells or dispenses fuel, even when the use isn't on the list.

  • Who does it. An environmental professional, following the federal All Appropriate Inquiries standard. The report must be dated within one year of the SBA loan number.
  • What it looks for. Waste oil, solvents, old floor drains, oil-water separators, and tanks above or below ground. A long history of repair work on the site matters even if your shop is clean.
  • If it finds something. The lender follows the professional's recommendation, usually a Phase II with soil or groundwater testing. Contamination doesn't end every deal, but it adds reporting to the SBA, time and cost.

Order the Phase I early. It is the item most likely to push a closing date, and you want to know before your purchase contract's inspection period ends.

Lifts, Bays and the Appraisal

An appraisal by a state-licensed or certified appraiser is required, and over $1 million in value the appraiser must be state-certified. The SOP's list of limited or special purpose properties includes service centers with pits and in-ground lifts. A building like that has fewer buyers, which some lenders weigh when deciding how far to go. A shop with surface-mounted lifts and standard bay doors usually appraises as a flex or light-industrial building with a wider market.

Lifts bolted to the floor are usually part of the real estate. New lifts, alignment racks and scan tools are better financed separately: see financing auto lifts and shop equipment.

Zoning and the Rest of the Lot

  • Zoning. Many towns only allow vehicle repair in certain districts or under a special permit. Confirm the use is allowed, or legally nonconforming, and that the permit transfers. A lender won't finance a use the zoning won't support.
  • Parking and storage. Paved parking for customer cars is part of the business. Extra acreage the shop doesn't use is excess land the SBA won't fund.
  • Extra units. In a building you don't fully use, your shop must occupy at least 51% of the rentable space. Our 51% occupancy rule guide shows how that's measured.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does every auto repair building need a Phase I for an SBA loan?

Yes. Automotive repair is on the SBA's list of environmentally sensitive industries, so the environmental investigation must begin with a Phase I regardless of loan size.

Can I buy my shop's building with no money down?

Some SBA 7(a) lenders finance up to 100% for an established shop that occupies the building and has the cash flow to cover the payment. Many still ask for 10% or more.

What happens if the Phase I finds contamination?

The lender usually orders a Phase II test. If contamination is confirmed, the loan can still be made in some cases, but the lender must report it to the SBA and it adds time.

Are in-ground lifts a problem?

Service centers with pits and in-ground lifts are on the SBA's list of special purpose properties. That doesn't make them ineligible, but some lenders are more cautious.

Should the shop equipment go in the same loan?

Fixtures that are part of the building go with it. New equipment is usually financed separately over a shorter term that matches its useful life.
