Working Capital Loans Explained

What working capital financing is, who it's for, and how to use it

← Back to Blog

Last updated: March 3, 2026 · 7 min read · Axiant Partners

Working capital is the money you use to run your business day to day-payroll, rent, inventory, utilities, and other short-term expenses. A working capital loan gives you a lump sum or line of credit to cover these needs when cash flow is tight or seasonal.

Who Needs Working Capital Financing?

Businesses that need to cover gaps between paying expenses and receiving revenue-seasonal businesses, companies scaling up, or those managing growth-often use working capital loans. They're also used for one-time needs like a large order, a short-term opportunity, or an unexpected expense.

Types of Working Capital Financing

Term loans provide a lump sum you repay over a set period with fixed or variable interest. They are ideal when you have a specific, one-time need—for example, funding a large contract, purchasing seasonal inventory, or covering a known cash flow gap. Lines of credit let you draw and repay as needed, so you only pay interest on what you use. They suit ongoing or recurring needs where the exact amount and timing may vary. Invoice financing (accounts receivable financing) advances cash against outstanding invoices—typically 70–90% of invoice value—with the remainder released when customers pay. Each has different terms and use cases; we can help you find the right fit. For a detailed comparison of term loans versus lines of credit, see working capital loan vs business line of credit.

How to Use Working Capital

Common uses include payroll, inventory purchases, rent and utilities, marketing, and covering slow seasons. Lenders typically expect the funds to be used for operational purposes rather than long-term assets. Working capital financing is not intended for purchasing real estate, heavy equipment, or funding business acquisitions—those needs are better served by SBA loans, equipment financing, or acquisition-specific structures. Using working capital for its intended purpose improves approval odds and ensures repayment aligns with your revenue cycle.

Example: Seasonal Retailer

A gift shop generates most of its revenue in Q4. To stock up for the holiday rush, it needs $40,000 in inventory by October but won't see strong cash flow until December. A working capital loan or line of credit lets the owner purchase inventory now, sell through the season, and repay the loan from holiday sales. Without this financing, the business would miss sales or operate with insufficient stock.

Example: B2B Service Provider

A consulting firm wins a large contract that requires hiring two staff and covering 90-day payment terms from the client. Cash flow is tight during the ramp-up period. A working capital loan or line of credit bridges the gap between payroll and contract payment. The firm draws funds as needed, repays from project revenue, and maintains operations without straining existing reserves. This type of use case is common for professional services, contractors, and businesses with delayed payment cycles.

Typical Qualification

Most working capital lenders prefer businesses with 1–2+ years in operation, consistent monthly revenue, and credit scores in the 600+ range. Approval amounts often scale with revenue—commonly 10%–30% of annual revenue depending on structure and risk. For more detail on amounts and credit requirements, see how much you can qualify for and what credit score is needed.

Repayment Structures: What to Expect

Term loans typically require fixed monthly principal and interest payments over 6–24 months or longer. Lines of credit usually charge interest only on the outstanding balance; you can draw and repay as needed during the draw period. Some lenders offer daily or weekly payment schedules for short-term loans. Understanding your repayment obligation before borrowing helps avoid cash flow strain and ensures the structure fits your revenue cycle. Use a loan calculator to estimate monthly payments based on amount, rate, and term.

How Fast Can You Get Funded?

Funding speed depends on the financing type. Business lines of credit and short-term term loans often fund within 5–15 business days for prepared applicants. SBA working capital loans typically take 30–60+ days due to government-backed underwriting. See our guide on how fast you can get a working capital loan for timelines by structure.

When Working Capital Isn't the Right Fit

Working capital financing is built for operational needs—payroll, inventory, and short-term gaps. If you are buying commercial real estate, heavy equipment, or need long-term capital with low monthly payments, alternatives such as SBA loans or equipment financing may be more appropriate. Learn when to choose other options in when a working capital loan is NOT the right option.

Working Capital vs. Other Financing

Working capital loans are for day-to-day operations. If you're buying real estate, use commercial real estate financing. If you're buying equipment, use equipment financing. If you need long-term, low-cost capital, explore SBA loans. For a deeper comparison, read what a working capital loan is and how it works and when a working capital loan is NOT the right option. Matching your need to the right product saves money and ensures appropriate repayment terms.

Rates and Terms Overview

Rates and terms vary by lender, structure, and risk profile. Term loans may offer 6–36 month terms with fixed or variable rates; lines of credit often have 12–24 month draw periods with interest-only payments during the draw. Rates typically range from single digits for strong credits to the mid-teens or higher for moderate profiles. Fees may include origination, documentation, or annual fees.
