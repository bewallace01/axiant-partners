Why Farmers and Ranchers Need Financing That Fits Their Industry

Agricultural businesses operate differently from most other industries. Revenue is seasonal—you incur costs for seed, fertilizer, fuel, and labor during planting and growing, then get paid at harvest or when livestock sells. The lag between spring planting and fall harvest—or between calving and sale—creates cash flow gaps that can stall operations and limit growth. Standard bank loans often don't align with how farmers actually run their businesses.

That's why agriculture-specific financing matters. Lenders who understand the industry evaluate your crop history, acreage, and seasonal cycles—not just static financials. They structure equipment loans around the useful life of tractors and combines, working capital around planting and harvest, and SBA loans for land and barns when you're ready to expand. What Agriculture Financing Can Be Used For

Farmers and ranchers need capital for tractors, combines, inputs, land, and growth. From seasonal operating needs to long-term equipment and land purchases, here are the most common uses—and how we connect you with the right product.

Tractors & Farm Equipment

Tractors, combines, sprayers, hay balers, skid steers, and grain handling. Equipment financing spreads the cost over the asset's useful life instead of tying up cash. Many lenders specialize in ag equipment and understand resale value, hours, and seasonal usage. Typical terms 36-84 months. Browse equipment by type.

Working Capital for Inputs

Seed, fertilizer, fuel, and labor during the growing season when harvest income is months away. Working capital loans bridge the gap between planting and harvest—or between calving and sale. Terms can align with your seasonal cycle. Essential when expanding acreage or managing input costs. Explore farm working capital.

SBA Loans for Agriculture

SBA 7(a) and 504 loans for land acquisition, barns, grain storage, equipment, and working capital. Lower down payments (10% for 504 real estate), longer terms (10-25 years), competitive rates. Use 7(a) for flexibility; 504 for owner-occupied land and buildings. Plan for 30-60+ days. See SBA loans for agriculture for farm eligibility and size limits, or all SBA loans.

Lines of Credit

Revolving credit for inputs, repairs, and off-season needs. Draw when planting or preparing; repay when harvest or livestock sales arrive. Ideal for seasonal cash flow where needs fluctuate. Many farmers use a line alongside equipment and SBA financing. Explore farm line of credit.

Agricultural Real Estate

Purchase or refinance land, barns, grain storage, and agricultural property. SBA 504 and agricultural mortgages build equity while freeing capital for equipment and inputs. Owner-occupied property often qualifies for favorable terms. Stop leasing; build equity instead. Explore farm & land financing.

Business Acquisition

Buy another farm, acquire acreage, or purchase an existing operation. SBA 7(a) loans finance acquisitions—often with as little as 10% down. Seller financing and thorough documentation improve approval odds. Grow through acquisition when organic growth isn't enough. SBA acquisition financing.

What Is a Farm Operating Loan?

A farm operating loan pays for the season: seed, fertilizer, chemicals, fuel and labor, spent in spring and repaid when the crop or the livestock sells. Most are set up as a revolving line, so you draw as each bill arrives and pay interest only on what is out, or as a loan whose payments are timed to harvest rather than to the calendar.

Put numbers on it. A farm planting 800 acres at about $400 an acre in all-in input costs commits roughly $320,000 between planting and mid-season, with little or no revenue until the fall. An operating line sized to that budget carries it, and the harvest pays it back. The seasonal input cost guide walks through that example, and cash flow between harvests covers using a line to hold grain for a better price.

FSA also makes direct operating loans of up to $400,000, aimed at production agriculture and beginning farmers. Commercial lenders and working capital programs fill in above that or when speed matters. Apply to see your options.

Farms can use SBA loans: agriculture is not on SBA's ineligible list. Most farm types must stay under a receipts limit of about $2.25 million to $5.5 million a year, depending on the crop or livestock, or pass SBA's alternative net worth test. Agriculture Financing Requirements

Requirements vary by product and lender. Here's what most agriculture lenders consider:

Equipment financing

600+ FICO opens most equipment lenders; 550-599 goes through specialty programs, and below 550 usually needs 25%+ down or a cosigner. Revenue, time in business, and equipment value matter. New equipment often easier than used. Down payment 0–25% depending on credit and asset.

Working capital

Revenue, cash flow, and time in business. Lenders want to see ability to repay. Crop history and acreage can support approval. Terms vary by lender.

SBA loans

Typically 650–680+ FICO, 2+ years in business, demonstrated profitability. Personal guarantees required. More documentation than equipment-only, but terms (10–25 years, 10% down for 504) often worth it.

Lines of credit

Revenue stability, credit, and existing debt. Revolving structure means ongoing monitoring. Draw as needed; repay when harvest or sales arrive.

Strong operations with clear use of funds and solid documentation typically qualify for favorable terms. Challenged credit? Options exist—terms may differ. Apply now and we'll match you with lenders whose criteria fit your profile.

Agricultural Loan FAQs

What loans do farmers get?

Most farms combine an equipment loan for tractors, combines and implements, an operating loan or line of credit for seed, fertilizer, fuel and labor, and a longer SBA 7(a), SBA 504, FSA or real estate loan for land, barns and grain storage. Amounts typically range from $25,000 to $5,000,000 depending on use and business profile. Apply to see what you qualify for.

How much would a payment be on a $100,000 land loan?

At an illustrative 9.75% rate, about $891 a month over 25 years, $949 over 20 years, or $1,308 over 10 years. The longer term lowers the payment but roughly triples the total interest compared with 10 years.

How many acres do you need for an ag loan?

SBA has no acreage test. It measures farms by average annual receipts, with most crop and livestock limits between about $2.25 million and $5.5 million, so a small operation can qualify. Lenders then size the loan on cash flow, collateral and credit.

What is a farm operating loan?

A farm operating loan covers seed, fertilizer, fuel, labor and livestock costs during the season and is repaid when the crop or animals sell. It is often a revolving line, so you pay interest only on what you draw. FSA makes direct operating loans of up to $400,000.

How fast can farmers get equipment financing?

Equipment lenders usually decide in 24-72 hours and fund in 5-10 business days. SBA loans typically take 30-60+ days. Working capital and lines of credit can fund in days to a few weeks depending on lender and documentation.

Can farmers finance used tractors?

Yes. Many lenders finance both new and used tractors, combines, and farm equipment. Used equipment may have shorter terms (36-60 months) and rates based on age, hours, and condition. Resale value and useful life affect terms. See our guide to used equipment financing.

What credit score do farmers need for agriculture financing?

For equipment, 600+ opens most lenders, 550-599 goes through specialty programs, and below 550 usually needs 25% or more down or a cosigner. SBA loans typically favor 650-680+ credit. Working capital and lines of credit vary by lender.

Can farmers get SBA loans for equipment?

Yes. SBA 7(a) and 504 loans finance tractors, combines, and farm equipment. 7(a) is flexible for general equipment; 504 suits long-lived machinery. Approval typically takes 30-60 days, so if you need equipment faster, an equipment-only loan usually decides in 24-72 hours. Compare SBA vs equipment financing.
