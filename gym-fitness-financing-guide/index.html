Gym Business Loans: How to Finance Buying, Opening or Growing a Gym

SBA, franchise, equipment and working capital financing for gyms, studios and fitness clubs.

Home › Industries › Gym Business Loans

Updated November 20, 2026

Quick answer

Most gym business loans are SBA 7(a) loans: up to $5 million to buy an existing gym or open a franchise, usually over 10 years with about 10% down, and SBA lenders look for 650-680+ credit. The equipment is often financed on its own, with an FMV lease for cardio you'll replace every few years and an equipment loan for strength gear that lasts 10-15 years. A $50,000 equipment loan at 8% over 60 months runs about $1,014 a month.

Lenders treat a gym differently from most small businesses. They want to see member retention, churn and monthly recurring revenue, they know cardio loses value faster than strength equipment, and the franchise brands have lenders that already understand their numbers. Get the structure right for what you're actually financing (buying a gym, opening a franchise, filling the floor or smoothing cash flow) and approval gets much easier. For the program details behind most of these deals, see SBA loans.

How do you get a business loan for a gym?

Start with the use of funds, because it picks the loan for you:

  • Buying an existing gym or opening a franchise: SBA 7(a), up to $5 million. Plan on about 10% down, 650-680+ credit, and 60-90 days from a signed LOI to closing.
  • Filling or refreshing the floor: an FMV lease for cardio, an equipment loan for strength. Equipment lenders approve faster and the gear is the collateral.
  • Buying your building: SBA 504, which can lend up to 90% of the project.
  • Cash flow between billing cycles or a marketing push: a line of credit, or member-revenue-based financing once the gym has a year of steady MRR.

Then put together the file a fitness lender expects: three years of P&Ls and tax returns (or the seller's, on an acquisition), the member roster with retention and churn, MRR from your billing system, an equipment list with values, the lease, and franchise disclosure documents if you're buying into a brand. Member retention through a change of ownership is the number lenders worry about most, so show it plainly.

What does a gym loan cost per month?

On equipment, your credit score sets the rate. Lenders price roughly 7-9% at 720+ with two years in business, 10-13% at 680-719, 13-16% at 640-679 and 16-22% at 580-639, with shorter terms as the score drops. Here is what that means for a $50,000 package, plus the SBA acquisition example below:

How do you finance buying an existing gym?

Most gym purchases combine an SBA 7(a) loan with a seller note. Take a $1.5M independent gym with about 5,000 square feet and roughly 1,200 active members:

  • SBA 7(a): $1.2M (80% of the price) over a 10-year amortization at 10.75%, about $16,361 a month in principal and interest.
  • Seller note: $225K (15%) on full standby for 24 months, then paid over 5 years at 6%.
  • Your cash: $75K (5%). With the standby seller note, that covers the SBA equity requirement.

SBA pricing on these deals usually lands around prime + 2.25-2.75%. Plan a 30-day handover with the seller so members, staff and the billing system all carry over, because losing members in the first months is what hurts a new owner most. The wider playbook for acquisitions is in the buying a business financing guide.

Can you get an SBA loan for a gym franchise?

Yes, and it's the most common route. The big fitness brands have long-standing SBA lending relationships, and lenders already know their unit economics. Typical build-out ranges:

  • Anytime Fitness: $250K-$500K
  • F45 Training: $300K-$500K
  • Orangetheory Fitness: $700K-$1.2M
  • Planet Fitness and Crunch Fitness (full-size): $1.5M-$3M, mostly multi-unit operators
  • 9Round (kickboxing): $200K-$300K
  • Snap Fitness, Pure Barre, CycleBar, StretchLab and other boutique concepts: $250K-$500K

Franchise-focused SBA lenders such as ApplePie Capital, Live Oak Bank and Newtek do most of this volume, alongside general SBA lenders like Wells Fargo and Huntington National.

Should you lease or buy gym equipment?

  • Cardio (treadmills, ellipticals, bikes, rowers): motors and consoles date in 4-6 years. An FMV lease keeps the payment low and lets you hand the machines back and upgrade. Life Fitness, Precor, Matrix and Technogym all offer vendor leasing.
  • Strength (racks, plates, machines, dumbbells): lasts 10-15+ years. Buy it with an equipment loan, own it outright, and take Section 179 or bonus depreciation.
  • Specialty gear (Tonal, Peloton commercial, Pilates reformers, hot yoga): terms are set by the vendor, and some of it is lease-only.

The trade-offs are laid out in equipment leasing vs. a loan.

Member-revenue-based financing

Once a gym has steady recurring revenue, some fintech lenders (Pipe, Capchase, Founderpath and others) will advance against it. They usually want 12+ months open, $50K+ in monthly recurring revenue and 70%+ member retention. Pricing runs about 6-12% effective, repaid as 5-10% of monthly revenue until 1.1-1.3x of the advance is paid back. You give up no equity, and the underwriting leans on MRR stability more than personal credit, which makes it a useful add-on to an SBA loan for an equipment refresh, a membership push or a new location.

Lenders that fund gyms

  • SBA and franchise lenders: ApplePie Capital (Anytime, Orangetheory, F45), Live Oak Bank (franchise and independent gyms), Newtek Small Business Finance.
  • Equipment lenders: Direct Capital, ENGS Commercial Finance, Stearns Bank, and the manufacturers' own finance arms (Life Fitness, Precor, Matrix, Technogym).
  • Recurring-revenue lenders: Pipe, Capchase, Founderpath.

Gym business loan FAQs

How do I get a business loan for a gym?

Decide what the money is for first: buying a gym or opening a franchise usually means an SBA 7(a) loan, and the equipment is often a separate lease or equipment loan. SBA lenders look for 650-680+ credit, about 10% down, and member numbers they can underwrite: retention, churn and monthly recurring revenue. Expect 60-90 days from a signed letter of intent to closing on an SBA acquisition.

Can I get an SBA loan to open a gym franchise?

Yes. The SBA 7(a) program goes up to $5 million and is the usual loan for franchise build-outs. Boutique concepts typically need $250K-$1M and full-size clubs $500K-$2M+, and lenders that know a franchise brand's numbers tend to move faster than general banks.

What is the monthly payment on a $50,000 gym loan?

On an equipment loan, about $1,014 a month at 8% over 60 months and $1,112 at 12% over 60 months. With fair credit, 15% over 48 months is about $1,392 and 19% over 36 months about $1,833. Your rate depends mostly on credit score and time in business.

Should I lease or buy gym equipment?

Lease cardio and buy strength. Treadmills, bikes and ellipticals date in 4-6 years, so an FMV lease keeps payments low and lets you upgrade at the end. Racks, plates and machines last 10-15+ years, so an equipment loan that you own outright, with Section 179 depreciation, usually costs less over time.

How do I finance buying an existing gym?

Most buyers use an SBA 7(a) loan with a 10-year amortization, about 10% equity and pricing around prime + 2.25-2.75%, plus a seller note of 5-25%. The lender will ask for P&Ls showing member retention, the churn rate, the member roster, an equipment list with values, the lease, and proof of your fitness-industry experience.

Can I get a gym loan with bad credit?

For equipment, a 600+ score opens most lenders, 550-599 narrows you to specialty lenders, and below 550 usually takes 25%+ down or a cosigner. SBA loans are harder: lenders there look for 650-680+. An established gym with strong recurring revenue can also look at member-revenue-based financing, which weighs MRR more than personal credit.
