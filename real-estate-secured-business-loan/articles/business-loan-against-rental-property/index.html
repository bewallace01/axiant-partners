Can You Get a HELOC on a Rental Property? Business Loan Options

When a rental HELOC is possible, what a business loan against the rental looks like instead, and what changes when the collateral has tenants

Quick answer

Often, yes, but a HELOC on a rental property is harder to get than one on your home. Expect a lower CLTV ceiling, higher pricing and a closer look at the rent, and not every home equity lender will write one on an investment property. Can you take out a HELOC on a rental property?

Often, yes, with conditions. A HELOC is consumer credit built around the home you live in, and not every home equity lender will go further. The ones that will open a line on an investment property usually set a lower combined loan-to-value (CLTV) ceiling, charge more, and look harder at whether the rent actually arrives. Most HELOCs are also variable rate, with a draw period followed by a repayment period where the payment steps up, and the lender can freeze or cut an undrawn line if the property's value or your credit changes. More on how HELOCs behave in using a HELOC for business.

If you do get one, it has a real advantage over a HELOC on your house: if the business fails, the investment property is at risk, not the home your family lives in. If you can't get one, or the money is for the business anyway, the same equity can be reached two other ways.

A business-purpose loan against a rental is not a HELOC. It's business credit that can sit in second position behind your existing mortgage, and it can be secured by a single-family rental, a multifamily building or commercial property. A DSCR loan cash-out refinance instead pays off the first mortgage and replaces it, so it only makes sense when your current rate isn't worth keeping. The full comparison is in second-position business loan vs. HELOC vs. cash-out refinance. (Axiant arranges business-purpose loans and does not originate or broker home equity loans.)

How much can you borrow against a rental property?

Take a rental appraised at $400,000 with a $200,000 mortgage, so you're at 50% CLTV today. A second-position lender capped at 65% CLTV leaves room for about $60,000; one that goes to 80% leaves about $120,000. A cash-out refinance at 75% LTV would put a $300,000 loan on the property and, after paying off the old $200,000, hand you about $100,000 before costs, at the new rate on the whole balance. In practice the rent often caps the loan before the CLTV does, which is the next test.

Why Lenders Like Tenanted Collateral

A rental is unusually good security. It has a market value like any property, and unlike a warehouse or an owner-occupied building it produces cash on its own.

That means a lender is not relying solely on a sale to recover. The property services debt while it is held, which lowers the risk and usually shows up in the pricing relative to unsecured business credit.

It is also why the rent gets underwritten rather than assumed. The question is not just what the property is worth; it is whether the income still covers everything secured against it once your new loan is added.

The arithmetic is the same one covered in how DSCR is calculated, applied here with your business loan added to the denominator. For scale, DSCR lenders on 1-4 unit rentals commonly want a ratio of 1.00 to 1.25, and commercial lenders 1.20 to 1.35.

The Risk Nobody Prices at Signing

This is the part worth sitting with.

Before the loan, the rental and the business are separate. The property has its own debt, its own income, and its own risk. If the business fails, the rental carries on.

After the loan, they are connected. A business failure now reaches the property, and the tenant's rent is servicing debt that a failing business took on. Owners who built a rental portfolio deliberately separate from an operating company can undo that separation with one signature.

That is not an argument against doing it. It is an argument for doing it consciously, and for asking whether the business need is worth the connection.

What Lenders Ask For

  • The lease, signed, for each unit
  • Evidence the rent arrives, usually bank statements showing deposits
  • The mortgage statement and exact payoff on existing liens, and the mortgage itself: many investment and commercial mortgages prohibit a second lien or need the first lender's consent, and the federal protection for adding a junior lien only covers owner-occupied homes of one to four units
  • Insurance with the new lender named, in the right entity's name
  • Entity documents if the property is held in an LLC; see holding title in an LLC
  • The business case, because this is business borrowing even though the collateral is property

When Something Else Fits Better

Pledging a rental is not automatically the cheapest route, and it is rarely the most reversible.

If the need is short and the business has revenue, unsecured working capital keeps the property out of it entirely — more expensive per dollar, but the failure mode is smaller. If the need is a property purchase rather than operating capital, a DSCR loan on the new property may be the better instrument. If it is equipment, the equipment can secure itself.

Reach for property equity when the cost difference genuinely matters and the need is defined, not because it is the largest number available. If you hold equipment as well as rentals, see what you sign in a cross-collateralized loan, and for the whole program see real-estate-secured business loans.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I get a HELOC on a rental property?

Often, yes, but not every home equity lender will open a line on an investment property, and the ones that do usually set a lower CLTV ceiling, charge more and look closely at the rent. For business money, a business-purpose second-position loan or a DSCR cash-out refinance on the rental are the usual alternatives.

Is a business loan against a rental property the same as a HELOC?

No. A HELOC is consumer credit, usually on your home, with Truth in Lending disclosures and a right to cancel. A loan against a rental for a business purpose is business credit: it can be secured by rental, multifamily or commercial property and is sized on combined loan-to-value, commonly capped at 65-80%.

Can I use a rental property as collateral for a business loan?

Generally yes, and lenders often prefer it to unsecured lending because the collateral produces income. The property has to keep covering everything secured against it once your new loan is added.

Does the rent have to cover the new loan too?

Yes. Lenders test net operating income (rent after vacancy, management, taxes and insurance) against total debt service including the new lien, alongside the combined loan-to-value across all liens on the property.

Will a second lien violate the mortgage on my rental?

It can. The federal rule that stops a lender calling a loan over a junior lien covers owner-occupied homes of one to four units, not investment property. Many investment and commercial mortgages prohibit additional liens or require the first lender's consent, so read yours before you apply.

What happens to the rental if the business fails?

It is exposed, which it was not before. Pledging the property connects two balance sheets that were separate, so a business failure can now reach an asset that would otherwise have carried on independently.

Is this cheaper than an unsecured business loan?

Usually on rate, because the lender has real security. Whether it is cheaper overall depends on what you are risking: unsecured borrowing costs more per dollar but keeps the property out of the question entirely.

Can I do this if the property is in an LLC?

Normally yes. The lender will want the entity documents, evidence of good standing, authority for whoever signs, and insurance in the entity's name. Expect a personal guarantee regardless of the entity.
