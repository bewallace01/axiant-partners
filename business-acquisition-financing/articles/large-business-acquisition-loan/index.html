Business Acquisition Loans From $1 Million to $5 Million: What Changes

Bigger purchases trip extra SBA rules: a Quality of Earnings report, a collateral shortfall that reaches your house, life insurance, and a $5 million ceiling

← Back to Business Acquisition Financing

Updated November 13, 2026

Quick answer

On a $1 million to $5 million purchase, SBA 7(a) rules add weight. A Quality of Earnings report is required at a $3 million purchase price, goodwill counts for nothing as collateral, so lenders must take equity in owners' personal real estate, and life insurance can be required. 7(a) stops at $5 million. What Changes as the Price Goes Up

SOP 50 10 8.1 applies the same Appendix 15 rules to a $400,000 purchase and a $4 million one, but several of them only bite at size:

Purchase priceWhat the SOP addsWhat lenders commonly add
Over $350,000Business valuation from an accredited appraiser, ordered by the lenderThree years of financials, ideally CPA-reviewed
$1M-$3MCollateral shortfall rules start to reach personal real estateMore cash or seller debt, a key-person plan
$3M and upQuality of Earnings report with a cash proof (Initial Acquisition and Business Expansion)Covenants, larger seller notes, a line for working capital
Over $5M7(a) maximum is $5 millionA pari passu bank loan, conventional debt or other structures

The $3 million QoE threshold is measured on the Business Purchase Price, excluding owner-occupied real estate, before any equity or seller debt. It's commissioned for the lender, reconciles the statements, tax returns and IRS transcripts, and its earnings figure is the one used for coverage.

Why the Collateral Shortfall Lands on the Owners

Most of a service company's price is goodwill, which isn't a fixed asset. The SOP defines "fully secured" using discounted values:

AssetValue counted toward fully secured
Used equipmentMachinery and equipment: 50% of net book value, or 80% of an orderly liquidation appraisal
Improved real estate85% of market value
Unimproved real estate50% of market value
Furniture and fixtures10% of net book or appraised value
Receivables and inventory10% of current book value

When a loan isn't fully secured, the lender must take available equity in personal real estate solely owned by co-borrowers, 20%+ owners and most guarantors, including investment property. The lien can be limited to the shortfall or to 150% of the equity, and a property with less than 25% equity doesn't have to be pledged.

Worked Example: A $3,500,000 Purchase

Illustrative, not a quote. A buyer acquires a distribution company for $3,500,000 with 10% equity (fees and costs left out to keep the math clear), so the 7(a) loan is $3,150,000. The business has $600,000 of used equipment at book, $400,000 of receivables, $300,000 of inventory and $100,000 of furniture and fixtures.

The price is above $3 million, so a QoE is also required. If the business depends on this one owner, life insurance is required for the shortfall amount, as the next section explains.

Life Insurance, Seller Notes and Reserves

  • Life insurance. On a standard 7(a) loan that isn't fully secured, the SOP requires life insurance in the amount of the collateral shortfall for a sole proprietor, a single-member LLC or any business dependent on one owner. The policy is collaterally assigned to the lender, an existing policy can be pledged, and credit life or whole life shouldn't be required. The lender's memo must say who is insured and for how much, or why none.
  • Bigger seller notes. Lenders often ask sellers of larger companies to carry more, both to cut the loan and to keep the seller invested. Only a note on full standby counts as equity; see the seller note rules.
  • Working capital lines. The SOP lets a line of credit hold a first lien on the acquired receivables and inventory if 20% to 50% of its day-one availability is used to fund part of the price.
  • Reserves. Lenders commonly want post-closing liquidity on top of the equity, and the cash flow test is run on the lower QoE earnings.

Deals Over $5 Million

A single 7(a) loan tops out at $5 million. Larger purchases usually pair it with another lender. The SOP bans piggyback structures, where another loan made within 90 days takes a senior lien over the SBA loan. A shared lien with the bank (pari passu) is allowed if the other loan's maturity isn't shorter than the SBA loan's. Banks also face lending limits: under 12 CFR 32.3 a national bank can generally lend one borrower 15% of its capital and surplus, so big deals often need a participation. Frequently Asked Questions

Can you get a $5 million SBA loan to buy a business?

Yes, $5 million is the 7(a) maximum, and acquisitions can use it. At that size expect a Quality of Earnings report, an accredited valuation and a close look at collateral and coverage.

When does the SBA require a Quality of Earnings report?

For Initial Acquisitions and Business Expansions with a Business Purchase Price of $3 million or more, excluding owner-occupied real estate, under SOP 50 10 8.1.

Will the lender take a lien on my house to buy a business?

Often, on larger deals. If the loan isn't fully secured, the SOP requires the lender to take available equity in personal real estate of 20%+ owners, unless the equity is under 25% of its value.

Is life insurance required on an SBA acquisition loan?

On a standard 7(a) loan that isn't fully secured, yes for a sole proprietor, single-member LLC or business dependent on one owner, in the amount of the collateral shortfall. Otherwise lender policy applies.

How do you finance an acquisition over $5 million?

Usually by pairing a 7(a) loan with a bank loan that shares the lien and has at least as long a maturity, or with conventional, seller or equity financing.

Why do lenders want a bigger seller note on larger deals?

It lowers the loan, keeps the seller invested in the transition and covers part of the collateral gap. To count as equity it must be on full standby.
