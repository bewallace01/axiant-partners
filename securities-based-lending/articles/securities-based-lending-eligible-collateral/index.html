Before you count on borrowing against your portfolio, it's worth knowing that lenders don't treat every holding the same—and some don't count at all. What qualifies, and at what advance rate, drives how much you can actually borrow. This page lays out eligible and ineligible collateral and how advance rates work, so your expectations match reality. For the dollar amounts that result, pair it with how much you can borrow with SBL.

What Usually Qualifies

Lenders favor assets that are liquid, easy to value, and stable. Commonly eligible collateral includes:

  • Government securities—Treasuries and agency bonds, the gold standard for collateral.
  • Investment-grade bonds—high-grade corporate and municipal bonds.
  • Blue-chip and large-cap stocks—widely held, liquid equities.
  • Exchange-traded funds (ETFs)—diversified, liquid, easy to price.
  • Many mutual funds—broad-based funds held in eligible accounts.

All of this generally needs to sit in a taxable brokerage account that can be pledged to the lender.

What's Usually Excluded or Restricted

  • Retirement accounts. IRAs, 401(k)s, and similar tax-advantaged accounts are typically not eligible.
  • Restricted or control stock. Shares subject to trading restrictions or affiliate rules are usually ineligible or heavily limited.
  • Low-priced or thinly traded securities. Hard-to-value, illiquid names are excluded.
  • Options and certain derivatives. Generally not accepted as collateral.
  • Concentrated single-stock positions. Allowed only up to limits, at reduced advance rates.
  • Annuities and private shares. Typically ineligible.

If your wealth is mostly in a retirement account or a single restricted holding, SBL may not be available—worth knowing before you plan around it. See the risks of securities-based lending for why lenders draw these lines.

How Advance Rates Vary by Asset Class

The advance rate is the percentage of an asset's market value you can borrow against. It scales with how stable and liquid the asset is:

Advance rates generally scale with stability (illustrative)
Asset class Relative advance rate Why
Treasuries / govt securitiesHighestMost stable and liquid
Investment-grade bondsHighLow volatility, predictable
Diversified equity funds / ETFsModerateLiquid but market-exposed
Individual blue-chip stocksModerateSingle-name risk
Concentrated / volatile positionsLow or noneHigher risk, concentration limits

Two practical takeaways: a diversified portfolio borrows more than the same value concentrated in one name, and your blended advance rate reflects your whole mix. Exact percentages are set by each lender and move with market conditions.

Concentration Limits

Even with an eligible single stock, lenders cap how much of your collateral one position can represent—a concentration limit. A large holding in one name is riskier collateral, so it earns a lower advance rate and may be capped as a share of the portfolio. This is one of the key SBL traps to plan around, because concentration also raises your margin-call exposure if that one stock falls.

Frequently Asked Questions

What securities can you borrow against with an SBL?

Lenders generally accept marketable, liquid securities in a taxable brokerage account: blue-chip stocks, ETFs, many mutual funds, corporate and municipal bonds, and government securities like Treasuries. The more liquid and stable the asset, the higher the advance rate. Each lender publishes its own eligible-collateral list.

What securities are not eligible as collateral?

Commonly excluded: retirement accounts (IRAs, 401(k)s), restricted or control stock, low-priced or thinly traded securities, options and certain derivatives, and highly concentrated single-stock positions beyond a lender's limit. Annuities and privately held shares are also frequently ineligible. Check the specific lender's rules.

How are SBL advance rates determined?

The advance rate is the percentage of an asset's value you can borrow against, and it varies by asset class and stability. Government securities and high-grade bonds typically carry the highest advance rates, diversified funds and blue-chips a moderate rate, and volatile or concentrated positions a lower rate or none. Diversification improves overall capacity.

Can I borrow against a concentrated single-stock position?

Sometimes, but with limits. Lenders apply concentration limits because a single large holding is riskier collateral, so the advance rate is usually lower and the position may be capped as a share of the portfolio. A diversified portfolio supports more borrowing capacity than the same value in one name.

Can I borrow against my IRA or 401(k)?

Generally no. Retirement accounts like IRAs and 401(k)s are typically not eligible as SBL collateral due to their tax-advantaged structure and rules. Securities-based lending usually relies on assets in a taxable brokerage account. If retirement assets are your main holdings, SBL may not be available and you should explore other financing.
