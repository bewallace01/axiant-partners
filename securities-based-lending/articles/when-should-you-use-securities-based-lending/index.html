Securities-Based Line of Credit: When an SBLOC Makes Sense and When It Doesn't

How an SBLOC works, how it compares with margin and business lines, and the cases where it backfires.

Home › Securities-Based Lending › Articles › Securities-Based Line of Credit

Updated November 20, 2026

Quick answer

A securities-based line of credit (SBLOC) is a revolving, usually interest-only line secured by the stocks, ETFs, mutual funds or investment-grade bonds in your brokerage account. Lenders typically advance 50-75% of eligible value, so a $1 million diversified portfolio at 60% supports about $600,000. Lines start around $10,000 and are priced at SOFR plus a spread. Use one for short- to medium-term cash without selling: a bridge, a tax bill, a business deal. Avoid it if your portfolio is concentrated or you would borrow near the limit, because a market drop can force a sale.

What is a securities-based line of credit?

A securities-based line of credit, often shortened to SBLOC, is a line of credit secured by the investments in your brokerage account. You pledge the account instead of selling it. The lender looks at what you hold, sets an advance rate, and gives you a limit you can draw on and repay as you go. Your shares stay invested and keep paying dividends.

Most lines are revolving and interest-only, priced at a short-term benchmark such as SOFR plus a spread that narrows as the pledged portfolio grows. There is no fixed amortization, so the cost is simply the rate times the balance for as long as you carry it. For step-by-step mechanics, see how to borrow against stocks; for current pricing, see typical SBL rates.

How is an SBLOC different from a margin loan or a business line?

Feature SBLOC Margin loan Business LOC
Collateral Stocks, ETFs, funds, investment-grade bonds Securities in a margin account Receivables, inventory or other business assets (if secured)
What the money can fund Broad business and liquidity uses Typically buying more securities Business operations
How it is set up Separate credit line against the pledged account Margin inside the brokerage account Credit line from a business lender
Underwriting Collateral value, diversification and LTV; light income review Brokerage-driven Revenue, credit, time in business
Size From $10,000 to $10 million+ Set by the brokerage Secured lines often $250,000 to $2 million+

The SBLOC is the one to look at when the cash is not for buying more stock and you would rather not pledge business assets or go through full business underwriting. If your company has strong receivables, a business line of credit avoids market risk entirely.

When should you use a securities-based line of credit?

  1. You need cash and selling would trigger a big gain. Borrowing is not a sale, so you keep the position and its upside. More on that in SBL for tax planning.
  2. You need a short-term bridge. Real estate deposits, a business purchase, a tax bill or a private placement, often paired later with a bridge loan, a term loan or SBA financing.
  3. Revolving access matters more than a fixed payment schedule.
  4. You hold a concentrated position as a founder or executive and selling a large block would move the price or trigger taxes. Expect a lower advance rate on that stock.
  5. Your portfolio is strong but your income is irregular. Underwriting leans on collateral, not tax returns and debt coverage.
  6. The investment plan should stay intact, whether that is long-term growth, dividend income or a set allocation.

Example: a $3,000,000 portfolio at a 60% advance rate supports about $1,800,000. The owner draws part of it for a property purchase, keeps the portfolio invested, and refinances the property with conventional financing later. See securities-based lending for real estate.

When is a securities-based line of credit a bad idea?

  • Your portfolio is concentrated in one stock or sector, or it is highly volatile.
  • The plan is to borrow near the maximum advance.
  • Heavy existing debt, or no cash or other assets to cover a collateral call.
  • The money is for a long-term need that fits amortizing debt better.

Here is what a call looks like. A $1,000,000 portfolio with a 60% line has a $600,000 limit, and you have drawn $500,000. The market drops 25% and the portfolio is worth $750,000. The most the lender will lend against it is now $450,000, so you owe about $50,000 more than the collateral supports. The usual window is 24-72 hours to add collateral or pay it down, or the lender sells shares, possibly at the bottom and with the gains you were trying to defer. Borrowing well under the limit is the single best protection. More in the risks of securities-based lending.

How much can you get?

Advance rates typically run 50-75% of eligible value, with diversified, investment-grade portfolios at the top and concentrated holdings at the bottom. Lines start around $10,000 and run to $10 million or more. For worked examples at different portfolio sizes, see how much you can borrow, or start at the securities-based lending page.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a securities-based line of credit?

It is a revolving line of credit secured by the investments in your brokerage account, such as stocks, ETFs, mutual funds and investment-grade bonds. Lenders typically advance 50-75% of eligible value. Most lines are interest-only with a variable rate, and your investments stay invested.

How does a securities-based line of credit work?

You pledge the account, the lender sets an advance rate, and you draw and repay like any line of credit, paying interest only on the balance. There is no fixed amortization. If the portfolio falls and the loan-to-value crosses the limit, you get a collateral call and usually 24-72 hours to add collateral or pay down the line.

Is a securities-based line of credit a good idea?

It can be when you need cash for a short to medium period and selling would trigger a large capital gain or break a long-term plan. It is a poor fit for a concentrated or volatile portfolio, a long-term need better suited to amortizing debt, or borrowing near the maximum.

What is the difference between an SBLOC and a margin loan?

Margin loans sit inside a brokerage account and are typically used to buy more securities. An SBLOC is a separate line against the pledged account and can fund broader business and personal liquidity needs. Both carry collateral-call risk when markets fall.

How much can you borrow with an SBLOC?

Typically 50-75% of eligible portfolio value, with concentrated or volatile holdings at the low end. A $3 million diversified portfolio at 60% supports about $1.8 million. Lines start around $10,000 and run into the millions.

What is a secured business line of credit?

It is a business line backed by company assets such as receivables or inventory rather than an investment portfolio. Secured business lines often run $250,000 to $2 million or more, carry lower rates than unsecured lines, and require a UCC filing. An SBLOC uses your portfolio as collateral instead.
