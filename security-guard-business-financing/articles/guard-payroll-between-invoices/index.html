Wages weekly, invoices at sixty days - what payroll funding typically costs, how much your payroll needs, and how to set it up before the next pay run

Why This Industry Feels It Worse

Almost every service business waits to be paid. Guard companies have the sharpest version of it, for three reasons that compound.

Labour is nearly the whole cost. There is little material spend to defer and no inventory to run down. When cash is short, the thing you cannot delay is the thing you mostly buy.

Wages are legally time-bound. Payroll is not a supplier who can be asked to wait. Federal wage and hour obligations under the Fair Labor Standards Act apply regardless of whether the client has paid you.

Clients are slow by convention. Property managers, facilities firms and public bodies pay on net-30 to net-60 as standard, and it is rarely negotiable for a smaller vendor.

Factoring dominates here for a structural reason: it underwrites your client's ability to pay rather than yours. A young guard company billing a well-rated property manager can often factor when it could not get a line of credit.

Payroll Funding or Payroll Financing? Same Product, Two Names

Funders use both names, and searchers do too. In the guard business they nearly always describe the same thing: money advanced against invoices you have already issued, timed so the wage run is covered. The difference that matters is not the name but the structure underneath it:

If the contract saysWhat it isHow it is priced
You sell or assign the invoiceFactoring (payroll funding)A fee per invoice, rising the longer it stays unpaid
You borrow against all receivablesAccounts receivable lineInterest on the balance you draw
You borrow against your next pay runPayroll advance or loanA fixed fee or factor; ask for the APR

Ask any provider which of the three you are signing. The same headline number, say 3%, is cheap as a fee on a 30-day invoice and very expensive as a fee on a one-week advance. See accounts receivable financing for the second structure.

What moves the fee. Slow-paying clients cost more, because the fee grows with how long the invoice is outstanding; so do small invoice volumes and concentration in one client. Recourse funding, where you buy back an invoice the client never pays, usually costs less than non-recourse, where the funder absorbs that credit loss. Worked example (illustrative). A guard company bills a property manager $60,000 at month end on net-45 terms. At an 85% advance it receives $51,000 within days of invoicing, which covers the next payroll runs. If the fee works out at 3% of the invoice, $1,800, the funder releases the remaining $7,200 once the client pays. The numbers are an example, not a quote; your advance and fee depend on your clients and volume.

Worked Example: How Much Funding Your Payroll Needs

A simple way to size the gap: multiply your weekly payroll, including taxes and workers’ comp, by the weeks between a guard’s first shift and the day the client pays for it. With monthly billing in arrears that is about 4.3 weeks of billing period plus the payment terms. The table does the multiplication (rounded to the nearest $1,000):

The last column is the point of payroll funding. Once each invoice is advanced the day it is issued, you only carry the month you have not billed yet, plus the 5-20% the funder holds back until the client pays. Billing every two weeks instead of monthly roughly halves that column again, where the contract allows it. This is a planning estimate, not a credit limit; a funder sizes the facility on your invoices and your clients.

Growth Makes the Gap Wider, Not Narrower

The counter-intuitive part, and the thing that catches owners out.

Winning a new contract means staffing posts immediately. Wages start in week one. The first invoice goes out at month end and pays perhaps sixty days later. So a new contract consumes cash for months before it produces any, and the bigger the win, the larger the hole.

A company growing quickly can be profitable on paper and unable to make payroll, which is a different problem from being unprofitable and has a different fix. Financing growth is reasonable; financing a loss is not, and the two look similar on a bank statement for a while.

What Funders Look At

Because factoring underwrites the receivable, the checks are different from a conventional loan:

  • Who your clients are. Their credit, not yours, carries most of the decision.
  • Concentration. One client at most of your revenue is a risk, however good that client is.
  • Contract terms. Clean payment terms and who you send the notice of assignment to. Under UCC 9-406 a clause banning assignment of a receivable is generally ineffective, but many funders still pass on a contract that has one, and federal contracts follow the Assignment of Claims Act instead.
  • Billing discipline. Invoices that go out late, or that clients dispute, undermine the whole structure.
  • Existing liens. A blanket UCC filing from an earlier lender can capture your receivables and has to be subordinated first.

Read the assignment clause in your contracts before you apply. It rarely makes factoring impossible, but it is a common reason a funder slows down or says no.

How to Set Up Payroll Funding Before the Next Pay Run

  1. List every client with its payment terms, average monthly billing and how late it really pays.
  2. Pull your contracts and flag any assignment clause or government client, since those change the paperwork.
  3. Run a UCC search on your company. An earlier lender with a blanket lien has to release or subordinate the receivables first.
  4. Apply with an aging report and recent invoices. The funder checks your clients’ credit more closely than yours.
  5. Send notices of assignment so clients pay the funder directly, or agree a different payment route if the funder allows it.
  6. Submit the first invoices two or three business days before payroll, so the first advance lands in time.

Setup commonly takes a few business days; after that, invoices often fund within 24 hours. If you need cash before the account is live, a line of credit or a short working capital loan can carry one pay run.

Fixing the Cause as Well as the Symptom

Financing the gap is sensible. Narrowing it is cheaper, and several levers are within your control:

  • Invoice immediately. A week's delay in billing is a week added to every payment term, at your cost.
  • Bill more often. Fortnightly rather than monthly billing halves the average wait where the contract permits it.
  • Get terms right at contract stage. Payment terms are negotiable before signature and almost never afterwards.
  • Chase early. Most late payment is administrative rather than deliberate — a missing purchase order number, an invoice sent to the wrong address.
  • Watch concentration. It affects both your risk and your access to funding.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does security guard payroll funding cost?

Commonly about 1-5% of invoice value, with the advance commonly 80-95% of the invoice. Slow-paying clients, small volumes and reliance on one client push the fee up. These are typical ranges across funders, not a quote.

Why do security guard companies struggle with payroll?

Because labour is nearly the entire cost, wages are legally time-bound, and commercial and municipal clients pay on net-30 to net-60 as standard. There is no material spend to defer and no inventory to run down, so the one cost you cannot delay is the one you mostly have.

What is payroll funding for guard companies?

Invoice factoring structured around the payroll cycle, sometimes with payroll administration attached. The funder advances against issued invoices and is repaid when your client pays, which aligns the money with the wage run rather than the billing cycle.

Can a new guard company get factoring?

Often yes, and this is the main reason factoring dominates the sector. It underwrites your client's ability to pay rather than your trading history, so a young company billing a well-rated property manager can frequently factor when it could not get a line of credit.

Why does winning a big contract make cash worse?

Because you staff the posts immediately and bill in arrears. Wages start in week one, the first invoice goes out at month end, and payment arrives perhaps sixty days after that. The bigger the contract, the larger the hole before any revenue arrives.

What can stop me factoring my invoices?

Most often an existing blanket UCC filing from an earlier lender that already captures your receivables, or a client contract with an assignment clause. Under UCC 9-406 that clause is generally ineffective, but some funders still decline the contract, and federal contracts follow the Assignment of Claims Act. Both are checkable before you apply.

Is payroll financing the same as payroll funding?

For guard companies, usually yes: both names describe advances against invoices you have issued, timed to the wage run. Check the contract. If you are selling invoices it is factoring, priced per invoice; if you are borrowing against your next pay run it is a loan, and you should ask for the APR.

How much payroll funding does a guard company need?

Roughly your weekly payroll times the weeks from first shift to client payment. With monthly billing on net-45 terms that is about 10.8 weeks, so a $25,000 weekly payroll ties up about $269,000. Advancing each invoice when issued cuts the gap to about the 4.3-week billing month.
