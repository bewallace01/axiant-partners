What Is a Business Line of Credit?

A business line of credit is a revolving credit facility that provides access to funds up to a set limit. Instead of receiving a lump sum, you:

  • Draw only what you need
  • Pay interest only on the amount used
  • Reuse available credit as you repay

Example: If approved for a $500,000 working capital line of credit, you might draw $75,000 to cover inventory and later draw $10,000 for payroll-as long as you remain within the limit. This revolving structure is ideal for recurring or unpredictable capital needs. If exploring structured options for established businesses, reviewing a commercial business line of credit solution can provide clarity on eligibility and terms.

What Is a Term Loan?

A term loan provides a one-time lump sum disbursement with a fixed repayment schedule. Key characteristics:

  • Full amount funded upfront
  • Fixed monthly payments
  • Defined repayment term
  • No revolving access

Typical uses for term loans: equipment purchases, business acquisitions, expansion projects, real estate, and large one-time investments. Once funds are disbursed and repaid, the loan is closed.

Key Differences Between a Line of Credit and a Term Loan

  • Funding Structure: Line of credit = revolving; Term loan = lump sum
  • Interest: Line of credit = on amount drawn; Term loan = on full loan amount
  • Flexibility: Line of credit = ongoing access; Term loan = one-time use
  • Best For: Line of credit = operating capital; Term loan = fixed investments
  • Reuse: Line of credit = yes; Term loan = no

The choice depends on whether your business needs ongoing liquidity or project-based financing.

When a Business Line of Credit Is the Better Option

A working capital line of credit is often ideal when:

  • Revenue fluctuates seasonally
  • Receivables create temporary cash flow gaps
  • Inventory purchases are recurring
  • Payroll cycles require liquidity management
  • Short-term opportunities arise

Because you only draw funds when needed, it prevents unnecessary interest costs and allows for efficient capital management. Established companies with consistent revenue often use a line of credit as a liquidity cushion rather than a one-time funding solution.

When a Term Loan Makes More Sense

A term loan may be the better structure if:

  • You're purchasing fixed assets
  • You're funding a defined expansion project
  • You need a specific lump sum
  • Repayment schedule certainty is preferred

Equipment purchases are typically better suited for structured term financing rather than a revolving line. If evaluating fixed asset purchases, exploring equipment financing or other options aligned with long-term investments may be more appropriate.

Cost Differences: Line of Credit vs Term Loan

Line of Credit: Interest applies only to the amount drawn. Rates may vary based on structure. Some facilities include draw fees.

Term Loan: Interest applies to the full disbursed amount. Fixed amortization schedule. Predictable repayment timeline.

While term loans may sometimes appear lower in rate, they may cost more overall if you don't need the full capital amount immediately. Evaluating total capital efficiency is more important than comparing headline rates alone.

Underwriting Differences

Underwriting for both structures considers revenue stability, time in business, debt service coverage, credit history, and industry risk. Because many lines of credit are unsecured, lenders may focus more heavily on cash flow performance. Understanding what lenders evaluate when approving working capital financing can help you prepare documentation and improve approval odds. Term loans often require more detailed projections and use-of-funds documentation, especially for larger amounts. Lines of credit typically emphasize current bank statements, revenue consistency, and existing debt levels. Using Both a Line of Credit and a Term Loan

Many established businesses use both structures for different purposes. A term loan might finance a major equipment purchase or expansion project with predictable monthly payments, while a line of credit covers seasonal cash flow, inventory buildups, or short-term opportunities. This hybrid approach lets you match the financing structure to the capital need. Just ensure your total debt service remains manageable–lenders will consider all existing and proposed obligations when underwriting new credit. Which Option Is Better for Established Businesses?

For established, revenue-generating companies: a line of credit often serves as a strategic liquidity tool; a term loan serves as project-specific capital. Many mature businesses maintain both–using a line of credit for operational flexibility and term loans for structured growth investments. The key is matching the financing structure to the purpose of the capital. If your primary need is managing uneven cash flow (e.g., slow receivables, seasonal dips), a line of credit is typically the better fit. If you’re making a one-time capital investment with a clear payoff timeline, a term loan provides structure and predictability. Evaluate your cash flow cycle, growth plans, and asset base before deciding. For more on how collateral affects your options, see do you need collateral for a business line of credit.

Final Thoughts

A business line of credit and a term loan serve different financial functions. If your business needs ongoing flexibility to manage cash flow cycles, vendor payments, or seasonal revenue shifts, a revolving line of credit may be the more strategic option. If you are funding a specific long-term investment, a term loan may provide clearer structure and amortization. Choosing the right capital solution requires evaluating how the financing aligns with your operational needs and financial profile. Monthly Review Rhythm and Performance Dashboard

Build a monthly dashboard that tracks utilization, average daily balance behavior, repayment pressure, and forecast variance. Include both expected and downside projections so decisions are not based on optimistic assumptions. This dashboard should be reviewed by ownership and operations together, because financing performance depends on execution in pricing, collections, purchasing, and staffing decisions.

Use a standard meeting agenda: first confirm what changed since last cycle, then assess whether thresholds were triggered, then assign corrective actions with due dates. Keep notes concise and measurable. If a threshold remains red for two cycles, escalate to a structure review and lender communication plan. Proactive escalation usually preserves flexibility and protects long-term terms.

  • Utilization guardrail: prevent persistent high usage from becoming structural debt stress.
  • Cash floor guardrail: maintain minimum liquidity buffer for normal volatility.
  • Variance guardrail: investigate repeated forecast misses and update assumptions quickly.
  • Governance guardrail: record action owners and completion status every month.

Over time, this review rhythm improves underwriting credibility. Lenders can see that management decisions are disciplined, data-based, and repeatable. That credibility often translates into better renewal outcomes and lower friction when you request additional flexibility or new capital.

Business Line of Credit: Underwriting and Revolving Discipline

Revolving credit approvals emphasize cash-flow stability and account behavior. Lenders review deposit consistency, existing debt payments, and whether your business can manage draws without chronic balance stress. Strong files show clear use-of-funds logic and a plan for draw and repayment—not only a limit request.

Before applying, reconcile your stated revenue to bank deposits and prepare a simple monthly surplus view after fixed obligations. If surplus is thin, request a conservative limit first; you can often grow the line with performance.

Operational Controls After Approval

  • Draw policy: define when the line is used versus operating cash.
  • Repayment cadence: align paydowns with receivable timing.
  • Reporting: track utilization and interest expense monthly.
  • Renewals: calendar renewal windows and covenant-like reporting early.

Comparing Offers and Avoiding Misalignment

Normalize competing LOC offers by annual fees, draw fees, rate index, margin, and billing frequency. A slightly lower rate with heavy fees can cost more over a year. Ask how rates change with prime and whether the line is renewable.

Risk Controls, Renewals, and Long-Term Credit Health

Revolving facilities reward disciplined behavior. Set internal guardrails: maximum utilization as a percent of limit, minimum operating cash buffer after interest, and escalation triggers when deposits trend down. If you approach those triggers early, reduce discretionary draws and accelerate paydowns before the lender sees stress in statements.

Document a clear draw-and-repay rhythm tied to receivables or project milestones. Lenders are more comfortable when the line supports working capital timing rather than chronic operating deficits. If deficits are structural, address pricing, costs, or revenue before borrowing more.

Before renewal, assemble updated financials, bank statements, and a short narrative on performance versus last review. Proactive communication about one-time events reduces surprise risk. Over multiple cycles, businesses that maintain clean utilization and repayment history typically see better limits and pricing than those that wait for problems to surface.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between a business line of credit and a term loan?

A business line of credit is revolving: you draw what you need up to a limit, pay interest only on the used balance, and reuse capital as you repay. A term loan delivers a one-time lump sum with fixed monthly payments and interest on the full amount.

When should you use a line of credit versus a term loan?

Use a line of credit for seasonal swings, receivables gaps, and recurring needs. Use a term loan for equipment, acquisitions, expansion, or any defined one-time project where repayment certainty matters.

Do you pay interest on the full amount with both?

No. A line of credit charges interest only on the balance you have drawn, while a term loan charges interest on the full lump sum.

Which is cheaper, a line of credit or a term loan?

It depends on usage. A line of credit can be cheaper for intermittent needs since you pay interest only on what you use; a term loan can be cheaper for a one-time lump sum repaid steadily.
