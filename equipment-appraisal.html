Quick answer

A heavy equipment appraisal is a professional valuation of excavators, dozers, loaders, trucks and other machinery, usually written to USPAP and stated three ways: fair market value (highest), orderly liquidation value and forced liquidation value (lowest). Lenders lend against liquidation value, not fair market value. For example, a paid-off excavator and skid steer appraised at $200,000 fair market, $160,000 orderly and $120,000 forced liquidation value might bring about $130,000-$150,000 in a sale-leaseback, and you keep running the machines.

How do appraisers determine the value of heavy equipment?

A qualified appraiser inspects the machine: make, model, year, hours or mileage, condition and maintenance history. Then they compare it with recent sales of similar equipment, sold prices rather than asking prices, to reach a value they can defend. Formal reports are written to USPAP (Uniform Standards of Professional Appraisal Practice), the standard banks and courts rely on.

What are the three types of appraisal value?

A proper appraisal states three numbers, because the right one depends on how fast the equipment would have to sell:

ValueAssumesAmount
Fair market value (FMV)A willing buyer and seller, with time to market the machineHighest
Orderly liquidation (OLV)Sold over a limited but reasonable periodMiddle
Forced liquidation (FLV)A quick, auction-style saleLowest

This is where most owners get surprised. Lenders advance against liquidation value, not fair market value, because that's what they could recover if they had to sell the collateral. Knowing all three numbers up front tells you what to expect. The same applies to heavy equipment, construction equipment, CNC and manufacturing machinery, trucks and trailers, and industrial processing equipment. For the details, see orderly vs forced liquidation value.

Who can appraise heavy equipment?

For a loan, lenders want an independent appraiser who holds a machinery and equipment credential and writes to USPAP. The American Society of Appraisers is one of the bodies that issues those credentials. A dealer's trade-in offer, a price guide printout and your own estimate are all useful, but none of them is an appraisal. More in who can appraise business equipment.

You'll also be asked whether a desktop appraisal will do. A desktop review is faster and cheaper but assumes the worst about condition; an on-site inspection costs more and adds scheduling, but it can prove a well-kept machine is worth more. See desktop vs on-site appraisal.

What raises or lowers your equipment's appraised value?

Two machines that look the same can appraise very differently. What appraisers and lenders weigh most:

  • Age, hours and mileage. Usage is the biggest driver of depreciation.
  • Condition and maintenance. Documented service history and good working order raise the value and the advance rate.
  • Make, model and demand. Well-known brands with an active resale market appraise higher than niche or highly specialized machines.
  • How easy it is to resell. Titled or serialized, long-lived equipment is easiest to value and lend against. A truck and an excavator of the same value don't support the same loan; see titled vehicles vs yellow iron.
  • Title and liens. Equipment you own free and clear, or with little left to pay, gets the best terms.

How do you turn appraised equipment value into cash?

There are three ways, and the right one depends on whether you want to keep using the equipment and whether you own it free and clear.

RouteKeep using?Keep title?Best when
Sale-leasebackYesNo (buy-back option)You own it free and clear, need cash, and want to keep operating
Refinance or collateral loanYesYesYou want to keep the title and depreciation and borrow against the value
Outright saleNoNoThe machine is idle or surplus

1. Sale-leaseback. You sell equipment you own to a lessor for a lump sum, lease it straight back and keep operating it. It turns trapped equity into working capital without a conventional loan, and lease payments are generally deductible. It suits paid-off, resaleable heavy equipment, trucks and machinery. Here's how an equipment sale-leaseback works.

2. Equipment refinance or collateral loan. You borrow against the appraised value and keep ownership and depreciation. That covers refinancing existing equipment debt into better terms and asset-based loans on machinery you own outright. See equipment financing and refinancing and asset financing.

3. Sell it outright. If a machine is idle or surplus, selling it is the cleanest way to free up cash, and a current appraisal helps you price it right. See our used and financing-ready equipment.

How much can you borrow against appraised equipment?

The math has four steps, and each one lowers the number: the appraised value on the lender's chosen basis, times the advance rate, minus the payoff on anything you still owe, minus costs such as the appraisal, UCC filing and documentation fees, which usually come out of the proceeds.

Example: a contractor owns a paid-off excavator and skid steer. The appraisal comes back at $200,000 fair market value, $160,000 orderly liquidation value and $120,000 forced liquidation value. A sale-leaseback lender advances against the orderly liquidation value and funds around $130,000-$150,000. The contractor keeps running the same machines and buys them back at the end of the lease. Refinancing and keeping the title would track the same liquidation value.

Watch the percentage. A higher advance rate can mean less money if it's applied to a lower value:

ItemLender ALender B
BasisForced liquidationOrderly liquidation
Value$40,000$60,000
Advance rate80%65%

Illustrative arithmetic, not a quote. Always ask which value a rate applies to, and compare offers in dollars. If you're buying equipment rather than borrowing against what you own, lenders generally lend against the lower of the appraised value and the price you pay. The full walk-through is in how appraisal value sets loan-to-value.

Why businesses get an equipment appraisal

  • To raise capital: borrow against it, refinance it or do a sale-leaseback. This is the most common reason and the one Axiant helps with.
  • To sell: price used equipment before listing it, or check a purchase so you don't overpay.
  • To insure: document replacement or actual cash value for coverage and claims.
  • For tax, estate or legal needs: buy-sell agreements, partnership changes, divorce and estate settlements often require a certified machinery and equipment appraisal.

Own the building too?

If you own the commercial property your business runs from, you can raise cash against it the same way, through a real estate sale-leaseback or a cash-out refinance. Owners who hold both the building and the machinery inside it often put the two into one plan. See commercial real estate financing, or ask us to look at both when you get matched.

Heavy equipment appraisal FAQs

What is a heavy equipment appraisal?

A professional valuation of machinery such as excavators, dozers, loaders, cranes and trucks. The appraiser inspects the equipment, checks make, model, year, hours, condition and maintenance records against recent sales of similar machines, and issues a report, usually written to USPAP. It states fair market, orderly liquidation and forced liquidation value.

How do you determine the value of heavy equipment?

By inspecting the machine and comparing it with recent sales of the same model and specification, not asking prices. Hours or mileage drive value the most, followed by condition and service records, brand and resale demand, how easy it is to resell, and whether the title is clear.

What are the three types of equipment appraisal value?

Fair market value assumes a willing buyer and seller with time to market the machine. Orderly liquidation value assumes a sale over a limited but reasonable period, and forced liquidation value assumes a quick auction-style sale, so it's the lowest. Lenders usually advance against orderly or forced liquidation value.

Who can appraise heavy equipment for a loan?

Lenders want an independent appraiser with a machinery and equipment credential, writing to USPAP. The American Society of Appraisers is one of the bodies that issues those credentials. A dealer's trade-in offer, a price guide printout or your own estimate is useful information, but none of them is an appraisal.

How much can I borrow against my equipment?

The appraised value on the lender's chosen basis, times the advance rate, minus the payoff on any existing lien, minus costs such as the appraisal and filing fees. Compare offers in dollars, not percentages: 80% of a $40,000 forced liquidation value is $32,000, while 65% of a $60,000 orderly liquidation value is $39,000.

Can I use an equipment appraisal to get a loan?

Yes. An appraisal is usually the first step in raising cash against equipment you own, through a sale-leaseback, an equipment refinance or collateral loan, or an outright sale. The appraised value, condition and title decide how much you can raise.
